## The ask

> &ldquo;Design a system that tells an application the moment one of its users enters or leaves a
> region it cares about, for a hundred thousand regions and ten million moving devices.&rdquo;

The words look like the proximity problem and the shape is inverted. When you match a rider to
drivers, the **data** is known and the **query** arrives unpredictably: someone opens an app, and
you go looking for the nearest points. Here the queries are registered in advance and never change
from moment to moment, while the data arrives continuously and never stops.

That inversion is the whole case study. Almost every wrong answer to this question comes from
carrying over an instinct that was correct for the other problem.

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| Are the regions circles or arbitrary polygons? | A circle is a distance comparison. A polygon needs a real point-in-polygon test and a bounding-box pre-filter. |
| How fast must an alert be after the crossing? | Five seconds and five minutes are different systems. The first forces streaming, the second permits batch. |
| Does the device report location, or do we infer it? | If the device reports, the client can do some of the work &mdash; which changes everything about cost. |
| Can a fence contain millions of devices? | An airport or a city centre makes one region a hot key regardless of how the index is built. |
| What happens to crossings while a device is offline? | Deciding whether to fire an hour-late alert is a product question with a large engineering consequence. |
| Do subscribers want every crossing, or a current state? | &ldquo;Tell me when anything happens&rdquo; and &ldquo;tell me who is inside now&rdquo; are different products. |

## Requirements

**Functional.** Register and remove regions. Ingest device location updates. Emit an entry event
when a device crosses into a region and an exit event when it leaves. Let a subscriber ask who is
currently inside a region.

**Non-functional.** Alert within five seconds of the crossing at the 99th percentile. Never emit an
entry without a matching exit. Do not emit a storm of alerts for a device sitting on a boundary.
One customer's pathological region must not degrade anybody else's alerts.

**Out of scope.** Map rendering, routing, address geocoding, and the privacy and consent framework
that any real version of this would need before a single line of it were written.

## Estimate

<Estimator preset="traffic" />

- **10,000,000 devices**, of which perhaps **20% are moving** at any moment.
- A moving device reports every **5 seconds**: 2,000,000 / 5 = **400,000 location updates per
  second.**
- **100,000 registered regions**, mostly small, changing rarely.
- A location update &mdash; device id, latitude, longitude, accuracy, timestamp &mdash; is about
  **50 bytes**, so ingest is roughly **20 MB/s**, which is unremarkable.

The unremarkable ingest is a trap. The cost is not in receiving the points; it is in deciding what
each one means.

<Claim label="inference">
Testing every update against every region is 400,000 &times; 100,000 = **4 &times; 10<sup>10</sup>
point-in-polygon tests per second.** That is not a number you optimise your way out of with faster
hardware or a bigger cluster; it is four orders of magnitude beyond what the whole design can
afford, and it means the naive evaluation is not a starting point to improve but an approach to
discard in the first minute.
</Claim>

So the arithmetic forces the central move, which is worth stating as a general principle rather
than a trick:

<Claim label="inference">
When the data streams and the queries are fixed and known in advance, **index the queries, not the
data.** A spatial index over the 100,000 regions turns each update from 100,000 tests into one
logarithmic lookup plus a handful of exact tests &mdash; roughly 400,000 index probes per second,
which is an ordinary workload for an ordinary machine.
</Claim>

This is the same inversion an <C id="inverted-index">inverted index</C> makes for text, and the
same one a rules engine makes for events. It is worth recognising by name, because the moment you
see it the problem stops being exotic.

## Data model

| Entity | Key | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Region | region id | Polygon or circle, owning tenant, bounding box, precomputed cell cover |
| Region index | spatial cell | Cell to region ids. The queries, indexed. Small, read constantly, written rarely. |
| Device position | device id | Last known point and timestamp. Ephemeral, overwritten, never a historical record. |
| Membership | (device id, region id) | The inside/outside bit that makes a crossing detectable. The only state that must persist. |
| Crossing event | (device id, region id, at) | Emitted, delivered, retained briefly for replay |

Membership is the entry worth dwelling on. A crossing is a **change** in a boolean, so detecting
one requires remembering the previous value. Store it only for pairs that are plausibly near each
other &mdash; a device carries membership rows for the handful of regions whose cells it has
touched, not a row for each of the 100,000 regions &mdash; and this stays small.

The index itself is the one place where the choice of structure matters. Regions are areas, not
points, so a structure that stores points will not do:

- **Cell cover.** Precompute the set of grid cells each region touches and store a cell-to-regions
  map. A lookup is a hash of the device's cell. Simple, fast, trivially cacheable, and slightly
  wasteful for long thin regions that cover many mostly-empty cells.
- **R-tree.** Bounding boxes in a balanced tree, which handles awkward shapes gracefully and is
  harder to distribute and to update concurrently.

Prefer the cell cover here. The regions change rarely, so precomputation is nearly free; the index
is small enough to hold in memory on every evaluator; and a hash lookup with no coordination beats
an elegant tree that has to be shared.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 &mdash; &ldquo;Why not just check every region on every update?&rdquo;

Because of the estimate above: 4 &times; 10<sup>10</sup> tests per second. Say the number out loud
early. It is the fastest way to establish that the design is forced rather than chosen, and an
interviewer who hears the arithmetic will usually skip ahead with you.

The cell cover gets it to about 400,000 hash lookups per second, each returning a handful of
candidate regions, followed by an exact test only for those. The bounding box is the cheap filter;
the polygon test is the expensive one; run them in that order and the expensive one almost never
runs.

### Round 2 &mdash; &ldquo;A courier parks on the boundary. We send two hundred alerts in a minute.&rdquo;

GPS is noisy &mdash; typically tens of metres, worse among tall buildings &mdash; so a stationary
device near an edge will appear to cross it repeatedly. The naive design is working exactly as
specified and the product is unusable.

Three mechanisms, and it is worth being clear that they are different:

**Hysteresis.** Enter at the boundary; exit only after moving a margin beyond it. The entry and
exit tests use different geometry, which feels wrong for about a minute and then feels obviously
right.

**Dwell time.** Require the device to be inside for *n* consecutive seconds before the entry fires.
This converts flapping into latency, and it is the knob the customer should control, because only
they know whether a three-second delay or a false alert costs them more.

**Accuracy gating.** Every fix carries an accuracy radius. If that radius is larger than the
distance to the boundary, the reading cannot answer the question. Do not guess &mdash; wait for a
better fix.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The requirement nobody states">
No customer asks for hysteresis, and every customer notices its absence within an hour of going
live. Boundary behaviour is the difference between a demo and a product, and it is the part of this
design that experience shows up in.
</Callout>

### Round 3 &mdash; &ldquo;One region contains a million devices.&rdquo;

An airport, a stadium, a city centre. Two separate problems hide in this and they need separating.

The **evaluation** is fine: work is partitioned by device, so a million devices inside one region
are spread across every evaluator. Nothing concentrates.

The **membership and delivery** are not. Every one of those devices writes a membership row keyed
by the same region, and every crossing produces a message for the same subscriber. That is a
<C id="hot-key">hot key</C> on the write path and a <C id="fanout-on-write">fanout</C> on the
delivery path.

Key the membership store by device rather than by region, so writes spread and the hot region is
only hot for the reader who asks &ldquo;who is inside?&rdquo; &mdash; a question that is rare and
can be served from a maintained count rather than a scan. For delivery, batch: a subscriber to a
stadium wants a rate, not a million individual notifications, so aggregate over a short window and
send a summary with a cursor into the detail.

### Round 4 &mdash; &ldquo;Updates for one device arrive out of order.&rdquo;

Then the membership bit flips backwards and you emit an exit after an entry that has not happened
yet. This is the failure that produces alerts nobody can reproduce.

Partition the log by **device id**, so every update for one device is handled by one evaluator in
one order. Then carry the device's own timestamp and **discard any update older than the one
already applied**. Ordering within a device is all that is needed here; there is no requirement to
order two different devices relative to each other, and trying to would be enormously expensive
for no benefit.

<Claim label="fact">
A device clock can be wrong by minutes and, on a handset whose user has changed the time, by
years. Compare a device's timestamps only against its own, use them for ordering rather than for
deciding what &ldquo;now&rdquo; is, and attach the server's receipt time for anything that has to
be reasoned about across devices.
</Claim>

### Round 5 &mdash; &ldquo;A device was offline for an hour and just uploaded its whole trail.&rdquo;

Ask what the customer wants, because both answers are defensible and they build different systems.

If alerts are **operational** &mdash; a driver arrived, unlock the gate &mdash; then an
hour-old entry event is worse than useless and should be dropped. Emit only crossings that are
still current, and record the rest as history.

If alerts are for **compliance** &mdash; did this vehicle enter a restricted zone &mdash; then
every crossing matters no matter how late, and the trail must be replayed through the same
evaluation path.

Build the second and make dropping a policy on top. The reverse is not possible, because
information you declined to compute is not recoverable, and the replay path is also what you will
use when a customer registers a region and asks who is inside it right now.

### Round 6 &mdash; &ldquo;Battery complaints. The app is the top drain on the phone.&rdquo;

The radio, not your servers. Reporting every five seconds is expensive on the device, and this is
the round where the right answer is to move work outward rather than inward.

Push the small set of nearby regions **down to the device** and let it evaluate locally, reporting
only crossings. The operating system's own geofencing API does exactly this, backed by hardware
that is far cheaper than a GPS fix. Server-side evaluation continues for devices that cannot or
will not do this, so the design keeps both paths.

The costs are real and should be named: fence definitions on the device are stale until it next
syncs; a device can lie about its own crossings, so anything with money or access attached still
needs server confirmation; and the local set has to be refreshed as the device moves, which is
itself a geospatial query.

### Round 7 &mdash; &ldquo;A customer registers a region covering an entire country.&rdquo;

Its cell cover is enormous, it matches nearly every update, and the exact test now runs constantly.
One tenant has just made themselves everybody's problem &mdash; and note that they have not done
anything wrong, which is what makes this a design failure rather than an abuse case.

Bound it explicitly. Cap the cell cover a single region may occupy and reject or simplify beyond
it. Meter evaluation cost per tenant rather than per request, since a request's cost varies by
orders of magnitude with the geometry behind it. Give large regions a coarser cover, trading
precision for cost where precision at a national boundary is meaningless anyway. And keep per-tenant
delivery budgets so one customer's storm cannot consume another's.

### Round 8 &mdash; &ldquo;Guarantee the alert within five seconds.&rdquo;

Write down the budget and see what is left:

| Stage | Budget |
|---|---|
| Device reporting interval | up to 5 s |
| Network and ingest | 200 ms |
| Log append and pickup | 300 ms |
| Index lookup and exact test | 10 ms |
| Membership compare and set | 20 ms |
| Delivery to subscriber | 500 ms |

The reporting interval alone can consume the entire budget, which means the guarantee is
unachievable at a five-second cadence no matter how fast the backend is. Either the device reports
more often &mdash; and Round 6 says what that costs &mdash; or it evaluates locally and reports
crossings immediately, or the number changes. Presenting this table is a better answer than any
optimisation, because it locates the constraint outside the part of the system you were being asked
about.

### Round 9 &mdash; &ldquo;An evaluator dies holding membership state for two million devices.&rdquo;

Membership must be durable outside the evaluator, or a restart silently re-emits entries for every
device that was already inside. Keep it in a store keyed by device, read it on the first update
after a reassignment, and cache it locally afterwards.

Then accept that delivery is <C id="at-least-once">at-least-once</C> and make the subscriber's life
easy: a stable event id per (device, region, transition) so duplicates are detectable, and
crossings that carry the resulting state rather than only the change, so a subscriber who missed one
can reconcile without asking.

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index the regions, not the points | Four orders of magnitude of evaluation cost | The index must be rebuilt when regions change | Regions changed as fast as positions do |
| Cell cover over R-tree | Hash lookups, cacheable, no shared structure | Wasted cells for long thin geometries | Geometries were mostly awkward shapes |
| Partition by device id | Ordering per device is free; membership reads are local | &ldquo;Who is in this region?&rdquo; needs a maintained count | Region queries outnumbered position updates |
| Durable membership store | Restarts do not re-emit entries | A store read on the path of every reassignment | Re-emitting on restart were acceptable |
| Dwell and hysteresis | Boundary behaviour becomes usable | Alerts are deliberately later than the raw crossing | False alerts were cheaper than delay |
| On-device evaluation where possible | Large battery and ingest savings | Stale fences, untrusted client, a second code path | Devices could not be trusted at all |
| At-least-once delivery with stable ids | Simple, survives everything | Subscribers must deduplicate | Subscribers could not be changed |
| Per-tenant cost metering | One customer cannot degrade another | More accounting, and some legitimate use is refused | The system were single-tenant |

## Cost

Ingest is 20 MB/s, which is nothing. The region index is a few hundred megabytes and sits in memory
on every evaluator. Neither is where the money goes.

The spend is **evaluation and delivery**, and both scale with movement rather than with the number
of devices installed. That is the useful thing to know commercially: a customer with a million
devices that mostly sit still costs less than one with fifty thousand vehicles driving all day, and
pricing that ignores this will be wrong in both directions.

The largest available saving remains Round 6. Moving evaluation onto devices removes most of the
ingest and most of the evaluation at once, and it is the reason the platform APIs exist in the form
they do.

## What we did NOT build

**A general spatial database.** We support one query &mdash; which registered regions contain this
point &mdash; and precompute for exactly that. A general engine would serve queries nobody asks and
be slower at the one that matters.

**Global ordering of updates.** Ordering is per device, which is all correctness requires. Ordering
across devices would need a shared clock or a consensus round on the hottest path in the system.

**Historical trajectory storage.** Positions are overwritten. The crossing stream is the durable
record. Storing every point for ten million devices is a separate system with separate economics
and a much larger privacy surface.

**Prediction.** Estimating arrival before it happens is a modelling problem, not an indexing one,
and it belongs on top of this rather than inside it.

## Evidence

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Multi-dimensional data can be indexed by a one-dimensional structure by translating coordinates
into a single value with a space-filling curve, which is what allows an ordinary ordered index to
answer a two-dimensional range query. This is the basis of the cell-cover approach used here.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="redis-docs">
Redis stores geospatial members in a sorted set scored by a 52-bit integer derived from a geohash
of the coordinates, so a proximity query becomes a range scan over that score. It is a working
example of the same encoding, available without building anything.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="uber-eng">
Uber published H3, a hierarchical hexagonal grid system for indexing geographic data, and describes
using cell identifiers as index keys for location workloads. Hexagonal cells have uniform distance
to all neighbours, which a square grid does not.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="kafka-docs">
Records are ordered within a partition but not across partitions, and consumers track their
position by offset against a retained log. Partitioning by device id is therefore what buys
per-device ordering, and the retention is what makes the replay in Round 5 possible.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
A spatial query is conventionally executed in two stages: a cheap filter over bounding boxes that
over-approximates the answer, followed by an exact geometric test on the survivors. The ordering of
those two stages is the reason the expensive test rarely runs.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-builders-library">
Shedding load at the edge of a system, before work is admitted, keeps a service responsive under
overload in a way that queuing the work does not, because a queue converts an overload into
unbounded latency rather than removing it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="sre-book">
Alerting should be driven by symptoms a user would notice rather than by internal causes. For this
system the symptom is consumer lag: alerts stop arriving long before any error rate moves, which is
why Round 9's stall is invisible to error-based monitoring.
</Claim>

<Claim label="speculation">
Device counts, the fraction moving, reporting intervals and the 100,000 regions are illustrative
figures chosen to make the arithmetic legible. They are the right order of magnitude for a
fleet-tracking product and should be re-derived for any real one.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention">
GPS accuracy on a consumer handset is commonly a few metres in the open and tens of metres among
tall buildings, and degrades further indoors. Treat the figure in Round 2 as an order of magnitude
rather than a specification; the design consequence - that a fix can be too imprecise to answer the
question being asked of it - holds regardless of the exact number.
</Claim>
