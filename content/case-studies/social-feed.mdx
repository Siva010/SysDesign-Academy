## The ask

> &ldquo;Design the timeline for a social network. Users follow other users and see their
> posts.&rdquo;

This is the canonical fanout problem, and it is famous for a specific reason: **the obvious
solution and the scalable solution are opposites, and neither works alone.**

The interviewer is looking for whether you notice that follower counts are not uniform. Almost
everything interesting follows from that one fact.

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| Chronological or ranked? | Ranking means read-time computation, so a precomputed list becomes a candidate set rather than the answer. |
| How many users, how many posts, how many feed views? | Decides whether write amplification is survivable. |
| What is the follower distribution? | **The critical question.** Uniform and heavy-tailed need different designs. |
| How fresh must the feed be? | Seconds of delay makes asynchronous fanout viable. Real-time does not. |
| Media in posts? | Adds a large-object problem, solved separately. |
| Can posts be deleted or edited? | Every mutation fans out too, and this is usually forgotten. |

**Assumed answers:** 500 million users, 200 million daily active, chronological with light
ranking, 100 million posts a day, 5 billion feed views a day, a few seconds of delay acceptable,
media supported, deletion required. Follower counts are heavy-tailed: the median user has a few
hundred followers and the largest accounts have tens of millions.

## Requirements

**Functional:** post; follow and unfollow; view a reverse-chronological feed of followed
accounts; delete a post.

**Non-functional**

- Feed load p99 under 200 ms.
- Feed may be up to a few seconds stale.
- A post must be visible to its author immediately.
- Deletion must propagate within seconds.
- Feed reads must remain available even when posting is degraded.

**Out of scope:** ranking model details, ads, direct messages, notifications.

## Estimate

<Estimator preset="traffic" />

With the assumed numbers:

- **Posts:** 100 million a day ÷ 86,400 ≈ **1,200 per second**, perhaps 4,000 at peak.
- **Feed views:** 5 billion a day ≈ **58,000 per second**, perhaps 200,000 at peak.
- **Read/write ratio:** roughly **50:1.**

Now the number that decides the architecture. If the average account has 500 followers and every
post is fanned out:

- Fanout writes: 1,200 posts/sec × 500 followers = **600,000 writes per second** average, and
  2 million at peak.

That is a large but tractable number for a partitioned key-value store. Then consider one account
with 40 million followers:

- One post = **40 million writes.**
- At a sustained 500,000 writes per second for the fanout tier, that single post occupies the
  entire fanout capacity for 80 seconds.

<Claim label="inference">
The estimate does not merely size the system; it disproves the pure strategy. Fanout-on-write is
correct for the median account and catastrophic for the tail, and the tail is not an edge case —
it is the accounts that generate the most engagement.
</Claim>

Storage:

- Feed entries: 500 followers × 100 million posts a day = 50 billion entries a day. At 50 bytes
  per entry, **2.5 TB a day** — before considering that this is per-follower duplication of
  something already stored once.
- This is why feeds are capped at a few hundred entries and old entries are trimmed.

## Data model

| Access pattern | Frequency | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Read a user's feed | 200,000/sec peak | Must be one sequential read; partition by user |
| Write a post | 4,000/sec peak | Cheap; partition by author |
| Fan out to followers | 2 million/sec peak | Partition by recipient, so writes spread |
| Read an account's followers | per post | Must be fast and can be large; partition by followee |
| Delete a post | rare | Must reach every feed it was copied into |

Three stores:

- **Posts**, partitioned by post id, holding the content once.
- **Follower lists**, partitioned by followee, holding who receives that account's posts.
- **Feeds**, partitioned by user, holding a capped list of post references in time order.

The feed holds **references, not content**. That keeps entries small, makes deletion a filter
rather than a rewrite, and means editing a post does not require touching millions of feeds.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 — &ldquo;An account with 40 million followers posts.&rdquo;

Fanout-on-write requires 40 million writes for one action. At 500,000 writes per second the
fanout tier is fully occupied for over a minute, delaying everyone else's posts.

**The hybrid.** Accounts above a follower threshold are excluded from fanout. Their posts are
stored once and merged into the feed at read time.

A feed read becomes:

1. Read the precomputed feed (one sequential read).
2. Read recent posts from the small number of large accounts this user follows (a handful of
   reads).
3. Merge and sort.

Because a user follows very few enormous accounts, step 2 is bounded and small.

**New cost:** two code paths, and a threshold that must be tuned. Too low and read-time merging
grows; too high and one post can still saturate fanout.

### Round 2 — &ldquo;Feed reads are 200,000 per second at peak.&rdquo;

Feeds are per-user, so they partition cleanly by user id — no hot partition from the read side.
The feed store must sustain 200,000 reads per second, which a partitioned key-value store handles
by adding partitions.

Cache the assembled feed briefly — tens of seconds — keyed by user. Users refresh more often than
their feed changes, so even a short TTL removes a large fraction of reads.

**New cost:** a user's own new post must appear immediately, so the cache must be invalidated for
that user on write, or bypassed for the author.

### Round 3 — &ldquo;A user deletes a post.&rdquo;

The post reference has been copied into millions of feeds. Deleting from each is another fanout,
as expensive as the original.

**The fix falls out of the data model:** feeds hold references, and the reader hydrates them from
the posts store. Deleting the post means the hydration returns nothing, and the reader filters it
out. No fanout required.

The cost is that feeds contain dangling references, so the reader must fetch slightly more
entries than it needs and filter. Trimming happens lazily.

<Callout kind="info" title="Why the reference-not-content decision earns its keep here">
Storing content in each feed entry would have made reads slightly faster and made deletion, edits
and privacy changes into millions of writes each. This is a good example of a decision whose
value only becomes visible three rounds later.
</Callout>

### Round 4 — &ldquo;A user follows someone new. Where are that account's old posts?&rdquo;

Two options, and it is a product decision:

- **Backfill:** insert the new followee's recent posts into the follower's feed. One-off work
  proportional to how many posts you backfill.
- **From now on:** the feed only contains posts made after the follow. Simpler, and the feed looks
  sparse for a new user with few follows.

Most systems do a bounded backfill — the last N posts — which caps the cost. A mass-follow
operation, such as importing a contact list, must be rate limited or it becomes a fanout storm in
the opposite direction.

### Round 5 — &ldquo;The fanout queue is four hours behind.&rdquo;

This is the silent backlog. Posts are being stored; they simply are not reaching feeds. Nothing
errors.

Diagnosis: is the bottleneck consumers, partitions, or the feed store? If the feed store is the
constraint, adding consumers makes it worse.

Mitigations, in order:

1. **Shed by recipient value.** Fan out to recently active users first; rebuild feeds on demand
   for users who return after a long absence. Most of the fanout work goes to feeds nobody reads.
2. **Batch writes** to the feed store per partition rather than one at a time.
3. **Raise the large-account threshold** temporarily, moving more accounts to read-time merge and
   reducing fanout load immediately.

**Alert on the age of the oldest unprocessed fanout job**, not on queue depth.

### Round 6 — &ldquo;Add images and video.&rdquo;

A separate primitive, and one that must not touch the fanout path.

- Client uploads directly to object storage using a presigned URL. Bytes never pass through the
  application.
- The post stores a key and metadata.
- Delivery is through a CDN.
- Transcoding happens asynchronously; the post is visible before every rendition is ready.

**New cost:** a media pipeline, and an orphan-cleanup job for uploads whose post was never
created.

### Round 7 — &ldquo;The feed store loses a partition.&rdquo;

Feeds are **derived data**. The posts store and the follower lists are the source of truth.

That means a lost feed partition is recoverable: rebuild those users' feeds from their follow
lists and recent posts. It is expensive and it is not data loss.

This is worth saying explicitly, because it changes the durability requirement for the largest
store in the system. Feeds can use cheaper storage with weaker guarantees than the posts store.

### Round 8 — &ldquo;Make it ranked rather than chronological.&rdquo;

The precomputed feed stops being the answer and becomes a **candidate set**.

- Fanout produces candidates.
- At read time, a ranking model scores them using signals available now — recency, affinity,
  engagement.
- The feed API returns the top N.

This adds read-time computation, which is what fanout-on-write was supposed to avoid. The
resolution is to keep the candidate set small — a few hundred entries — so ranking is bounded.

**New cost:** the ranking service is now on the read path, with its own latency and availability.
It needs a fallback: if ranking is unavailable, serve chronological. That is a visibly worse
product and a far better outcome than an error.

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid fanout | Bounded write amplification | Two code paths, a tuned threshold | Follower counts were uniform |
| Feeds hold references | Cheap deletion and edits | An extra hydration read per feed | Read latency were the binding constraint |
| Asynchronous fanout | Fast posting, available reads | Seconds of staleness | Real-time delivery were required |
| Feeds as derived data | Cheaper storage, rebuildable | Rebuild cost after loss | Rebuild were impossible from the sources |
| Capped feed length | Bounded storage | Deep history in the feed | Users routinely scrolled back years |
| Media via object storage and CDN | Bytes bypass the application | A separate pipeline and orphan cleanup | Media were small and rare |

## Cost

1. **Feed storage** is the largest line: billions of entries, duplicated per follower. Capping
   feed length and trimming aggressively is the main lever.
2. **CDN egress** for media, which typically exceeds all compute.
3. **Fanout compute**, proportional to total follower relationships rather than to posts.
4. **Posts storage**, small by comparison because each post is stored once.

<Claim label="recommendation">
The two decisions that dominate cost are the feed cap and whether you fan out to inactive users.
Both are adjustable without redesigning anything, which makes them the right first levers when
the bill becomes a problem.
</Claim>

## What we did NOT build

- **Pure fanout-on-write.** Disproved by the estimate: one large account would consume the fanout
  tier.
- **Pure fanout-on-read.** A scatter-gather across hundreds of partitions on the most frequent
  operation in the product.
- **Strong consistency on feeds.** Nobody can tell whether a post arrived 200 ms or 3 seconds
  ago, and paying coordination cost for it would be indefensible.
- **Content stored in feed entries.** It would make deletion, editing and privacy changes into
  millions of writes each.
- **A separate service per component.** The fanout worker and the feed API are deployables, not
  an excuse for eleven services.

</Claim>
