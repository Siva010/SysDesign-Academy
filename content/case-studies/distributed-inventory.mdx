## The ask

> &ldquo;Design inventory for a retailer selling ten million products from two hundred warehouses
> and stores, where the same item can be fulfilled from many places.&rdquo;

Ticket booking taught the primitive: a conditional update on a row, checked by affected row count,
which cannot oversell. That primitive is still here and it is no longer sufficient, because three
things about this problem are inverted.

Units are **fungible**. Nobody wants that specific tin; any unit of the product will do. There is
no J14 to adjudicate, only a count.

Stock is **physically distributed**. The same product exists in two hundred places, and the
question is not only whether it can be sold but from where it should ship, which is an
optimisation rather than a lock.

And &mdash; the one that changes everything &mdash; **the count is a belief, not a fact.** A seat
exists because your database says so. A tin of beans exists because it is on a shelf, and the
number in your database is a model of a physical world that drifts away from it continuously.

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| Is overselling recoverable? | Backorder, substitute, or ship from further away are all real remedies. That makes this a cost optimisation rather than a hard invariant, which ticket booking was not. |
| How accurate is the physical count? | If shrinkage and miscounts are material, exactness in the database buys less than it costs. |
| Must we promise a delivery date at checkout? | Promising a date binds you to a location and a shipment, which is far stronger than promising a unit. |
| Can we sell stock that has not arrived? | Selling against inbound supply raises availability and adds a new way to fail. |
| Who owns the stock, us or a marketplace seller? | Third-party counts arrive by feed, are stale by construction, and cannot be trusted the way your own can. |
| What is the read-to-write ratio? | Browsing checks availability constantly and buying is rare. The answer decides where the caching goes. |

## Requirements

**Functional.** Show availability on every product view. Reserve stock at checkout. Allocate a
fulfilment location. Release reservations that lapse. Reconcile against physical counts. Support
returns and restocking.

**Non-functional.** Oversell rarely enough that the remedy cost is acceptable. Never lose a
reservation that was confirmed to a customer. Survive a flash sale on one product. Keep browsing
available even when reservation is saturated.

**Out of scope.** Replenishment and forecasting, pricing, warehouse robotics and pick paths, and
carrier selection beyond choosing the shipping location.

## Estimate

<Estimator preset="traffic" />

- **10,000,000 products** across **200 locations**. Stocked combinations are sparse: perhaps
  **100,000,000** (product, location) rows, not two billion.
- A row &mdash; product, location, on-hand, reserved, damaged, inbound &mdash; at about 100 bytes is
  **10 GB**. Once again the contended dataset is small.
- **5,000 orders per second** at peak, so roughly 5,000 reservations per second spread over millions
  of products.
- **500,000 availability reads per second**, because every product page, search result and basket
  view asks.

<Claim label="inference">
Reads exceed writes by about **100 : 1**, which is the reverse of ticket booking, where nearly every
request was a purchase attempt destined to fail. The consequence is architectural: the display path
and the commit path have almost nothing in common and should not share a mechanism. Browsing is a
cache problem; reserving is a concurrency problem.
</Claim>

Then the number that decides how much correctness is worth buying:

<Claim label="convention">
Retail inventory record accuracy is commonly reported in the mid-90s as a percentage, meaning a
material fraction of (product, location) counts disagree with the shelf at any moment, through
shrinkage, damage, misplacement and receiving error. Treat the specific figure as something to
measure in your own operation rather than a constant, but treat the existence of the gap as
certain.
</Claim>

<Claim label="inference">
If several per cent of counts are already wrong physically, then engineering the database to be
perfectly consistent buys accuracy the warehouse cannot honour. The design objective is therefore
not to prevent overselling absolutely &mdash; that is not achievable from software &mdash; but to
make overselling **rare, detectable, and cheap to remedy**, and to spend the effort where the count
is actually trustworthy.
</Claim>

That reframing is the whole case study, and it is the thing a candidate who has only done ticket
booking will resist.

## Data model

| Entity | Key | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Stock | (product, location) | On-hand, reserved, damaged, inbound. The contended row. |
| Availability | product | Aggregate across locations, cached and deliberately approximate |
| Reservation | reservation id | Product, location, quantity, expiry, order reference |
| Ledger entry | (product, location, seq) | Every movement, append-only. The audit trail and the repair mechanism. |
| Count event | (product, location, at) | A physical count. Truth arriving from outside the system. |

The ledger is not decoration. Because the count is a belief, you need to be able to answer &ldquo;how
did it get to this number?&rdquo; when it disagrees with a shelf. An append-only movement log makes
the current value derivable and the discrepancy attributable, and it is what a cycle count corrects
against.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 &mdash; &ldquo;Two customers order the last unit at the same instant.&rdquo;

The same primitive as ticket booking, now against a quantity rather than a state:

    UPDATE stock SET reserved = reserved + 1
    WHERE product_id = ? AND location_id = ? AND on_hand - reserved >= 1

Affected row count of one means the reservation is yours. This is a
<C id="lost-update">lost update</C> waiting to happen if you split it into a read and a write, and
the predicate in the statement is what prevents it.

What is new is that failing on one location is not failing. There are 199 others, and the natural
implementation &mdash; try locations in preference order until one succeeds &mdash; is correct and
gives far better availability than a single global count ever could.

### Round 2 &mdash; &ldquo;The warehouse says there are four. There are three.&rdquo;

Nothing in the database caused this and nothing in the database can prevent it. Someone dropped
one, or it was mis-scanned at receiving, or it walked out of the store.

So the system needs three things it did not need for seats. A **movement ledger**, so a discrepancy
can be attributed rather than merely absorbed. **Cycle counting**, where locations physically count
a rotating subset and emit corrections, treated as authoritative because they came from reality.
And a **safety buffer**: hold back a small quantity per (product, location) so the sellable count is
deliberately lower than the believed count.

<Claim label="inference">
The buffer is the honest response to an inaccurate model, and it is a business decision expressed
as a number: a larger buffer means fewer oversells and more stock that exists but cannot be sold.
Because units are fungible, this knob exists at all &mdash; it has no analogue in ticket booking,
where holding back seat J14 means nobody can buy J14.
</Claim>

### Round 3 &mdash; &ldquo;We oversold. Now what?&rdquo;

You find out at the pick, when someone reaches for an item that is not there. The remedies are
ordinary commerce: ship from another location at higher cost, substitute with the customer's
consent, backorder with a revised date, or cancel and refund.

Each has a cost, and those costs are what set the safety buffer. That is the loop worth making
explicit in an interview: **measure the oversell rate, price the remedy, and tune the buffer until
the marginal cost of a lost sale equals the marginal cost of a failed one.** That sentence is the
difference between treating this as a correctness problem and treating it as what it is.

<Callout kind="info" title="Why ticket booking cannot do this">
There, overselling is unrecoverable: two people hold seat J14 and only one can sit in it, and no
amount of money fixes the second person's evening. Here, every failure has a price and a remedy. The
same primitive &mdash; a conditional update on a contended row &mdash; sits inside two completely
different design philosophies because the cost of being wrong differs by orders of magnitude.
</Callout>

### Round 4 &mdash; &ldquo;A single product goes viral. Ten thousand attempts per second.&rdquo;

Now you have ticket booking again, in miniature: a <C id="hot-key">hot key</C> on a handful of
(product, location) rows, and the same techniques apply.

Split the row: divide one location's stock into *n* sub-rows and reserve against a random one,
which multiplies contention capacity by *n* at the cost of a slightly conservative count when the
remainder is scattered across buckets. Admit at a controlled rate rather than letting every attempt
reach the row. Coalesce the availability reads &mdash; 500,000 people looking at one product page
is one cache entry, not 500,000 database reads, provided the regeneration is guarded against
<C id="cache-stampede">stampede</C>.

The transferable observation: the hot-key techniques are the same everywhere. What differs is that
here the hot key is rare, temporary, and identifiable in advance from the marketing calendar, so it
can be handled by exception rather than by designing the whole system around it.

### Round 5 &mdash; &ldquo;The customer wants it tomorrow. The nearest warehouse is empty.&rdquo;

Reservation and **allocation** are different decisions and conflating them is a common error.
Reservation asks whether a unit can be promised at all. Allocation asks which location should ship
it, and it is an optimisation over shipping cost, delivery speed, stock-out risk at that location,
and whether the order can be shipped whole.

Splitting an order across two locations doubles the shipping cost and is sometimes the only way to
meet a promised date. That is a business rule with a number attached, and the design should keep it
as configuration rather than embedding it in code, because it will be tuned constantly and by
people who do not deploy.

Allocate **late** where you can. The longer you wait, the more you know &mdash; other orders have
arrived, receipts have landed, and the decision improves. Deciding at checkout is simpler and
strictly less informed.

### Round 6 &mdash; &ldquo;Can we sell what has not arrived yet?&rdquo;

Yes, and it is where a lot of revenue lives. Available-to-promise counts confirmed inbound
shipments against a date, so a product with none on hand and a container arriving Thursday is
sellable for delivery Friday.

The risk added is a supply-chain risk rather than a software one: the container is late, and now the
promise breaks for every order that depended on it, all at once and in a correlated way. Keep the
inbound quantity and its confidence separate from on-hand stock, discount it by supplier
reliability, and never let it silently become indistinguishable from what is physically on a shelf.

### Round 7 &mdash; &ldquo;Half the catalogue belongs to marketplace sellers.&rdquo;

Their counts arrive by feed, are minutes to hours stale, and are maintained by systems with a wide
range of quality. They cannot be trusted the way your own warehouse can.

Treat third-party stock as a separate class: larger buffers, no promised dates, and a per-seller
reliability score derived from how often their fulfilment fails. Cancellation rate per seller is
the metric that matters and it belongs in the allocation decision, so persistently unreliable
sellers are ranked down automatically rather than by escalation.

### Round 8 &mdash; &ldquo;It is Black Friday and the inventory service is the bottleneck.&rdquo;

Check which path is actually saturated, because the answer is almost always the read path, and the
read path does not need to be exact.

Serve availability from cache with a short lifetime. Better, serve a **band** rather than a number
&mdash; &ldquo;in stock&rdquo;, &ldquo;low stock&rdquo;, &ldquo;out of stock&rdquo; &mdash; because
a band changes far less often than a count, so it caches vastly better and tells the customer
everything they actually use. Precision in the display is a cost with no corresponding benefit.

Under real pressure, degrade deliberately: freeze the cache for longer, show bands only, and keep
the reservation path exact. Reservation is 1% of traffic and it is the 1% that must not be wrong.

### Round 9 &mdash; &ldquo;Prove the numbers are right.&rdquo;

You cannot, and the useful answer is what you do instead. Reconstruct the count from the movement
ledger and compare it against the stock row on a schedule; a divergence means a bug, because the
ledger and the row should agree by construction even when both disagree with the shelf.

Compare against cycle counts to measure the drift from physical reality, and track that as a
service level for the warehouse rather than for the software. Alert on oversell rate by location,
because a location whose rate jumps has an operational problem &mdash; a broken scanner, an
untrained shift &mdash; that the inventory system can detect long before the warehouse notices.

<Claim label="inference">
The system cannot make the count true. What it can do is make the count **explainable**, its
divergence **measurable**, and the cost of divergence **visible to the people who can reduce it.**
That is the achievable version of correctness here, and stating it plainly is a better answer than
claiming a guarantee the physical world does not support.
</Claim>

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate display and commit paths | The exact guarantee is bought for 1% of traffic | Two representations that can disagree | Reads and writes were balanced |
| Availability as a band, not a number | Caches enormously better; matches what customers use | Precision the customer never needed | Exact counts were contractually shown |
| Stock per (product, location) | Can promise a date and choose a shipment | More rows, and cross-location queries | Fulfilment were from one place |
| Safety buffer per location | Oversell rate falls to a chosen level | Stock that exists cannot be sold | Physical counts were reliable |
| Tolerate oversell, remedy it | Availability stays high and the system stays simple | Real remedy cost, and some unhappy customers | Oversell were unrecoverable, as with seats |
| Append-only movement ledger | Discrepancies are attributable and repairable | Storage, and a second thing to keep consistent | Nothing ever disagreed, which it always does |
| Late allocation | Better decisions with more information | Complexity, and a later commitment to a location | A date had to be promised at checkout |
| Split hot rows by exception | Normal products stay simple | Hot products need detection and handling | Every product were hot, as in ticket booking |

## Cost

Ten gigabytes of stock rows is nothing, and the ledger is the only thing that grows without bound
&mdash; every movement of every unit, retained for years because it is the audit trail. Partition
it by time and tier it out; this is the one place where a retention policy genuinely matters.

The operational cost is the **read path**, and it is dominated by cache hit rate. At 500,000 reads
per second, the difference between 95% and 99% hit rate is 20,000 versus 5,000 database reads per
second, which is the difference between a modest cluster and a large one. Serving bands rather than
counts is worth more here than any amount of hardware, because it changes the invalidation rate
rather than the capacity.

The cost that dwarfs both is **the remedy**: expedited shipping, split shipments, and cancellations
run to real money per incident. A one percentage point improvement in oversell rate is worth more
than the entire infrastructure bill, which is the argument for spending engineering effort on
reconciliation and buffer tuning rather than on shaving milliseconds.

## What we did NOT build

**A globally consistent count.** It would require distributed coordination on every read of a
number that is already wrong by several per cent for physical reasons. The coordination would be
real and the accuracy would not.

**Exact availability on product pages.** Bands cache better, change less, and answer the customer's
actual question.

**A distributed transaction across reservation and payment.** They are separate steps with
compensations, in the same shape as ticket booking, for the same reason: an external call must not
sit inside a lock on a contended row.

**Forecasting and replenishment.** Deciding what to buy is a different discipline with a different
failure mode, and it consumes this system's output rather than living inside it.

## Evidence

<Claim label="fact" source="postgres-docs">
A read followed by a separate update permits a concurrent transaction to invalidate the read at
levels below serialisable, which is the classic lost update. Making the check a predicate of the
update statement, and testing the affected row count, avoids it without explicit locking.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Where a value is derived from a sequence of events, retaining the events allows the current value to
be recomputed and discrepancies to be attributed, which a system storing only the current value
cannot do. This is the argument for the movement ledger.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
Contention on a single row limits throughput regardless of the storage engine, since serialisation
occurs at the row rather than at the system. Distributing one logical quantity across several
physical rows is the standard remedy and trades read cost for write concurrency.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-builders-library">
Coalescing concurrent identical requests and adding jitter to expiry prevents the synchronised
regeneration that follows a cache expiry on a hot key, which would otherwise deliver the full read
load to the origin at once.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="stripe-eng">
Operations made safe to retry through caller-supplied idempotency keys return the original outcome
rather than repeating the effect, which is what keeps a retried checkout from reserving twice.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="sre-book">
Service levels should be set at the point where the cost of additional reliability exceeds its
value, rather than at the highest achievable number. Applied here, that is the argument for tuning
the safety buffer against remedy cost rather than pursuing zero oversell.
</Claim>

<Claim label="speculation">
The catalogue size, location count, order rate and 100:1 read-to-write ratio are illustrative. The
ratio is the load-bearing one, and it is worth measuring first in any real system, because it
decides whether the caching layer or the contention layer is the design's centre of gravity.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention">
Published figures for retail inventory record accuracy vary by sector, by counting method and by
how the measurement is defined, and the widely cited mid-90s range should be treated as an
indication that the gap is material rather than as a number to design against. Measure your own,
per location; the variation between locations is usually larger than the average is informative.
</Claim>
