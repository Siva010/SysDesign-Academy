## The ask

> &ldquo;Design the service that decides, inside a card authorisation, whether a transaction is
> fraudulent, at fifty thousand transactions per second.&rdquo;

This looks like the attribution problem &mdash; join a stream of events against other events and
produce a verdict &mdash; and one clause changes everything about it: **inside the authorisation**.

Attribution can take minutes, can be replayed when it turns out to be wrong, and is reconciled
against a ledger before anybody is paid. None of that is available here. The answer must be
produced while a cardholder stands at a terminal, it is sent to the card network and cannot be
recalled, and being late is identical to being wrong.

The design consequence is severe and it is the thing to say first: **a join you must finish in a
hundred milliseconds is not a join you can perform when the question arrives.**

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| What is the latency budget, and at which percentile? | A hundred milliseconds at the median and at the 99.9th are different systems by an order of magnitude in cost. |
| If we cannot answer in time, is the transaction approved or declined? | This is the single most consequential requirement in the problem, and it is a business decision, not a technical default. |
| Are we scoring, or deciding? | A score handed to someone else's policy engine is a much smaller obligation than owning the decline. |
| How soon do we learn we were wrong? | Chargebacks arrive weeks later, which decides how the system is evaluated and retrained. |
| Which signals may we use? | Data residency and regulatory limits on profiling constrain the features before any engineering does. |
| Is the adversary adaptive? | Unlike almost every other workload in this library, the input distribution changes in response to the system. |

## Requirements

**Functional.** Score every authorisation. Apply a policy to produce approve, decline, or
challenge. Record the decision and the evidence behind it. Support rules that can be changed
without a deploy.

**Non-functional.** Answer within the budget at the 99.9th percentile. Degrade to a defined
behaviour rather than hanging. Never let a downstream failure block the payment path. Reproduce any
past decision exactly, for a dispute or a regulator.

**Out of scope.** Model training and selection, chargeback operations, identity verification flows,
and the manual review queue that sits behind a challenge.

## Estimate

<Estimator preset="traffic" />

- **50,000 authorisations per second** at peak.
- Total authorisation budget end to end is a second or two; the fraud decision is allocated
  **100 ms at the 99.9th percentile**.
- **500,000,000 active cards.** Rolling aggregates per card at ~200 bytes is about **100 GB** &mdash;
  large for memory on one machine, unremarkable across a cluster.
- Chargeback feedback arrives **30 to 90 days** after the transaction.

The percentile is not decoration, and it is the number most candidates skip past.

<Claim label="inference">
At 50,000 per second, the 99th percentile is **500 transactions every second** falling outside the
budget. The 99.9th is still fifty a second, or four million a day. A latency target expressed at
the median describes a system that fails continuously and invisibly, so the tail is the
requirement, and designs that are fast on average but occasionally slow are disqualified rather
than merely suboptimal.
</Claim>

Now the arithmetic that removes the obvious design. Answering &ldquo;how many transactions has this
card made in the last hour, in how many countries, at how many merchants&rdquo; by querying
transaction history costs several indexed reads, each with its own tail. Even at five milliseconds
each at the median, the 99.9th percentile of a handful of dependent reads lands far outside a
hundred milliseconds &mdash; because tails compound, and the slowest of several calls dominates.

<Claim label="inference">
The join therefore cannot happen on the request path. The only shape that fits is a **single keyed
lookup of state that was already computed** before the transaction arrived. The stream job's
purpose is not to answer questions; it is to make sure the answers already exist.
</Claim>

## Data model

| Entity | Key | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Card profile | card id | Rolling counts and sums over 1 min, 1 h, 24 h, 30 d. The join state. |
| Merchant profile | merchant id | Same shape, wildly skewed. Some merchants are millions of times busier than others. |
| Device / IP profile | device id | Shorter windows, higher churn |
| Decision record | authorisation id | The verdict, the policy version, and **the feature values as they were at decision time** |
| Rule set | version | Deterministic policy, changeable without deploying code |

The decision record is the entry that is easy to under-specify and expensive to get wrong. It
stores the feature *values*, not a reference to the profile they came from, because the profile
will have moved on by the time anyone asks. Round 6 is about why that matters more than it looks.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 &mdash; &ldquo;Why not just query the card's recent transactions?&rdquo;

Because tails compound. A design that makes four dependent lookups, each with a five-millisecond
median and a forty-millisecond 99th percentile, does not have a forty-millisecond tail &mdash; it
has one considerably worse, because the slowest of the four decides the answer and the probability
that at least one is slow grows with every call added.

<Claim label="fact" source="tail-at-scale">
When a request depends on several parallel or sequential sub-requests, its latency is governed by
the slowest of them, so tail latency at the component level is amplified rather than averaged at
the request level. Systems with modest per-component tails routinely exhibit unacceptable
end-to-end tails for this reason.
</Claim>

The fix is structural, not incremental: reduce the number of things that can be slow. One lookup
against one store, with a hard timeout and a defined fallback, is a shape whose tail you can
actually reason about.

### Round 2 &mdash; &ldquo;So what exactly is precomputed?&rdquo;

Windowed aggregates, keyed by the entities a rule can name: counts and sums per card, per merchant,
per device, over a minute, an hour, a day, and thirty days. Distinct counts &mdash; how many
countries, how many merchants &mdash; kept approximately, because an exact distinct count over
thirty days per card is far more state than the answer is worth.

That collection **is** the join. &ldquo;This transaction against this card's recent behaviour&rdquo;
has been computed continuously by the stream job, so the request path performs a lookup rather than
a join.

Size it out loud: 500 million cards at roughly 200 bytes is about 100 GB, which is a moderate
cluster holding everything in memory. Merchants and devices add less. The state is affordable
precisely because it is aggregates rather than history, and the discipline that keeps it affordable
is refusing to keep anything per transaction.

### Round 3 &mdash; &ldquo;The profile store is slow. Approve or decline?&rdquo;

The most important question in the problem, and the correct answer is that **it is not yours to
choose**. Bring the arithmetic and let the business decide.

Failing **closed** &mdash; declining when unsure &mdash; costs the margin on every legitimate
transaction declined, plus customer trust, which is the expensive part and the part that does not
appear on the incident report. Failing **open** costs the fraud that slips through, at roughly the
full transaction value.

With a fraud rate of a fraction of a per cent and a decline affecting whatever share of traffic the
outage touches, failing open is usually cheaper by a wide margin &mdash; which is why most
authorisation paths fail open, and why the fraud service is built so it can never block the
payment.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Fail open is a decision, not a default">
Fail open is normally right and it is not automatically right. It is exactly the state an attacker
would like to induce, so pair it with a much more conservative static rule set for the degraded
path, cap the exposure while degraded, and alarm loudly. A fallback nobody tested is a fallback
that will be exercised for the first time during an incident.
</Callout>

### Round 4 &mdash; &ldquo;One merchant is five per cent of all transactions.&rdquo;

A national retailer, and the merchant profile for it is a single key taking 2,500 updates per
second while most merchants take one an hour. Partitioning by merchant id puts all of that on one
partition, and that partition is now the system's throughput ceiling.

Split the key: maintain the hot merchant's aggregate across *n* sub-keys and sum them on read. The
read cost rises from one lookup to *n*, which is affordable when *n* is small and applied only to
the handful of keys that need it, and detecting which keys need it is a matter of watching update
rates rather than of guessing in advance.

Note that the card profile has no such problem: cards are numerous and individually slow, so
partitioning by card id is naturally even. Two keys in the same system, two entirely different
distributions &mdash; and a single partitioning strategy applied to both would be wrong for one of
them.

### Round 5 &mdash; &ldquo;A card is used in London and Singapore four minutes apart.&rdquo;

The signal everyone reaches for, and it is a clock problem wearing a fraud costume.

The two authorisations arrive from different acquirers, over different paths, with different
delays, carrying timestamps set by terminals whose clocks are not synchronised with each other or
with you. Processing order tells you nothing, and terminal time cannot be trusted absolutely.

Partition the stream by **card id** so one card's events are ordered relative to each other, use the
acquirer's timestamp for reasoning and your receipt time for bounding, and treat the aggregate as
provisional within a short window. If a late event changes the picture, the profile corrects itself
for the *next* decision; the one already sent cannot be recalled, which is the permanent asymmetry
of this system and worth stating plainly rather than designing around.

### Round 6 &mdash; &ldquo;You learn it was fraud six weeks later.&rdquo;

The label arrives long after the decision, and this creates a subtle failure that ruins evaluation
work quietly.

If you recompute the features later to analyse the decision, you get **different values**, because
the profile has absorbed six weeks of subsequent activity. The analysis then measures a decision
nobody made.

So log the feature values **as used**, in the decision record, at decision time. It is more storage
and it is the only way to answer &ldquo;why did we decline this?&rdquo; correctly &mdash; which is
also a question a regulator or a court may ask, and &ldquo;we can no longer reconstruct it&rdquo; is
not an answer.

<Claim label="inference">
This generalises past fraud. Any system whose decisions are judged later must record the inputs to
the decision, not merely the output, because the inputs are derived from state that keeps moving.
Recomputation feels equivalent and is not.
</Claim>

### Round 7 &mdash; &ldquo;We need a new feature: transactions per hour on the shipping address.&rdquo;

Adding an aggregate to a running stream job is not a deploy. The new key has no history, so the
feature is empty for every entity until enough traffic has accumulated &mdash; and the window
determines how long that takes. A thirty-day aggregate is genuinely empty for thirty days.

Two options, and both are worth knowing. **Backfill** by replaying retained history through the
job, which requires that the retention actually covers the window and that the job is
deterministic. Or **run it forward in shadow**, deciding nothing on it until the state is mature.

Version the state schema and run old and new side by side through the transition, in the usual
<C id="expand-contract">expand and contract</C> shape. State migrations on a live stream job are
one of the genuinely hard parts of operating this, and a candidate who mentions it unprompted is
telling you they have done it.

### Round 8 &mdash; &ldquo;An attacker probes with tiny transactions until something works.&rdquo;

This system has a property almost nothing else in the library has: **the input distribution changes
in response to the system's own behaviour.** Every rule you deploy is published to the adversary
by its effects, and a threshold at fifty pounds teaches them to try forty-nine.

Consequences worth naming. Prefer relative and adaptive thresholds over fixed ones, because a fixed
number is a target. Do not leak the reason for a decline into a response the attacker can read.
Rate-limit probing behaviour itself &mdash; many small declines from one entity is a signal
independent of any individual transaction. And accept that model decay here is adversarial rather
than incidental, so retraining cadence is a security control and not merely hygiene.

### Round 9 &mdash; &ldquo;The model says approve, the rules say decline.&rdquo;

Keep both, deliberately, and make the precedence explicit rather than emergent.

Deterministic rules exist for the things that must be certain: sanctions lists, hard limits, and
whatever a regulator requires be enforceable and explainable. They win, always, because their
purpose is to be a guarantee rather than an estimate.

The model handles the large space where a rule would be arbitrary. Deploy changes to it in shadow
first &mdash; scoring live traffic and recording what it *would* have done without acting &mdash;
and compare against the current decision on the same traffic. Because feedback takes six weeks,
shadow mode is often the only honest evaluation available before a change goes live.

Keep the rule set behind a versioned, hot-reloadable configuration, and record the version on every
decision. When someone asks in a year why a transaction was declined, the version is what makes the
answer possible.

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-joined profiles, not query-time joins | One lookup fits the budget with a reasonable tail | State must be maintained continuously and can go stale | The budget were seconds rather than milliseconds |
| Aggregates rather than history | 100 GB instead of petabytes | Only questions anticipated in advance can be answered | Storage and latency were both free |
| Approximate distinct counts | Bounded state per card | Small counting error on some features | Exactness affected the decision, which it does not |
| Fail open on the degraded path | A dependency cannot stop payments | Fraud exposure during degradation | Fraud loss exceeded the cost of declining, as for very high value transactions |
| Partition by card, split hot merchants | Even distribution for both key types | Two strategies to operate and understand | Both distributions were similar, which they are not |
| Feature values logged at decision time | Decisions remain explainable and evaluable | Considerable extra storage | Nobody ever audited a decision |
| Rules ahead of the model, always | Hard requirements are guaranteed, not estimated | Rules are blunt and get gamed | Nothing required a hard guarantee |
| Shadow evaluation before activation | Changes are measured before they cost money | Slower to ship a change | Feedback were fast, which at 30-90 days it is not |

## Cost

The dominant cost is **keeping 100 GB of state hot and current**: memory across the profile
cluster, plus the stream job maintaining it, plus enough headroom that the tail stays inside budget
at peak. Provisioning to a tail rather than to a mean is inherently expensive, and that is the
honest characterisation of this bill.

The decision log is the fastest-growing thing here, because storing features per authorisation is
far larger than storing verdicts. It is also the least negotiable, since its retention is usually
set by regulation rather than by engineering, so treat it as a fixed obligation and put it on cheap
storage from the start.

Set against the fraud it prevents, all of this is small &mdash; which is the useful framing when
asked to justify the spend. The comparison that matters is not infrastructure against budget but
basis points of fraud loss against basis points of false declines, and the second number is
routinely the larger of the two.

## What we did NOT build

**A real-time join over transaction history.** The budget forbids it. Everything joinable is joined
in advance.

**Exactly-once decisioning.** A retried authorisation should reach the same verdict, which is
idempotence of the decision rather than exactly-once processing of the stream. The aggregates
tolerate at-least-once updates because small double counting does not change a threshold outcome.

**Synchronous model training.** Labels arrive weeks later; nothing about training belongs on this
path.

**A manual review queue.** A challenge verdict hands off to one. Building it is a separate product
with its own staffing model and its own latency expectations, measured in hours.

## Evidence

<Claim label="fact" source="tail-at-scale">
In services composed of many components, the latency of a request is determined by its slowest
dependency, so component-level tail latency is amplified at the request level. Techniques such as
hedged requests and bounded fan-out exist specifically to contain this effect.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="kafka-docs">
Records are ordered within a partition and not across partitions, and consumer position is tracked
by offset against a retained log. Partitioning by card id is what provides per-card ordering, and
retention is what makes the Round 7 backfill possible at all.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Stream-table joins are implemented by maintaining the table side as local state updated from a
changelog, so the join is a local lookup rather than a remote query. This is the mechanism the
profile store implements.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
Approximate distinct-count structures answer cardinality queries in space that grows far more
slowly than the number of distinct values, at a bounded relative error - the trade this design
makes for the distinct-country and distinct-merchant features.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-builders-library">
A caller that cannot distinguish a slow dependency from a failed one must impose its own deadline,
because a dependency's latency becomes the caller's availability once the timeout is longer than
the caller's budget.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="stripe-eng">
Payment operations are made safe to retry through caller-supplied idempotency keys identifying the
logical operation, which is what allows a retried authorisation to reach the same recorded outcome.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="sre-book">
Service levels should be expressed at the percentile that reflects user experience, and error
budgets make the cost of missing them explicit. At this volume the difference between the 99th and
99.9th percentile is four million requests a day.
</Claim>

<Claim label="speculation">
The transaction rate, card count, profile size and 100 ms budget are illustrative and vary by
processor and by card scheme. The structural conclusion - that the budget forbids a query-time join
and forces pre-computation - holds across the plausible range, which is the reason to derive it
from arithmetic rather than assert it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention">
Fraud rates and false-decline rates are commercially sensitive and rarely published in comparable
form. The widely repeated observation that false declines cost merchants more than fraud itself is
directionally well supported and should be treated as a prompt to measure both, not as a constant.
</Claim>
