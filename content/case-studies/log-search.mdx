## The ask

> &ldquo;Design log storage and search for a fleet of fifty thousand services, retained for thirty
> days and searchable during an incident.&rdquo;

The words say search, and the instinct is to reach for the machinery that serves a product
catalogue: tokenise everything, build an inverted index, rank the results. That machinery is
correct and it is pointed the wrong way round, because the ratio it was designed for is inverted
here.

A catalogue index is written once and read a thousand times. A log index is written a thousand
times and read once, if ever. Almost every decision follows from that one reversal, and a candidate
who states it in the first two minutes has most of the design.

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| Who queries, and how often? | A handful of engineers during incidents is a different system from a dashboard that every service polls. |
| Is the common query &ldquo;this service, last hour&rdquo; or &ldquo;this string, last month&rdquo;? | The first is served by partitioning alone. The second needs an index and is far more expensive. |
| Do logs have structure, or are they lines of text? | Structured fields can be indexed cheaply and selectively. Free text cannot be, at this volume. |
| Is losing some logs acceptable, and which ones? | The answer decides what happens at the moment the system is most stressed, which is also when logs matter most. |
| Must all thirty days be equally fast? | If day 29 may take minutes, most of the storage bill and most of the index disappears. |
| Are trace or request ids searched directly? | The highest-cardinality field in the data is also the one people most want to look up, and that tension drives the whole index design. |

## Requirements

**Functional.** Ingest structured and unstructured log events from fifty thousand sources. Retain
thirty days. Search by time range, by service and host, by severity, and by content. Look up a
single request across the whole retention window.

**Non-functional.** Never let query load compromise ingest. Bounded, predictable retention cost.
One noisy team must not degrade another's ingest or search. Recent data must stay fast while an
incident is in progress.

**Out of scope.** Metrics and traces as separate products, alerting rules, log parsing at the
source, and the retention policy negotiation that decides whether thirty days is even the right
number.

## Estimate

<Estimator preset="storage" />

- **50,000 services** producing **500,000 log lines per second** in aggregate.
- At an average **500 bytes** a line: **250 MB/s**, or about **21 TB per day** raw.
- Compressed at roughly 10:1: **2.1 TB per day**, and **63 TB** for thirty days.
- Query load: perhaps **two searches per second** in normal operation, from a few dozen engineers.

Put the two rates beside each other, because the comparison is the design.

<Claim label="inference">
Five hundred thousand writes per second against two reads per second is a ratio of roughly
**250,000 : 1**. Product search runs the same ratio the other way. Every technique that is obviously
correct for a catalogue &mdash; index every field, precompute rankings, denormalise generously
&mdash; is paid for once and recovered over a thousand reads. Here it is paid for a quarter of a
million times and recovered once.
</Claim>

The second number that decides things is the cost of the index itself, which is the part people
consistently underestimate.

<Claim label="inference">
A full inverted index over log content typically costs as much as or more than the compressed data
it indexes, so indexing everything roughly **doubles the storage bill and adds the index write to
every one of 500,000 events per second**. At this ratio the index is not an optimisation that pays
for itself; it is the single largest line item, bought to serve two queries a second.
</Claim>

So the design problem is not how to search 63 TB. It is **how little indexing you can get away
with**, and the answer turns out to be surprisingly little, because of what the queries look like.

## Data model

| Entity | Key | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Event | (tenant, service, hour, offset) | The log line, plus structured fields. Immutable, never updated, never individually deleted. |
| Block | (tenant, service, hour, block id) | A few thousand events, compressed together. The unit of storage, retention and scan. |
| Label index | (tenant, service, severity, hour) | Small, structured, low cardinality. Prunes to a set of blocks. |
| Block filter | block id | A Bloom filter over the tokens the block contains. Prunes further without an inverted index. |
| Retention unit | (tenant, hour) | Whole partitions, dropped rather than deleted. |

The partition key contains **time**, and that is load-bearing rather than tidy. It means a query
with a time range touches only the blocks in that range, that retention is the removal of whole
partitions, and that yesterday's data is never rewritten by today's arrivals.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 &mdash; &ldquo;Why not just put it all in a search engine?&rdquo;

Because of the ratio, and it is worth being concrete rather than dismissive: a full-text search
engine is an excellent piece of technology being asked to do the thing it is worst at.

Its index is optimised for many reads amortising one write. Here each write is amortised over
roughly zero reads, and the index must still be built, merged, compacted and replicated. You pay
for the index in ingest capacity, in storage, and in the merge work that continues long after
anybody stopped caring about that hour of logs.

The alternative is not &ldquo;no index&rdquo;. It is **an index over the fields that prune, and a
scan over the content that does not**. Which brings the next question.

### Round 2 &mdash; &ldquo;Then which fields do you index?&rdquo;

The low-cardinality structured ones: tenant, service, host, severity, and time. Perhaps a dozen
values to a few thousand each.

The arithmetic that justifies the choice is worth doing out loud. A query for one service, one
hour, at error severity, selects perhaps one part in a hundred thousand of the corpus. Sixty-three
terabytes becomes a few hundred megabytes, and a few hundred megabytes can simply be **read**.

<Claim label="inference">
An index earns its cost by pruning. Once the pruning is good enough that the survivors can be
scanned inside the query budget, further indexing buys nothing and still costs the same to
maintain. Log search sits well past that point with only a handful of fields indexed, which is why
the design that looks lazy is the correct one.
</Claim>

What you must **not** index is the high-cardinality content. A request id appears in a handful of
lines out of billions, so an index entry for it is nearly one entry per event: an index the size of
the data, which prunes perfectly and costs everything.

### Round 3 &mdash; &ldquo;I have a trace id and I need every line for it across thirty days.&rdquo;

This is the query the previous round just refused to index for, it is the single most valuable
query during an incident, and reconciling those two facts is the heart of the design.

Attach a **Bloom filter per block** over the distinct tokens the block contains. At query time, ask
each block's filter whether it might contain the id. Filters are small &mdash; a few kilobytes for
a block of thousands of events &mdash; and a false-positive rate of one per cent means scanning one
per cent of the corpus rather than all of it.

<Claim label="fact">
A Bloom filter answers &ldquo;definitely not present&rdquo; exactly, and &ldquo;possibly
present&rdquo; with a tunable error rate, in space independent of the size of the values stored.
That asymmetry is what makes it usable here: a false positive costs one wasted block scan, and a
false negative &mdash; which cannot occur &mdash; would cost a wrong answer.
</Claim>

So the trace lookup becomes: filter check on every block in range, then scan the survivors. It is
orders of magnitude slower than an inverted index lookup and orders of magnitude cheaper to
maintain, and at two queries a second that trade is obviously right.

### Round 4 &mdash; &ldquo;Delete everything older than thirty days.&rdquo;

If retention is a `DELETE` over rows, it is the most expensive operation in the system: every
deletion rewrites index structures, generates tombstones, and produces compaction work that
competes with ingest &mdash; forever, because the deletions never stop.

Because the partition key leads with time, retention is instead **dropping whole partitions**.
No row is examined, no index is rewritten, and the cost is closing files.

<Callout kind="info" title="A general shape">
When data has a lifetime, partition by it. Retention then becomes deletion of a whole partition,
which is close to free, rather than deletion of rows, which is close to the most expensive thing a
storage engine does. This applies well beyond logs, and it is a common reason that a table which
worked fine for a year becomes unmanageable in its second.
</Callout>

### Round 5 &mdash; &ldquo;There is an incident. Ingest triples and every engineer queries at once.&rdquo;

This is the round that separates this system from most others in the library, and it should be
named explicitly:

<Claim label="inference">
Ingest load and query load are **positively correlated**. An outage produces more logs, because
errors log more than successes and retries multiply requests, and simultaneously produces more
queries, because that is when people look. The peaks coincide by construction, so any capacity plan
that treats them as independent is wrong in exactly the situation the system exists for.
</Claim>

Two consequences. First, **isolate the paths**: query capacity and ingest capacity must not come
from one pool, or an incident's searches will consume the capacity needed to record the incident.
That is a <C id="bulkhead">bulkhead</C>, and it is not optional here.

Second, decide the priority in advance, because the moment is too late. Ingest wins. A missing log
line is permanently missing; a slow query is an inconvenience to somebody who will run it again.

### Round 6 &mdash; &ldquo;We are dropping logs at the ingest tier.&rdquo;

Then drop deliberately rather than arbitrarily, because uniform shedding destroys the errors along
with the noise, and the errors are the reason anybody built this.

Shed by **severity first**: debug and info before warnings, warnings before errors, and errors
essentially never. Then by tenant against quota, so the team generating the flood absorbs its own
consequences. Sample rather than truncate where a rate must simply come down &mdash; one line in
ten with a recorded sampling rate preserves the ability to estimate the true rate, which
truncation does not.

Buffer at the agent as well. A short outage in the ingest tier then costs nothing, because the
agents hold their lines and deliver them late, and late logs are worth almost as much as prompt
ones.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Backpressure has to reach the source">
If the ingest tier accepts everything and queues internally, an overload becomes unbounded latency
and then loss with no signal. Refuse explicitly at the edge so agents can buffer, and alert on
queue depth rather than error rate &mdash; the failure here is silent and slow.
</Callout>

### Round 7 &mdash; &ldquo;Somebody just ran a thirty-day content search. It has been forty minutes.&rdquo;

Unbounded queries are the main way a shared log system fails, and the fix is that the system, not
the user, owns the bound.

Require a time range, and default it to something short. Give every query a cost estimate from the
label index before it runs, and refuse or downgrade the ones that exceed a budget. Return
**progressive results** &mdash; newest blocks first, streaming as they are scanned &mdash; because
the engineer usually stops reading after the first few matches, and a query that can be abandoned
early is one that mostly costs nothing. Cap concurrent scans per user and per tenant.

Offer sampling explicitly for the genuinely large search: scanning one block in ten answers
&ldquo;does this happen and roughly how often&rdquo; ten times faster, and that is the actual
question most of the time.

### Round 8 &mdash; &ldquo;One team's debug logging is sixty per cent of the volume.&rdquo;

Common, and mostly not a technical problem. Meter per tenant and publish the numbers, because
volume is invisible to the team producing it and obvious the moment they are shown a bill.

Technically: hard quotas with shedding at the ingest tier, separate ingest partitions so one
tenant's flood cannot make another's blocks late, and a per-tenant retention that can be shortened
independently. The <C id="tenant-isolation">isolation</C> has to be real; a shared queue with a
fairness policy behaves fairly right up to the moment it saturates.

### Round 9 &mdash; &ldquo;Nobody can actually query day twenty-nine.&rdquo;

Usually true, and worth confronting rather than claiming otherwise. Data on object storage has high
per-request latency, so a scan that touches thousands of objects is dominated by round trips rather
than bytes.

Make the tiering honest. Keep two days hot, and accept that older queries are slower by a stated
factor rather than pretending they are equivalent. Fetch cold blocks with high parallelism, since
the bandwidth is available even when the latency is not. Keep the **label index and the Bloom
filters on fast storage for the whole retention window** &mdash; they are a tiny fraction of the
size and they are what makes a cold query touch a hundred objects instead of a million.

And if the honest answer is that thirty days of content search is not affordable, say so and
propose what is: thirty days of retention with full search over the recent window, and structured
fields only over the rest.

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index labels only | Index cost stays a small fraction of storage | Content queries scan rather than seek | Query rate approached write rate |
| Bloom filters per block | High-cardinality lookup without a high-cardinality index | A false-positive scan rate, and slower than a real index | Trace lookup were the dominant query |
| Time-leading partition key | Retention is a drop; ranges prune for free | Queries without a time range are pathological | Data had no natural lifetime |
| Two days hot, rest on object storage | Most of the bill disappears | Older queries are honestly slower | All thirty days needed equal latency |
| Ingest prioritised over query | The record survives the incident | Searches degrade when they are most wanted | Logs were reproducible, which they are not |
| Severity-ordered shedding | What survives overload is what matters | Debug data disappears first, and sometimes it mattered | Every line were equally valuable |
| Per-tenant quotas and partitions | One team cannot starve another | Capacity sits reserved and idle | The system were single-tenant |
| Bounded, streaming queries | No single search can take the system down | Some legitimate large searches are refused | Query volume were low and trusted |

## Cost

Storage dominates and is easy to compute: 63 TB compressed, most of it on object storage, plus the
recent window on SSD. The label index and Bloom filters together are a low single-digit percentage
of that, which is the entire argument for the design &mdash; the rejected alternative is not
slower, it is roughly twice the storage plus a continuous merge workload.

The two elastic levers are **retention** and **the hot window**, and they behave very differently.
Halving retention halves the storage bill and changes nothing about performance. Halving the hot
window saves much less money and makes many more queries slow. Reach for retention first, which
also means the most valuable conversation about this system's cost is not with engineers.

Ingest compute is real but unremarkable, and it is dominated by compression rather than by parsing
or indexing &mdash; which is the expected shape once the index is small.

## What we did NOT build

**A full inverted index over log content.** Its cost is proportional to write volume and its
benefit to read volume, and those differ here by five orders of magnitude.

**Relevance ranking.** Log search wants everything matching, in time order. Ranking answers a
question nobody asked and would need to be maintained per write.

**Updates or deletes of individual events.** Logs are immutable. Nothing except a legal erasure
request should ever modify one, and that is best handled by making the affected partition
re-writable as a rare exception rather than by making every partition mutable.

**Strong consistency for search.** A line may be searchable a few seconds after it is written.
Making that instantaneous would force the index onto the write path, which is the choice this whole
design exists to avoid.

## Evidence

<Claim label="fact" source="elasticsearch-docs">
Index size varies substantially with mapping choices, and indexing high-cardinality fields or
enabling full-text analysis on large text fields materially increases both storage and indexing
cost. The documentation's tuning guidance is largely a list of things to stop indexing.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="clickhouse-docs">
Columnar storage with a time-leading sort key answers range-scoped queries by reading only the
relevant granules, and partitions can be dropped as a metadata operation rather than by deleting
rows. This is the mechanism behind the retention design here.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
A Bloom filter reports set membership with false positives but no false negatives, in space that
does not grow with the size of the stored values. Storage engines use it to avoid reading segments
that cannot contain a key, which is exactly the use in Round 3.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
In log-structured storage, deletion is recorded as a tombstone and the space is reclaimed only by
later compaction, so a delete-heavy workload generates continuing background work in addition to
the write itself.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-builders-library">
A service that queues work it cannot process converts overload into unbounded latency, and shedding
load at admission keeps a system responsive under conditions where queueing does not.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="sre-book">
Monitoring should alert on symptoms visible to users rather than on internal causes. For an ingest
pipeline the symptom is end-to-end lag, which moves long before any error rate does.
</Claim>

<Claim label="speculation">
The fleet size, line rate, average line length and 10:1 compression ratio are illustrative. The
compression figure in particular varies widely with log structure and is worth measuring rather
than assuming, since the storage estimate is directly proportional to it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="inference">
The claim that a full content index costs about as much as the compressed data is a working rule of
thumb rather than a constant. It depends on tokenisation, field selection and compression, and the
useful discipline is to monitor the index-to-data ratio continuously, because the number moves
whenever somebody adds a field.
</Claim>
