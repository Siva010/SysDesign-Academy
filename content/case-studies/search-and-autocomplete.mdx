## The ask

> &ldquo;Design search over a large product catalogue, including the autocomplete that fires on
> every keystroke.&rdquo;

This is two workloads sharing one name, and treating them as one system is the first mistake
available.

**Search** runs once per query. It can afford tens of milliseconds of real work: term lookups,
intersections, scoring, faceting.

**Autocomplete** runs on every keystroke. A user typing eight characters generates eight requests,
each of which must return before they type the next one. That is roughly **eight times the traffic
at a fifth of the latency budget**, for a result that matters less.

Underneath both: matching is the easy part, ranking is the product, and freshness is what you pay.

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| How large is the corpus, and how fast does it change? | Ten million documents changing hourly and ten billion changing continuously are unrelated problems. |
| How quickly must a change be searchable? | Minutes allows batch indexing. Seconds forces a streaming pipeline and changes the storage engine. |
| Which fields change often? | A field that changes every minute rewrites its whole document every minute, because index segments are immutable. |
| Is ranking personalised? | Personalisation at retrieval time destroys cacheability. Personalisation as a re-rank does not. |
| Filters and facets? | A filter is set membership with no score, which caches perfectly. Scored terms do not. Conflating them means caching nothing. |
| Autocomplete over what — past queries or document titles? | Different data source, different pipeline, different failure mode when it is empty. |

**Assumed answers:** 50 million products; price and stock change continuously while text rarely
does; text changes searchable within a minute, stock within seconds; lightly personalised ranking;
facets required; typo tolerance required; autocomplete drawn from popular past queries with
product names as a fallback.

## Requirements

**Functional:** full-text query with filters and facets; typo tolerance; prefix completion;
ranked results; pagination.

**Non-functional**

- Search p95 under 200 ms; autocomplete p95 under 50 ms end to end, which leaves roughly 20 ms
  of server budget after the network.
- A text change is searchable within 60 seconds; a stock change is reflected within 5.
- Indexing failures must not take search down, and search load must not stop indexing.
- Zero-result rate is a tracked product metric, not an engineering afterthought.

**Out of scope:** crawling and ingestion of external catalogues, training the ranking model, ads
and sponsored placement, image and vector retrieval.

## Estimate

- **50,000,000 documents** at about 2 KB of source text each → roughly **100 GB** of documents.
- The inverted index is typically the same order as the text it indexes, larger if positions and
  stored fields are kept: call it **100 to 200 GB**.
- **5,000 searches per second** at peak.
- Eight keystrokes per search → **40,000 autocomplete requests per second**.
- Catalogue updates: price and stock churn across 50 million products gives roughly
  **1,400 document updates per second**, essentially all of them to two fields.

<Claim label="inference">
The traffic profile inverts the intuition. The feature people describe as a nice touch carries
eight times the query volume of the feature they describe as the product, at a quarter of the
latency budget. Any design that treats autocomplete as &ldquo;search with a shorter query&rdquo;
has already committed to being eight times too slow.
</Claim>

<Estimator preset="storage" />

The number to watch is the working set. If the index does not fit in page cache, every term
lookup becomes a disk read, and a query touching six terms becomes six disk reads before scoring
starts. The difference between an index that fits in memory and one that does not is usually two
orders of magnitude in tail latency, and it is decided by the size estimate, not by tuning.

## Data model

| Structure | Shape | Serves |
|---|---|---|
| Document store | product id to fields | Fetching what to display |
| Inverted index | term to posting list of document ids | Matching |
| Forward index | document id to field values | Sorting, filtering, faceting |
| Prefix store | prefix to top-k completions | Autocomplete |

**Analysis is the part that quietly decides whether anything works.** Text is lowercased, split,
stemmed, stripped of stop words and expanded with synonyms before it enters the index. The query
must go through **the same chain**. When it does not, searching for a product's exact title
returns nothing, and the bug looks like a mystery rather than a mismatch.

**Filters are not queries.** A filter answers yes or no about set membership and contributes
nothing to the score, so its result is a bitset that can be cached and reused across every user
who applies it. A scored term cannot be. Systems that treat every clause as scored throw away the
largest easy optimisation available.

**Autocomplete is not a search.** A prefix match against an inverted index means scanning a range
of the term dictionary and unioning many posting lists — expensive, and worst for exactly the
short prefixes that are most common. The right structure is a precomputed map from prefix to its
top completions: one lookup, no scoring, no merging.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 — &ldquo;Why not just use LIKE on the products table?&rdquo;

Three separate reasons, and only the first is about speed.

A leading wildcard cannot use a B-tree index, because a B-tree orders by prefix and the query has
no prefix. Every such query is a full scan of the table.

It does no ranking. `LIKE` gives you a set, not an order, and the order is the product.

And it puts a scan-shaped workload on the transactional database that also takes orders. One
enthusiastic search session degrades checkout.

So: build an inverted index. Name the cost immediately — it is **a second copy of the data,
derived from the catalogue and asynchronously updated**. Search results are eventually consistent
with the catalogue from the moment this decision is made, and no amount of later engineering takes
that back.

### Round 2 — &ldquo;Stock changes every minute and the index cannot keep up.&rdquo;

This is the round that separates people who have used a search engine from people who have
operated one.

Index segments are **immutable**. Updating a document is not an update: it is a new document plus
a tombstone marking the old one deleted, with the space reclaimed later by a merge. A product
whose stock count changes every minute therefore rewrites its entire document — title,
description, attributes, all of it — every minute, and leaves a deleted version behind each time.
Merge pressure rises, disk churns, and query latency degrades for a field nobody searches on.

The fix is to **keep volatile fields out of the index**:

- Text and attributes, which change rarely, live in the index.
- Price and stock, which change constantly, live in a fast key-value store and are applied at
  query time to the results actually returned.

This costs a second lookup on the read path and saves continuous rewriting of 50 million
documents. It also decouples the two freshness requirements — 60 seconds for text, 5 seconds for
stock — which the original single-index design had accidentally fused into one.

### Round 3 — &ldquo;The index no longer fits on one machine.&rdquo;

Two ways to split an inverted index, and the choice is not close in practice.

**By document.** Each shard holds a complete index over a subset of documents. Every query goes to
every shard; each returns its local top *k*; the coordinator merges. Document count and indexing
throughput scale linearly.

**By term.** Each shard owns a subset of terms. A query touches only the shards holding its terms,
so fan-out is smaller. But a multi-term query must intersect posting lists that live on different
machines, which means shipping posting lists across the network — and posting lists are longest
for exactly the common terms that appear in most queries.

Document sharding wins because its cost scales with the number of shards while term sharding's
cost scales with the popularity of the terms, which is the thing you cannot control.

One consequence worth knowing: with document sharding, term statistics are per-shard, so a
relevance score computed on shard A is not strictly comparable to one from shard B. With
reasonably even document distribution the error is small and universally tolerated. Correcting it
requires an extra round trip to exchange global frequencies, which is a real technique and almost
never worth it.

### Round 4 — &ldquo;Search p99 is 2 seconds and every shard reports p99 of 100 ms.&rdquo;

Both statements are true. With twenty shards and each slow 1% of the time, roughly **18% of
queries touch at least one slow shard**, and the query is as slow as its slowest shard.

- **Fewer, larger shards.** Fan-out is a cost, and more shards is not automatically better. There
  is an optimum where per-shard work and fan-out width balance, and it is usually far fewer shards
  than people expect.
- **Hedged requests.** After the p95 elapses, send the same shard request to a replica and take
  whichever answers first. A few percent more load buys a dramatically better tail.
- **Deadline with partial results.** Return what arrived. Search results are allowed to be
  incomplete — but measure how often it happens and for which shards, because a shard that is
  quietly excluded from every query is a shard whose documents have become unfindable.

### Round 5 — &ldquo;Forty thousand autocomplete requests per second.&rdquo;

Do not run a search. Precompute.

Build a map from prefix to its top completions, scored by past query frequency. A lookup is one
key fetch and no scoring. For a few million distinct popular queries and prefixes up to about
twenty characters, this is tens of millions of small entries — a few gigabytes, comfortably
memory-resident, and completely read-only between builds.

Two properties make this cheap. The prefix distribution is **extremely skewed**: there are only a
few hundred plausible two-character prefixes, and they absorb most of the traffic, so a small
cache achieves a very high hit rate. And the data is derived, so a replica in every region is
correct by construction.

Then the cheapest optimisation in the whole design: **debounce on the client.** Firing after
roughly 100 ms of no typing rather than on every keystroke removes more than half the traffic and
improves the result, because a partial word matches worse than a finished one. It belongs in the
architecture, not in a client backlog, because the capacity plan depends on it.

### Round 6 — &ldquo;A product launches and one prefix takes a million requests per second.&rdquo;

A hot key — and this is the easy kind.

**A read-only hot key is solved by copying it.** The prefix store is derived, immutable between
builds and small, so it can be replicated to every serving node and cached at the edge with a
short TTL. There is no coordination to do, because there is nothing to keep consistent.

Contrast that with the write hot key in the proximity study, where a cell that everyone is writing
to cannot be copied at all — a mutable thing has one authority by definition. Recognising which of
the two you have is worth more than any specific technique, because it tells you immediately
whether the answer is *replicate* or *split*.

One detail that is easy to omit: on a cache miss, a thousand simultaneous requests for the same
hot prefix must not become a thousand origin requests. Coalesce them into one and let the rest
wait for its result — otherwise the moment the entry expires is the moment the origin falls over.

### Round 7 — &ldquo;Users add out-of-stock products to their basket.&rdquo;

Do not try to make the index authoritative. Search returns candidates; the authority decides.

The results actually displayed — twenty-four of them, not ten thousand — are checked against the
stock service before rendering, and out-of-stock items are filtered or marked. The cost is a
result count that is slightly wrong and a page that occasionally shows twenty-one items instead of
twenty-four. Both are acceptable. Showing a purchasable item that cannot be purchased is not.

This is the same structure as the seat map in the ticket study and the driver list in the
proximity study: **a large, fast, stale read narrows the field, and a small, exact read decides.**
Once you see the shape, it transfers to any problem where the authority is too expensive to
consult at retrieval width.

### Round 8 — &ldquo;The indexing pipeline fell behind six hours ago and nobody noticed.&rdquo;

Queue depth is the wrong metric, because a pipeline that is dropping messages has a queue depth of
zero. Measure **end-to-end freshness**: stamp each change at the source, and record the time from
that stamp to the moment the document is searchable. That number is a service level objective and
should page.

Three more pieces:

- **A poisonous document must not stop the pipeline.** One product with a malformed field should
  be dead-lettered with its error, not retried forever ahead of everything behind it.
- **Full reindex must be routine.** Not a disaster procedure — a Tuesday.
- **The index schema is not migratable in place.** Changing a tokeniser, a stemmer or a synonym
  list changes what is in the posting lists, so every document must be reprocessed. The only
  correct migration is build a new index, verify it, swap the alias, keep the old one until you are
  sure. That means **capacity for two full indexes**, which is a real cost that surprises people
  the first time they need it.

### Round 9 — &ldquo;Relevance is bad, and everyone has an opinion.&rdquo;

The engineering contribution here is not a better ranking. It is making relevance **measurable**,
so that opinions become experiments.

- Log queries, results shown, and clicks. Without this, every ranking discussion is anecdote.
- Track the **zero-result rate** and the **no-click rate** by query. They point at the failures
  users never report, because a user who finds nothing leaves rather than complaining.
- Hold an offline evaluation set for fast iteration, and confirm online, because offline metrics
  and user behaviour disagree often enough to matter.

And one structural rule: **personalise the re-rank, not the retrieval.** Retrieving the top few
hundred candidates identically for everyone keeps that stage cacheable and comparable across
users; personalising the final ordering of those few hundred costs little and caches poorly, which
is fine at that width. Personalising retrieval makes every query unique, and every cache in the
system stops working at once.

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Derived inverted index | Fast matching and real ranking | Search is eventually consistent, forever | The corpus were small enough for the database |
| Volatile fields outside the index | No segment churn from stock changes | A second lookup on the read path | Volatile fields needed to be searchable |
| Document sharding | Even scaling, simple indexing | Every query fans out to every shard | Queries were single-term and posting lists small |
| Precomputed prefix store | Autocomplete at 40k rps for almost nothing | Completions are only as fresh as the last build | Completions had to reflect the catalogue instantly |
| Authority check on the shown page | Never offer an unbuyable item | Slightly wrong result counts | Stock changed rarely |
| Personalised re-rank only | Retrieval stays cacheable | Less personalisation than is theoretically possible | Caching were not on the critical path |
| Alias plus rebuild for schema changes | Safe analysis changes with no downtime | Capacity for two full indexes | Analysis never changed, which it does |

## Cost

1. **Index serving memory.** The dominant cost, and the one with a cliff in it: an index that
   fits in page cache and one that does not differ by two orders of magnitude in tail latency, and
   only one of them meets the requirement.
2. **Indexing compute**, proportional to the change rate. Removing volatile fields from the index
   cut this by most of its original size, which is why Round 2 is worth more than it looks.
3. **The prefix store**, small, replicated everywhere, and cheap. It carries eight times the
   traffic of search at a small fraction of the cost, precisely because it does no work at request
   time.
4. **Headroom for a second index copy**, needed permanently rather than occasionally.

A cheaper design drops typo tolerance and synonyms, both of which increase index size and query
work. What that sacrifices is the recall of exactly the queries users type badly, which is most of
them — so it lowers cost by lowering the zero-result rate's own denominator. Measure before
trading it away.

## What we did NOT build

- **A custom search engine.** Inverted indexes, scoring, merge policies and analysis chains are
  decades of well-understood engineering. Building one is justified by a requirement no existing
  engine can meet, and &ldquo;ours would be faster&rdquo; is not that requirement.
- **Term sharding.** Fewer shards per query, at a network cost proportional to the popularity of
  the terms. The cost scales with the thing you least control.
- **Personalised retrieval.** It makes every query unique, which disables every cache in the
  system in exchange for an improvement that a re-rank of the top few hundred delivers nearly all
  of.
- **The index as the source of truth.** It is derived, and a derived store that accumulates
  unrecoverable state has silently become an authority — the failure the CQRS pattern warns about
  directly.
- **Vector or semantic retrieval.** A real and increasingly standard addition, deliberately out of
  scope here. It does not remove the lexical path — exact identifiers, part numbers and brand
  names are matched, not approximated — and it changes the freshness and cost profile enough to be
  its own design, not a paragraph in this one.

## Evidence

<Claim label="fact" source="elasticsearch-docs">
Search is near-real-time rather than immediately consistent: a document becomes searchable after a
refresh, and the refresh interval is a configurable trade between indexing throughput and
freshness.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="elasticsearch-docs">
Index segments are immutable. Updating a document writes a new version and marks the old one
deleted; the space is reclaimed only when segments are merged. Deleting and reindexing is what an
update physically is.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="elasticsearch-docs">
The same analysis chain must be applied to indexed text and to query text for terms to match, and
filter clauses that do not contribute to scoring can be cached and reused independently of the
scored part of a query.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
An inverted index maps each term to a posting list of the documents containing it, so a
conjunctive query becomes an intersection of posting lists, and query cost is governed by the
length of the shortest list involved.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="tail-at-scale">
When a request fans out to many servers and must wait for all of them, the overall latency is
governed by the slowest response, so tail latency degrades as fan-out width grows. Hedged requests
to a second replica are described as an effective mitigation at modest extra load.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-builders-library">
Bounding work with explicit deadlines and returning partial results rather than waiting
indefinitely is established practice for fan-out services, on the grounds that a late complete
answer is often worth less than a prompt partial one.
</Claim>
