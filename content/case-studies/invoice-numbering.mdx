## The ask

> &ldquo;Design identifiers for a payments platform. Orders need ids at high volume, and invoices
> in several jurisdictions must carry a gapless sequential number per legal entity per
> year.&rdquo;

The trap is hearing one problem. There are two, they want opposite things, and a candidate who
designs a single scheme for both will produce something that is either too slow for orders or
illegal for invoices.

An **order id** should be unguessable, cheap to mint anywhere, and never a coordination point.
An **invoice number** must be sequential with no gaps, which is a statement about a shared counter
and therefore about serialisation. One is designed to avoid agreement; the other is defined by it.

The interesting work is establishing that these are different populations with different rates,
and then making the expensive requirement apply only to the small one.

## Clarify

| Question | Why it changes the design |
|---|---|
| Does gapless apply everywhere, or in specific jurisdictions? | If it is a subset, most invoices avoid the serialisation point entirely. Designing for the strictest rule globally is the expensive mistake. |
| Gapless per what? Legal entity, document series, year? | This is the shard key for the whole problem. The narrower the scope, the lower the rate on any one sequence. |
| Must a cancelled invoice keep its number? | Almost always yes, corrected by a credit note. If numbers could be reclaimed, the design would be very different and much worse. |
| What fraction of orders become invoices? | Decides whether the constrained path carries 5% of traffic or all of it. |
| Is the order id ever shown to a customer? | If yes, it must not leak volume. A visible incrementing id tells a competitor your daily order count. |
| Who owns &ldquo;the year&rdquo;? | Year rollover happens in a timezone, and the entity's jurisdiction decides which one. |

## Requirements

**Functional.** Mint an order id on every order. Allocate an invoice number when an invoice is
issued, sequential and gapless within its (entity, series, year). Render, store, and retain both.
Support cancellation by credit note. Answer an auditor's question about completeness.

**Non-functional.** Never reuse a number. Never skip one. A retried request must not consume two.
Allocation must survive a crash at any point. One entity's volume must not stall another's.

**Out of scope.** Tax calculation, invoice rendering and delivery, dunning, and the actual legal
advice, which is not an engineering artefact.

## Estimate

<Estimator preset="traffic" />

- **20,000 orders per second** at peak; roughly 1.5 billion a year.
- An order id at 16 bytes, indexed, is a few hundred gigabytes a year with its index. Unremarkable.
- **5% of orders produce an invoice**: about **1,000 invoices per second** at peak.
- Those invoices are spread over **200 legal entities** and several document series each.

Now the number that decides the design:

<Claim label="inference">
One thousand invoices per second across roughly six hundred (entity, series) pairs is **under two
per second on any one sequence**, and the busiest is perhaps fifty. A serialised counter at fifty
writes per second is *nothing* &mdash; a single row updated fifty times a second is comfortable on
hardware from twenty years ago.
</Claim>

That single observation is the whole design, and it is worth being explicit about why:

<Claim label="inference">
The gapless requirement is impossible at 20,000 per second and trivial at 50. The engineering task
is therefore not to make a distributed gapless counter &mdash; which is a genuinely hard problem,
and one nobody here needs solved. It is to establish that the constrained path carries a
thousandth of the traffic, and then to keep it that way.
</Claim>

A candidate who starts inventing distributed sequence protocols has skipped the arithmetic and is
solving a problem the requirements do not pose.

## Data model

| Entity | Key | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Order | order id | Time-ordered, unguessable, minted anywhere without coordination |
| Sequence | (entity id, series, year) | A counter row. The only serialisation point in the system. |
| Invoice | invoice id | Carries its allocated number, its sequence key, and the idempotency key that produced it |
| Allocation | idempotency key | Maps a logical issuance to the number it received. Makes retries free. |
| Credit note | credit note id | Its own sequence. Corrects an invoice; never replaces its number. |

Two identifiers per invoice, and the separation is deliberate. The **invoice id** is an internal,
unguessable primary key minted like an order id. The **invoice number** is the human- and
regulator-facing sequential string. Conflating them forces every foreign key in the system through
the serialisation point, which is exactly what we are trying to keep small.

## Pressure rounds

### Round 1 &mdash; &ldquo;Why not use a random UUID for everything?&rdquo;

For orders it is nearly right, and the reason to hesitate is not uniqueness but **insertion
order**.

A random identifier used as the clustering key of a <C id="btree">B-tree</C> writes to a random leaf
page every time. With a working set larger than memory, each insert touches a different page, so
every page must be read before being written and dirtied for the sake of one row. Insertions
arriving in ascending order all land in the same rightmost page, which stays in memory and is
written once per fill.

The fix is a **time-ordered** identifier: a millisecond timestamp in the high bits, then a node or
shard identifier, then a per-millisecond counter. Ordered enough to keep insertion local,
unguessable enough not to leak, and mintable with no coordination whatsoever.

<Callout kind="info" title="What the timestamp prefix costs">
Time-ordered ids leak approximate creation time, and adjacent ids from one node reveal that node's
rate. For an internal order id that is fine. If the identifier is shown to customers or partners,
decide deliberately whether you are comfortable publishing your volume, because a visible
incrementing id is a business metric anyone can read.
</Callout>

### Round 2 &mdash; &ldquo;Fine. Use a database sequence for the invoice numbers.&rdquo;

This is the round that separates people who have shipped this from people who have read about it,
because the obvious answer is wrong for a reason that is documented and still surprises almost
everyone.

<Claim label="fact" source="postgres-docs">
Sequences are non-transactional. A number obtained from `nextval` is not returned if the
surrounding transaction rolls back, and concurrent transactions receive distinct values that may
commit out of order. Sequences are explicitly documented as producing gaps.
</Claim>

That property is not a defect; it is the entire point. A sequence is fast **because** it refuses to
participate in your transaction. Participating is what would serialise it. So the mechanism whose
name sounds exactly like the requirement is the one mechanism guaranteed not to satisfy it.

The same is true of auto-increment columns, for the same reason.

### Round 3 &mdash; &ldquo;So how do you actually get a gapless number?&rdquo;

You take the lock the sequence exists to avoid. The counter becomes an ordinary row, updated inside
the same transaction that writes the invoice:

    UPDATE invoice_sequence SET last = last + 1
    WHERE entity_id = ? AND series = ? AND year = ?
    RETURNING last

Because the update holds a row lock until commit, every other issuance for that sequence waits.
Because the increment is in the transaction, a rollback returns the number. Gapless follows
directly from both facts, and neither can be relaxed.

This is a deliberate serialisation point, and the honest way to present it is with the number from
the estimate attached: the sequence is serialised at about fifty writes per second in the worst
case, and a row lock held for the length of a small transaction supports that with room to spare.

<Claim label="inference">
The design is not clever, and that is the argument for it. Every alternative &mdash; block
pre-allocation, distributed counters, optimistic retry &mdash; either reintroduces gaps or costs
more than the lock it replaces. When a requirement genuinely demands serialisation, the useful
skill is bounding its scope, not evading it.
</Claim>

### Round 4 &mdash; &ldquo;The client times out and retries. Do we burn two numbers?&rdquo;

With the design so far, yes, and this is the failure that produces a gap in the sequence you
promised had none &mdash; because the first transaction may well have committed.

The issuance carries an <C id="idempotency-key">idempotency key</C> derived from the invoice's
identity rather than from the attempt. Allocation writes `(key, number)` in the same transaction
that increments the counter, with a uniqueness constraint on the key. A retry either finds the
existing row and returns the same number, or loses the race on the constraint and reads it.

<Claim label="inference">
Delivery is <C id="at-least-once">at-least-once</C> and no design here changes that. What is
exactly-once is the **allocation**, which holds because the counter increment and the allocation
record commit atomically. Split them across two transactions or two stores and the guarantee is
gone, which is why this must not become a distributed transaction.
</Claim>

### Round 5 &mdash; &ldquo;An invoice was issued in error. Take the number back.&rdquo;

No. Cancel by issuing a **credit note**, which carries its own number from its own sequence and
references the invoice it corrects. The original number stays allocated, its invoice stays stored,
and the pair records what happened.

This is worth arguing with an interviewer who pushes, because reclamation is not merely
inconvenient: a number that once existed and later does not is precisely the gap the requirement
prohibits, and it destroys the audit trail that makes the number worth having. The domain's answer
&mdash; corrections are additions, never deletions &mdash; is the same shape as an append-only
<C id="audit-log">log</C>, and recognising that is the transferable part.

### Round 6 &mdash; &ldquo;It is 23:59 on the 31st of December.&rdquo;

The sequence key contains a year, so at some instant a new sequence begins at one. Which instant is
a question about the entity's jurisdiction, not about your servers, and getting it wrong is a
filing error rather than a bug.

Take the boundary from the **entity's** configured timezone, evaluated at the moment of issuance,
and store both the number and the resolved period on the invoice so the decision is auditable
later. Never take it from the server's clock, which is in UTC and belongs to whichever region
happened to serve the request. Related: an entity that moves jurisdiction changes its boundary,
which is a data change, not a deploy.

<Claim label="convention">
Several jurisdictions require invoice numbers that are sequential and unbroken within a period,
and the details &mdash; whether the period is a calendar year, whether series may be split by
branch or document type, what a correction must reference &mdash; vary between them and change
over time. Treat the scope of the sequence key as configuration to be confirmed with counsel per
market, not as a constant to be compiled in.
</Claim>

### Round 7 &mdash; &ldquo;One entity issues forty per cent of all invoices.&rdquo;

Then its sequence runs at a few hundred writes per second and is still comfortable, but it is now
worth being precise about what cannot be done: **the sequence cannot be sharded.** Splitting one
entity's numbering across two counters produces two interleaved sequences, and interleaving is
indistinguishable from gaps.

What is available is narrowing the key. If the jurisdiction permits a **series** per branch, per
site, or per document type, then each series is an independent gapless sequence and the load
divides legitimately. Note the shape of this: the relief comes from a change in the *requirement's
scope*, negotiated with the business, and not from anything technical. Recognising which
constraints are negotiable is most of what senior judgment consists of here.

Keep the entity's sequence rows away from the noisiest tenants' — a lock convoy on one entity's
counter must not become everyone's latency.

### Round 8 &mdash; &ldquo;Pre-allocate blocks of a thousand numbers to each server.&rdquo;

This is the most attractive wrong answer in the problem, and it should be refused precisely.

A server holding numbers 4001&ndash;5000 that dies after using six leaves 994 numbers that will
never appear on an invoice. That is a gap. Every variant fails the same way: the block is either
committed before use, which loses the unused remainder, or after, which means two servers can hold
the same block.

Block allocation is the right answer to the *adjacent* problem &mdash; identifiers that must be
unique and roughly increasing but may skip. It is worth naming the distinction out loud, because
&ldquo;sequential&rdquo; and &ldquo;gapless&rdquo; are used interchangeably in conversation and
mean different things here. Only one of them forbids the optimisation.

### Round 9 &mdash; &ldquo;An auditor asks you to prove no invoice is missing.&rdquo;

The design makes this cheap, which is the payoff for accepting the lock:

    SELECT entity_id, series, year, COUNT(*), MIN(number), MAX(number)
    FROM invoices GROUP BY entity_id, series, year

Completeness holds when the count equals the maximum minus the minimum plus one, per group. Run it
on a schedule rather than when asked, and alert on a discrepancy, because the useful time to learn
that the invariant broke is the day it breaks and not the day somebody audits it.

The migration case is the one that will actually produce a discrepancy: importing a legacy system
that issued numbers up to 84,312 means seeding the counter above that and accepting that history
began mid-sequence, so the completeness check needs a per-sequence floor rather than assuming one.
Run both systems' numbering side by side through a cutover, using
<C id="expand-contract">expand and contract</C>, and switch reads only once the new sequence has
been issuing correctly for a period.

## Trade-off ledger

| Decision | Chosen | Gave up | Would change if |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate id schemes for orders and invoices | The expensive rule applies to 5% of traffic | Two concepts to explain and keep straight | Invoice volume equalled order volume |
| Time-ordered order ids | Index locality, no coordination | Approximate creation time and rate are inferable | Ids were public and volume were sensitive |
| Counter row in the invoice transaction | Genuinely gapless, and simple to reason about | A deliberate serialisation point per sequence | Gaps were permitted, which makes a sequence correct and far faster |
| Sequence keyed by entity, series and year | Per-sequence load stays trivial | The key is regulatory configuration, not a constant | A jurisdiction demanded one global sequence |
| Idempotency record in the same transaction | Retries cannot consume a number | Every issuance carries a key from its caller | Callers could not supply a stable key |
| Credit notes rather than reclamation | The audit trail is append-only | More documents, and users must understand two kinds | Numbers could legally be reused, which they cannot |
| No block pre-allocation | No gaps from a dead holder | The lock stays on the hot path | The requirement were sequential-but-skippable |
| Scheduled completeness check | The invariant is monitored, not assumed | A recurring query over a large table | The count were too large to aggregate, which it is not |

## Cost

The order path costs what its volume costs and nothing extra: ids are minted in process, so there
is no service, no round trip, and no dependency to run.

The invoice path's cost is not throughput but **latency under a lock**, and the sensitivity worth
knowing is that it scales with transaction duration rather than with invoice rate. A transaction
that holds the counter for two milliseconds supports hundreds per second on one sequence; one that
calls a tax service while holding it supports perhaps ten. Keeping foreign work outside the lock is
worth more than any amount of hardware.

Storage is unremarkable. The audit obligation is the durable cost: invoices are retained for years
by law, which makes this a system whose data outlives most of the services around it, and a good
reason for the invoice record to be self-describing rather than needing a join to something that
may have been refactored away.

## What we did NOT build

**A distributed gapless counter.** It is a real problem, it needs consensus on every allocation,
and the arithmetic says nobody here has it. Solving it would be the most impressive wrong answer
available.

**One identifier for both purposes.** Every foreign key in the platform would then run through the
serialisation point, and the 5% path would become the 100% path.

**Number reclamation.** Prohibited by the requirement, and it would destroy the completeness check
that makes the requirement verifiable.

**A central id service.** Time-ordered ids need no coordination, so a service to hand them out
would add a network hop and a shared dependency in exchange for nothing.

## Evidence

<Claim label="fact" source="postgres-docs">
PostgreSQL documents that sequences are non-transactional: a value obtained from `nextval` is not
returned to the sequence when the transaction rolls back, so gaps are expected and the mechanism is
explicitly unsuitable for a gapless requirement.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="mysql-docs">
MySQL's AUTO_INCREMENT behaves the same way. Values consumed by a rolled-back or failed statement
are not reused, and the lock mode chosen for InnoDB trades gap-freedom against concurrency rather
than offering both.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Inserting rows in random key order into a B-tree scatters writes across pages and forces reads
before writes as the index outgrows memory, whereas ascending keys concentrate insertions in the
rightmost pages. This is the mechanism behind Round 1.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
B-tree insertion cost is dominated by page splits and by whether the target page is resident.
Sequential insertion patterns keep the working set for writes small, which is why the ordering of
an identifier is a storage-engine concern and not only an aesthetic one.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="stripe-eng">
Idempotency keys supplied by the caller allow a retried request to return the result of the
original rather than perform the operation again, and the key must identify the logical operation
rather than the attempt for this to hold.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-builders-library">
A client that times out cannot distinguish a request that failed from one that succeeded with a
lost response, which is why the retry must be made safe at the server rather than avoided at the
client.
</Claim>

<Claim label="speculation">
The order rate, the 5% invoice fraction, and the two hundred legal entities are illustrative
figures chosen so the arithmetic is legible. The conclusion they support &mdash; that per-sequence
load is orders of magnitude below total load &mdash; holds across a wide range of inputs, and is
the first thing to re-derive for a real platform.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention">
Sequential invoice numbering obligations exist in a number of jurisdictions and differ in scope,
period, and what a correcting document must reference. Nothing here is legal advice; the design
treats the sequence key as configuration precisely because the rule is a moving external input
rather than an engineering constant.
</Claim>
