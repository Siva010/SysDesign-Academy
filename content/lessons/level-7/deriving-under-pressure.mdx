## The problem

You know the method. You know the primitives. You sit down, the interviewer says
&ldquo;design a system for scheduling deliveries&rdquo;, and forty-five minutes later you have
produced a diagram, answered some questions, and have no idea whether it went well.

The gap between knowing the framework and executing it is the same gap as between knowing the
rules of chess and playing. This lesson closes it with a transcript: **what to actually say, in
what order, and what each move buys.**

## Intuition

Three things the framework does not tell you, and they matter more than the framework.

**The first two minutes set the frame.** If you start drawing boxes, you have signalled that you
design by recall. If you start asking questions whose answers change the design, you have
signalled the opposite, and the interviewer relaxes.

**Narrate the search, not just the answer.** The interviewer cannot see you thinking. &ldquo;At
40,000 reads per second the primary is the constraint, so the question is caching or
replicas&rdquo; is worth far more than silently drawing a cache. They are assessing reasoning,
and reasoning is only visible if you speak it.

**Time is the scarcest resource.** Forty-five minutes is roughly: five clarifying, five
estimating, five modelling, ten designing, ten under pressure, ten on failure and trade-offs.
Spending twenty minutes on requirements means never reaching the part that differentiates you.

## Concretely

<Example title="The first fifteen minutes, transcribed">

**Interviewer:** &ldquo;Design a system for scheduling deliveries.&rdquo;

**You (0:00):**
&ldquo;Before I design anything, let me pin down what changes the shape of this. How many
deliveries a day, and how far ahead are they scheduled? Is a delivery assigned to a specific
courier, and can that change? What happens if we fail to dispatch one on time — is it a delay or
a lost order?&rdquo;

*What this buys:* three questions, each of whose answers changes the design. Volume sizes the
system. Assignment tells you whether this is a contention problem. The failure cost tells you how
much correctness machinery is justified.

**Interviewer:** &ldquo;Two million a day, scheduled up to a week ahead. Assigned to a courier at
dispatch time, and reassignment happens. A missed dispatch is a very unhappy customer.&rdquo;

**You (2:00):**
&ldquo;Two million a day is about 23 per second average. Delivery demand is concentrated —
mornings and evenings — so call it 100 to 200 per second at peak. That is a small write rate. The
interesting parts are going to be the scheduling and the assignment, not the throughput.

&ldquo;Storage: two million a day, seven days of future schedule, so around 14 million pending at
any time, plus history. At a kilobyte each that is well under a terabyte. Also not the hard
part.&rdquo;

*What this buys:* the numbers, out loud, with the conclusion drawn. You have just eliminated
sharding and streaming platforms from the conversation, and you did it with arithmetic rather
than assertion.

**You (4:00):**
&ldquo;So what makes this hard: it is a scheduling problem — do this at a specific time, exactly
once, at scale — with an assignment problem attached that has contention, because two dispatchers
must not assign the same courier to overlapping deliveries. There is probably a geospatial
element in choosing which courier, but I would like to confirm that is in scope before I spend
time on it.&rdquo;

*What this buys:* you have named the primitives and asked permission to scope. The interviewer
now knows you can see the structure, and they can steer you toward the part they want.

**Interviewer:** &ldquo;Courier selection is out of scope. Assume you are given a courier.&rdquo;

**You (5:00):**
&ldquo;Good, that removes the geospatial piece. So: a store of scheduled deliveries, and a
dispatcher that picks up work as it becomes due.

&ldquo;The simplest version: deliveries in a table with a due time and a status, a partition key
of delivery id, and a secondary structure ordered by due time. A dispatcher polls for due work
and claims it with a conditional update, so two dispatchers cannot claim the same delivery. At
200 per second, one database handles this comfortably.&rdquo;

*What this buys:* a complete design, deliberately small, with the contention question already
answered. You have not added a queue, a cache or a second service, and you can justify every
component.

**You (8:00):**
&ldquo;Two things I would flag as weak. Polling for due work does not scale gracefully if the
poll interval has to be short, and I have made the claim atomic but I have not said what happens
if a dispatcher claims a delivery and then dies. Which of those would you like me to go
into?&rdquo;

*What this buys:* you have found your own weaknesses before the interviewer did, and offered
them a choice. This is the single strongest move available in a design interview, and almost
nobody makes it.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

The transcript demonstrates five moves. Each is small and each changes how the rest of the
interview goes.

**Move 1 — ask questions that change the design.** Not "what tech stack?" but "what happens when
this fails?" The test of a good clarifying question is whether two different answers would lead
you to two different designs.

**Move 2 — convert to per-second and draw the conclusion.** The number alone is not the point.
&ldquo;23 per second, so this is not a throughput problem&rdquo; is the point.

**Move 3 — name the primitives out loud.** It tells the interviewer you are reasoning from
structure, and it gives you a plan for the remaining time.

**Move 4 — design the smallest thing, and say that you are doing so.** &ldquo;This is the
simplest version and I expect to evolve it&rdquo; pre-empts the criticism that you have
under-designed, and sets up the pressure rounds as evolution rather than correction.

**Move 5 — critique your own design and offer a choice.** It demonstrates that you evaluate your
work the way a reviewer would, and it lets the interviewer spend the remaining time on what they
care about.

<Callout kind="info" title="What to do when you do not know">
Say so, then reason. &ldquo;I have not operated one at that scale. From first principles I would
expect the constraint to be the number of concurrent claims on the same rows, because that is
what serialises. Is that where you were going?&rdquo;

Interviewers can distinguish a gap from a bluff easily, and only one of the two is
disqualifying.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Managing the clock">

Rough allocation for 45 minutes, and what to cut when you are behind:

| Phase | Minutes | Cut it by |
|---|---|---|
| Clarify | 5 | Stating assumptions instead of asking, and moving on |
| Estimate | 5 | Doing only the one number that decides the architecture |
| Model | 5 | Naming the access patterns without drawing the schema |
| Design v1 | 10 | Drawing fewer boxes and saying what you would add |
| Pressure | 10 | This is the part being assessed. Do not cut it |
| Failure and trade-offs | 10 | Also being assessed. Do not cut it |

The two phases people over-spend on are clarification and the data model, and the two they run
out of time for are pressure and failure — which are the ones that differentiate.

If you are 20 minutes in and still discussing requirements, say: &ldquo;I am going to assume X
and Y and start designing, and we can revisit if those are wrong.&rdquo; That recovery is itself
a positive signal.

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Silent designing',
      symptom:
        'You produce a good diagram and the feedback says "unclear reasoning" or "hard to follow".',
      cause:
        'The thinking happened internally. The interviewer assessed the artefact rather than the reasoning, and the artefact is the smaller part.',
      mitigation:
        'Narrate the search: what you are considering, what you rejected, and why. Silence is not read as competence.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Running out of time before the pressure rounds',
      symptom:
        'The interview ends during the initial design, with the differentiating phases untouched.',
      cause: 'Over-spending on clarification and the data model.',
      mitigation:
        'Watch the clock explicitly. At 15 minutes you should have a design on the board, even a small one.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Defending a decision that has been shown to be wrong',
      symptom:
        'The interviewer challenges a choice, you defend it, and the interview becomes adversarial.',
      cause:
        'Treating the challenge as an attack rather than as information.',
      mitigation:
        'Take it seriously and answer on the merits. If they are right, say so and adjust. Changing your mind on evidence is explicitly assessed.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Restarting when the requirements change',
      symptom:
        '"Now it is 100 times bigger" produces a blank page and a new design.',
      cause:
        'The original design had no derivation, so there is nothing to evolve.',
      mitigation:
        'Identify which component saturates first at the new number, change that one, and name the new problem the change created.',
    },
  ]}

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Present a deliberately minimal first design and evolve it, rather than a complete one up front"
  options={[
    'Present the full production architecture immediately',
    'Present the minimal design and evolve it under pressure',
    'Present the minimal design without saying it is minimal',
  ]}
  why="An interview is assessed on reasoning, and reasoning is only visible in transitions. A complete architecture presented at once has no visible derivation, so the interviewer cannot tell whether you derived it or recalled it."
  gain="Every subsequent addition is justified by a constraint that has appeared, which is exactly what an interviewer wants to see. It also leaves room for the pressure rounds, which is where the assessment happens."
  sacrifice="It looks less impressive in the first five minutes. An interviewer who is pattern-matching against a checklist may briefly think you are under-designing."
  failureModes={[
    'Not saying it is deliberately minimal, so it reads as naive',
    'Starting so small that you run out of time before reaching a realistic design',
    'Being unable to evolve it, which exposes that the minimalism was not a choice',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Not applicable, but the preparation equivalent is: you must actually be able to evolve it, which requires knowing the primitives rather than one architecture."
  cost="A few minutes of apparent under-confidence, repaid across the rest of the interview."
  whenNot="If the interviewer explicitly asks for the production architecture, or says they have limited time and want the end state, give them that. Reading the room beats following a script — and the fact that you chose the approach deliberately is what you should say when you do."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="hello-interview">
Interview guidance consistently emphasises communication and structured progression over
completeness of the final diagram, and identifies requirements clarification followed by
estimation as the standard opening.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="exponent">
Mock interview guidance emphasises narrating reasoning aloud, on the basis that interviewers
assess the visible thought process rather than only the artefact produced.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="fundamentals-arch">
Architecture decisions should be recorded with their alternatives and reasoning. The interview
equivalent is stating what you rejected and why, which converts a diagram into a defensible
design.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'Starting with the architecture to show breadth.',
      why: 'You commit to decisions before knowing anything that would justify them, and you spend the rest of the interview defending choices made blind.',
      instead:
        'Ask three questions whose answers would change the design, then estimate, then design. The breadth shows up later, in the pressure rounds, where it counts.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Answering "what would you improve?" with "nothing".',
      why: 'Every design has a weakest part. Not knowing yours reads as either overconfidence or a shallow model of the system.',
      instead:
        'Name a specific weakness with a threshold: "the dispatcher poll interval is the weak point above roughly N per second, and here is what I would do instead."',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Treating a challenge as an attack.',
      why: 'Interviewers challenge to see whether you reason or defend. Defending a position you cannot support is worse than changing it.',
      instead:
        'Engage on the merits. "That is a fair point — if writes are that concentrated, my partition key is wrong. Let me change it" is a strong moment, not a weak one.',
    },
  ]}
