## The problem

The list of system design interview questions looks endless: URL shortener, chat, feed, ride
hailing, food delivery, video streaming, ticket booking, ad serving, metrics platform, job
scheduler, payment system, search engine.

Preparing by memorising architectures for each is a losing strategy. There are more questions
than you can memorise, the interviewer will change the requirements, and you will be asked
something not on the list.

But the list is misleading. **Ride hailing and food delivery are the same problem.** So are
Instagram and Twitter. So are ticket booking and flash sales. The nouns differ; the underlying
difficulty does not.

## Intuition

Ask, of any prompt: **what makes this hard?**

Not what the product does — what makes it *difficult to build*. Strip away the domain and you
find a small number of recurring difficulties:

- Something must be uniquely identified without coordination.
- One write must reach many readers.
- One item is far more popular than the rest.
- A duplicate request must not double-apply.
- A limited resource has concurrent claimants.
- The data is too large to pass through your application.
- Text must be matched and ranked.
- Points on a map must be queried by proximity.
- Enormous volumes of timestamped data must be ingested and summarised.
- Work must happen later, once, reliably.
- Events from different sources must be correlated.

That is eleven. **Every system design prompt is a combination of two or three of them**, and
each has a small set of known approaches with known trade-offs.

Recognising the combination is the transferable skill. It is also what lets you say something
useful about a system you have never thought about before.

## Concretely

<Example title="The same problem, different nouns">

| Prompt | Primitives | The actual difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| Ride hailing | geo-index, scheduling | Find nearby drivers fast; match them exactly once |
| Food delivery | geo-index, scheduling | Identical, with different latency tolerance |
| Twitter timeline | fanout, hot-key | One write reaches many readers; some accounts are enormous |
| Instagram feed | fanout, hot-key, large-object | Same, plus images that must not pass through your app |
| Ticket booking | inventory-contention, exactly-once-effect | Concurrent claims on limited units; no double-selling |
| Flash sale | inventory-contention, hot-key | Same, concentrated on one item |
| Payments | exactly-once-effect | A timeout is an unknown outcome, not a failure |
| Order placement | exactly-once-effect, scheduling | Same, with a workflow across services |
| Metrics platform | time-series-ingest | Cardinality, retention, rollups |
| Logging platform | time-series-ingest, search-index | Same, plus text search over it |
| URL shortener | id-generation, hot-key | Unique codes without coordination; viral links |
| Video streaming | large-object, geo-index | Bytes bypass your application; served near users |

Two consequences worth noticing:

**Ride hailing and food delivery differ in latency tolerance, not in structure.** If you can
design one, you can design the other by changing one non-functional requirement.

**Instagram is Twitter plus large objects.** The feed problem is identical; the images are a
separate, well-understood problem bolted on.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

Each primitive has a recognisable **signature** — the question that reveals it — and a small set
of approaches with known costs.

### id-generation

*Signature:* something needs a unique identifier, and coordinating on every one is too expensive.

Approaches: a counter allocated in blocks per instance; random with a uniqueness constraint; a
hash of content; a composite of timestamp, machine id and sequence. Trade-offs are between
coordination cost, guessability, and whether identifiers must be ordered.

### fanout

*Signature:* one write must become visible to many readers, and assembling at read time is too
expensive.

Approaches: <C id="fanout-on-write" /> precomputes; <C id="fanout-on-read" /> assembles on
demand; hybrid does both. The distribution of follower counts decides, and it is always
heavy-tailed, so the answer is always some hybrid.

### hot-key

*Signature:* one item receives more traffic than the rest combined.

Approaches: replicate toward readers (edge cache, local cache) for hot reads;
<C id="key-splitting" /> for hot writes. Never partitioning — a key cannot be divided.

### exactly-once-effect

*Signature:* a retry must not double-apply, and the network cannot tell you whether the first
attempt succeeded.

Approaches: caller-supplied idempotency key recorded atomically with the effect; reconciliation
for attempts with unknown outcomes. Never "make the network reliable".

### inventory-contention

*Signature:* a limited resource with concurrent claimants, where overselling costs money.

Approaches: conditional decrement with the condition inside the write; hold-then-confirm with
expiry; split the pool into buckets when contention exceeds one row's throughput.

### large-object

*Signature:* the payload is too big to sit in a database row or pass through your application.

Approaches: object storage with presigned upload URLs and CDN delivery. The rule is that **bytes
never pass through your application**; the database stores metadata and a key.

### search-index

*Signature:* text must be matched approximately and ranked by relevance.

Approaches: an inverted index maintained as derived data, updated asynchronously. Ranking is the
product; matching is the easy part. Expect a consistency window and a reindex procedure.

### geo-index

*Signature:* "find things near me", at scale.

Approaches: divide space into cells and index by cell id, so proximity becomes a key lookup over
a small set of neighbouring cells. The parameter is cell size, which trades false positives
against the number of cells to query.

### time-series-ingest

*Signature:* enormous volumes of timestamped data, queried mostly over recent ranges.

Approaches: append-optimised storage, rollups, retention tiers. The failure mode is always
**cardinality**, not volume.

### scheduling

*Signature:* do this later, exactly once, at scale, possibly at a specified time.

Approaches: a durable queue with visibility timeouts, or a table of due work claimed with a
conditional update. Idempotency is mandatory because at-least-once is what you get.

### stream-join

*Signature:* correlate events arriving from different sources, where one may be late.

Approaches: windowed joins with a watermark policy. The hard decision is how long to wait for
late data, which trades completeness against latency.

<Callout kind="info" title="How to use this in an interview">
When you receive an unfamiliar prompt, ask yourself which two or three of these it is. Then say
so out loud: &ldquo;This is fundamentally a fanout problem with a hot-key complication, and the
inventory piece is contention.&rdquo; That framing tells the interviewer you are reasoning from
structure rather than from recall, and it gives you a plan for the next thirty minutes.
</Callout>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Pattern-matching to a company instead of a primitive',
      symptom:

      cause:

        'A clean design that collapses when the interviewer mentions a celebrity account or a flash sale.',
      cause:

      mitigation:
        'For any fanout or partitioning problem, ask about the distribution before designing. Heavy tails are the norm, not the exception.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Solving a primitive that is not there',
      symptom:
        'A sharded database and a streaming platform for a workload one machine would serve.',
      cause:
        'Reaching for machinery associated with a category rather than with a constraint that has appeared.',
      mitigation:
        'Estimate first. If the numbers do not force a primitive, it is not in this problem.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Treating the domain as the difficulty',

        'Twenty minutes on the business rules of ride matching and none on the geospatial index.',

        'Ask "what makes this hard?" explicitly, and answer in terms of load, distribution and failure rather than in terms of features.',

    'Learn the underlying distributed systems theory and derive everything',

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="ddia">
The recurring problems in data-intensive systems — partitioning, replication, fanout,
deduplication, derived data — appear across products with very different domains, which is why a
treatment organised by problem transfers better than one organised by product.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="hello-interview">
Interview preparation material converges on a small set of recurring problem shapes rather than
on an unbounded list of questions, and interview guidance consistently emphasises adaptability
over recall.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation">
For each primitive, work one concrete example end to end, including the estimate and the failure
walk. A primitive you have only read about will not survive an interviewer asking "why?" three
times.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>
