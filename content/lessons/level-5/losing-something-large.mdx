## The problem

An executive asks: &ldquo;are we multi-region?&rdquo;

It sounds like a question about seriousness. It is actually four questions with different
answers and very different costs:

- Can we survive one machine failing? (Yes, trivially.)
- Can we survive one data centre failing? (Usually yes, and cheaply.)
- Can we survive an entire region failing? (Expensive, and it protects against something rare.)
- Can we survive **ourselves** — a bad deploy, a bad migration, a mistaken deletion? (This is the
  common case, and multi-region does not help at all.)

The fourth is the one that actually happens, and it is the one the question never means.

## Intuition

Redundancy only works across **independent** failures. Two machines in one rack share a power
supply and a top-of-rack switch, so they fail together. Two availability zones share a region's
network fabric and control plane. Two regions share your deployment pipeline, your
configuration, and your code.

That last one matters more than it sounds:

**A bad deploy replicates to every region in seconds.** So does a bad configuration change, a
poisoned cache entry, a schema migration that corrupts data, and an accidental deletion. These
are the most common causes of serious outages, and geographic redundancy is completely
transparent to all of them.

So the honest framing of multi-region is:

- It protects against a **rare** class: an entire region becoming unavailable.
- It provides no protection against the **common** class: change.
- It **adds** failure modes: cross-region consistency, replication lag, split brain, and a
  failover path that is exercised rarely and therefore usually broken.

That does not make it wrong. It makes it a specific purchase, and the question is whether the
budget would buy more availability spent on progressive rollout, fast rollback, and tested
backups.

## Concretely

<Example title="Four failure scales, four different answers">

| Failure | Protection | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| One instance | Run several behind a load balancer | Trivial |
| One availability zone | Spread instances and replicas across zones in the region | Small; a few ms of latency and some cross-zone data transfer |
| One region | A second region, warm or active, with data replicated | Roughly double infrastructure, plus cross-region consistency design |
| A bad deploy or deletion | Progressive rollout, fast rollback, tested backups, retention | Engineering discipline; almost no infrastructure |

The pattern almost everyone should follow: **exhaust multi-AZ and change safety before
considering multi-region.** Multi-AZ is cheap and protects against a genuine and reasonably
frequent failure. Change safety protects against the most common cause of outages. Multi-region
is the expensive option that protects against the rarest one.

<Claim label="recommendation">
If your reliability budget is limited, spend it in this order: multi-AZ redundancy, progressive
rollout with fast rollback, tested restores, then multi-region. The first three are cheaper and
address more of the outage distribution.
</Claim>

</Example>

## Formal explanation

Two numbers define a disaster recovery plan, and both are business decisions:

- **<C id="rpo" />** — how much data you accept losing, measured in time. Synchronous
  replication gives zero; asynchronous gives whatever the lag was at the moment of failure.
- **<C id="rto" />** — how long you accept being down, measured in time. Determined by
  detection, decision, and the failover mechanism.

Both must be stated before choosing an architecture, because they determine it. An RPO of zero
across regions requires synchronous cross-region replication, which puts a cross-region round
trip on every commit — a decision with a visible latency cost every day.

Two multi-region shapes:

**<C id="active-passive" />.** One region serves; another is warm. Simpler consistency — there
is one writer — and the failover path is rarely exercised, so it is usually the part that fails.
RTO is minutes to hours.

**<C id="active-active" />.** Both regions serve. Best latency and utilisation, near-zero RTO,
and now two regions accept writes concurrently, so conflict resolution is a product decision
rather than a technical detail.

<C id="cell-architecture">Cell-based architecture</C> is the underrated middle. Instead of two
large regions, run many independent cells, each serving a subset of users, each a complete
stack. A failure — including a bad deploy, if deployment is per-cell — affects one cell.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Replication is not backup, and backup is not restore">
Replication copies your DELETE faithfully to every region. Backups protect against logical
corruption, which replication accelerates. And a backup you have never restored is a belief:
you do not know how long it takes, whether it is complete, or whether the procedure works.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Blast radius is the more useful frame">

&ldquo;Multi-region?&rdquo; is a binary question about geography.
&ldquo;<C id="blast-radius">What is our blast radius?</C>&rdquo; is a question about how much a
single failure — of any kind — can take with it.

Reframed that way, the useful moves are broader:

- **Deploy progressively.** One cell, then one zone, then one region, with automated checks
  between. Blast radius of a bad deploy becomes one cell rather than everything.
- **Partition the fleet into cells.** Each cell serves a subset of users with its own full
  stack. A poisoned cache, a hot tenant or a bad config affects one cell.
- **Isolate the control plane from the data plane.** The data plane must keep serving when the
  control plane is unavailable, because control planes are where configuration errors land.
- **Shuffle-shard tenant assignment.** Assign each tenant to a random subset of cells so that no
  two tenants share exactly the same set. One abusive tenant then degrades a small, overlapping
  fraction rather than a fixed group.

Every one of these reduces the size of a failure without requiring a second region, and each
also helps against the change-related failures that multi-region does not touch.

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Failover path never exercised',
      symptom:
        'The region fails, failover is attempted, and it does not work: stale configuration, missing capacity, expired credentials, a dependency that only exists in the primary region.',
      cause:
        'The standby has never taken real traffic, so nothing has ever verified it can.',
      mitigation:
        'Fail over on a schedule, in production, during business hours. If that sounds too risky, that is the finding — the plan does not work and you have not discovered it yet.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Bad deploy reaching every region',
      symptom:
        'A global outage minutes after a release. Every region affected identically.',
      cause:
        'The deployment pipeline is a shared failure domain that spans all geographic redundancy.',
      mitigation:
        'Progressive rollout with automated checks between stages, and per-cell or per-region deployment with a bake period. This is where most of the availability actually comes from.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Cross-region write conflicts',
      symptom:
        'Data silently diverges. Users see different values depending on where they are routed.',
      cause:
        'Active-active accepts concurrent writes to the same data in two places, and the conflict resolution rule was never chosen deliberately.',
      mitigation:
        'Route writes for a given entity to one region, or use data types whose merge is defined, or accept and surface conflicts explicitly. Do not let last-write-wins be the accidental default.',
    },

      what: 'Untested backups',
      symptom:
        'A restore is needed and takes far longer than assumed, or the backup is incomplete, or the procedure references systems that no longer exist.',
      cause:
        'Backups were configured and never restored.',
      mitigation:
        'Restore on a schedule and time it. The measured restore duration is your real RTO for data loss scenarios, and it is usually much longer than the plan claims.',

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-book">
Reliability practice emphasises that changes to production are a leading cause of incidents, and
that progressive rollout with automated verification between stages is among the highest-leverage
reliability investments available.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-well-architected">
Architecture review frameworks treat recovery objectives as design inputs: the RPO and RTO
determine whether multi-AZ, warm standby or active-active is appropriate, and they emphasise
regularly testing the recovery procedure rather than documenting it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="spanner">
Providing strongly consistent transactions across geographically distributed replicas requires
bounded clock uncertainty and commit wait, which imposes latency proportional to that
uncertainty on every commit. Global strong consistency has an inherent, ongoing cost.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="gcp-arch">
Rehearse failover on a schedule rather than relying on documentation. A recovery procedure that
has not been executed recently should be assumed not to work.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We are multi-region, so we are highly available.',
      why: 'It addresses one failure class. Deploys, configuration changes, data corruption and dependency failures all propagate across regions, and those account for most serious incidents.',
      instead:
        'State which failure class each investment covers. Multi-region covers regional loss; progressive rollout covers change; tested backups cover corruption. They are not substitutes.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We have backups.',
      why: 'Having backups and being able to restore are different claims. Untested restores commonly turn out to be incomplete, far slower than assumed, or dependent on a procedure that no longer works.',
      instead:
        'Restore on a schedule and record how long it took. That measured number is your real RTO for data loss.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Active-active gives us zero downtime.',
      why: 'It gives near-zero RTO for a regional failure and introduces concurrent writes to the same data in two places. Conflicts become normal, and if the resolution rule was not chosen deliberately, data is being silently discarded.',
      instead:
        'Say how writes are routed and how conflicts are resolved. If the answer is last-write-wins by timestamp, say what that costs, because it is silent data loss.',
    },
  ]}
