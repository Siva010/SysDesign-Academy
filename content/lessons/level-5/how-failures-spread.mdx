## The problem

One dependency — a recommendation service, entirely optional — gets slow. Not down. Slow: p99
goes from 30 ms to 4 seconds.

Twenty minutes later the whole product is down. Not the recommendations. Everything. Checkout,
login, the home page.

The recommendation service never failed. It got slow, and the system's response to slowness did
the rest.

This is the most important failure pattern in production systems, and almost none of it is
visible in a design diagram.

## Intuition

Follow the chain, one step at a time.

1. The dependency slows to 4 seconds.
2. Your service calls it with a 10-second timeout, so each request now occupies a worker for 4
   seconds instead of 30 ms.
3. By Little's law, concurrency is arrival rate times service time. Service time went up 130x,
   so required concurrency went up 130x. Your worker pool did not.
4. Workers are all busy waiting. Requests that do not need recommendations at all queue behind
   them, because they need a worker too.
5. Your service is now slow for every endpoint. Its callers time out and **retry**.
6. Retries triple the arrival rate at a service that is already saturated.
7. Health checks time out. The load balancer removes instances, concentrating the same traffic
   on fewer machines.
8. The remaining instances fail faster.

Notice that steps 5, 6 and 7 are all things the system did **in response to the problem**. Every
one of them made it worse.

**The initial failure was small and bounded. The response was unbounded.**

## Concretely

<Example title="Retry amplification, in numbers">

Three layers, each retrying three times on failure.

- The browser retries: 3 attempts.
- The API gateway retries: 3 attempts per browser attempt.
- The service retries its dependency: 3 attempts per gateway attempt.

One user action becomes up to 27 requests at the bottom of the stack.

Under normal conditions nothing retries, so the amplification is invisible. It appears only when
the bottom layer starts failing — which is precisely when it can least absorb 27 times the
load.

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-jitter">
Retries without jitter cause clients to synchronise, so retry attempts arrive in waves rather
than spread out. Adding randomised delay spreads the load and materially improves recovery.
</Claim>

The second-order effect is worse than the arithmetic suggests: a service under 27x load fails
more requests, which triggers more retries, which is a feedback loop. Systems in this state
often cannot recover even after the original cause is fixed, because the retry load alone is now
enough to keep them down. Recovery requires shedding load or stopping traffic entirely.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="cascading-failure" /> is failure that spreads because the response to a local problem
consumes resources elsewhere. Four mechanisms do most of the work:

**Resource exhaustion through waiting.** A slow dependency does not consume CPU; it consumes
*concurrency*. Workers, connections and memory are held by requests that are doing nothing but
waiting. This is why the classic signature is high latency with low CPU.

**<C id="retry-amplification" />.** Retries multiply load exactly when capacity is lowest. Each
layer that retries independently multiplies the layer below.

**<C id="thundering-herd" />.** Synchronised behaviour. Clients that all failed at the same
moment all retry at the same moment. Caches that were all populated together all expire
together. A restarted service is hit by every client's reconnection at once.

**Load-shedding-by-accident.** Health checks remove instances, so the same traffic lands on
fewer machines, so those fail their health checks too. The mechanism intended to route around
failure manufactures it instead.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Throughput can look fine while goodput is zero">
A saturated service can be completing thousands of requests per second while every caller has
already timed out and stopped waiting. <C id="throughput-vs-goodput">Goodput</C> — work that
somebody still wants by the time it is finished — is the number that matters, and a throughput
graph will not show you that it has collapsed.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Why timeouts must come from the budget, not the dependency">

The instinct is to set a timeout from observed dependency latency: &ldquo;it usually responds in
30 ms, so 10 seconds is generous&rdquo;.

That reasoning is backwards. The right question is: **how long is the caller willing to wait?**

If the user-facing request has a 2-second budget and this call is one of four in sequence, this
call gets a few hundred milliseconds. A 10-second timeout on a call inside a 2-second budget is
not generous — it is a resource leak, because the caller has already gone and your worker is
still held.

The discipline:

1. Start from the user-facing latency objective.
2. Subtract what you need for your own work.
3. Divide the rest among sequential calls.
4. Propagate a **deadline**, not a timeout, so each hop knows how much of the budget remains
   rather than restarting the clock.

Deadline propagation is the version that composes. With plain timeouts, four hops each allowing
one second means the last hop can still be working three seconds after the client gave up.

</Deeper>

      cause:
        'Retries at multiple layers multiply load; the load itself is now enough to keep the service down.',
      mitigation:
        'Retry budgets capping retries as a fraction of traffic, exponential backoff with jitter, retry at one layer only, and circuit breakers to stop calling a dependency that is clearly broken.',

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Propagate a deadline through the call chain instead of setting independent timeouts"
  options={[
    'No timeouts: wait indefinitely',
    'Independent per-call timeouts chosen from observed dependency latency',
    'A deadline set at the edge and propagated, each hop passing the remainder',
  ]}
  why="With independent timeouts, a chain of four hops each allowing one second can still be working three seconds after the client gave up. Every second of that is a worker held for a result nobody will read."
  gain="Work stops when it stops being useful, so goodput is protected. Concurrency requirements stay bounded under dependency slowness, and the whole chain fails fast together rather than in confusing stages."
  sacrifice="Every service and client library must understand and propagate the deadline, which is a cross-cutting change. Requests that would have succeeded slightly late are now cancelled."
  failureModes={[
    'A service that ignores the deadline and restarts the clock, silently breaking the chain',
    'Deadlines set too tight, cancelling work that would have succeeded',
    'Cancellation not actually releasing resources, so the benefit is theoretical',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate and cross-cutting. It requires framework support and a convention every service follows. Retrofitting it into an existing estate is a real project."
  cost="No infrastructure cost. Engineering cost is in the plumbing and in the discipline to keep it correct as new services are added."
  whenNot="For genuinely asynchronous background work with no waiting caller, a deadline is meaningless — the work should complete whenever it can. Deadlines belong on the synchronous request path, where somebody is waiting."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="sre-book">
Google's SRE writing documents cascading failure as a distinct pattern in which a system's
response to overload — retries, failover, and reduced capacity from health checking — amplifies
the original problem. Recovery frequently requires reducing load rather than adding capacity.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-builders-library">
Timeouts derived from latency budgets, retry budgets that cap retries as a proportion of
traffic, and load shedding during overload are standard practice for services operating at
scale. The common thread is bounding the system's own response to failure.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-jitter">
Adding randomised jitter to backoff spreads retry attempts over time rather than allowing
clients to synchronise into waves, and measurably improves recovery under contention.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="release-it">
Stability patterns — timeouts, circuit breakers, bulkheads — exist specifically to bound the
propagation of failure between components. Their common purpose is to make one component's
failure survivable by its callers.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We retry failed requests to improve reliability.',
      why: 'Retries improve reliability for transient, isolated failures and multiply load during correlated ones. They are most dangerous precisely when they are most tempting.',
      instead:
        'Retry with backoff and jitter, cap retries as a fraction of traffic, retry at one layer only, and stop retrying entirely when a circuit breaker indicates the dependency is broken rather than flaky.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We set a generous timeout so we do not fail requests unnecessarily.',
      why: 'A timeout longer than the caller will wait is not generous — it holds a worker for a result nobody will read. Generous timeouts are how a slow dependency consumes a service.',
      instead:
        'Derive timeouts from the user-facing latency budget and propagate a deadline so the whole chain gives up together.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Add more capacity when the service is struggling.',
      why: 'During a cascading failure, more capacity often means more concurrent load on the saturated dependency, which prolongs the failure. Recovery usually requires less load, not more capacity.',
      instead:
        'Shed load first to restore goodput, then restore capacity gradually. Adding capacity into an active cascade frequently extends it.',
    },
  ]}
