## The problem

&ldquo;It needs to be more reliable.&rdquo;

Three engineers hear that and start three different projects. One adds a second region. One turns
on synchronous replication. One adds retries everywhere.

Six weeks later the system fails again, in a way that none of the three changes addressed, and the
review concludes that reliability is hard.

It is not that the work was bad. It is that the sentence did not contain an instruction. Six
different properties travel under that vocabulary, they are achieved by different mechanisms, and
at least three pairs of them are in direct opposition — so a team told to improve &ldquo;it&rdquo;
can improve one while damaging another and honestly report success.

## Intuition

Imagine a library.

**Can you get in right now?** Doors open, staff present. That is availability, and it says nothing
about whether the book you want is on the shelf.

**If you deposit a manuscript, will it still exist in fifty years?** Fireproof storage, several
copies in several buildings. That is durability, and a vault that is only open on Tuesdays is
extremely durable and mostly unavailable.

**Does the catalogue tell the truth about where books are?** That is correctness, and a library can
be open every day and consistently direct you to the wrong shelf.

**Does it keep working when a librarian is ill, or the heating fails?** That is fault tolerance —
and only with respect to the faults you planned for. Illness, yes. Flood, perhaps not.

**Can it serve ten times as many readers without ten times the building?** That is scalability, and
a small library that is perfect for its town is not therefore unscalable — it has simply never been
asked.

**Does it consistently do all of the above?** That is reliability, which is not a sixth mechanism
but a summary of the other five holding over time.

The reason this matters is that these are not layers of one quality. **Some are bought by giving up
others**, and if you cannot name which one you are being asked for, you cannot know what you are
allowed to trade.

## Concretely

<Example title="Four systems, each strong in one dimension and weak in another">

**A backup archive.** Data written to it survives fires, decades and neglect. Reading it takes
hours and it is unreachable during maintenance windows. Extremely durable, poorly available. Nobody
considers that a defect — it is the design.

**A cache.** Answers in under a millisecond, replicated across a fleet, essentially always
reachable. Every value in it is a copy that can vanish without warning. Highly available, not
durable at all, and this is also fine, because nothing is stored there that exists only there.

**A payment ledger.** Refuses the transaction when it cannot confirm the balance. Correct
absolutely, available conditionally — and the refusal is a feature. A ledger that guessed in order
to stay available would be worse in every way that matters.

**A social feed.** Serves something instantly, always. What it serves may be stale, out of order,
or missing a post from thirty seconds ago. Highly available, deliberately not strictly correct, and
users are entirely satisfied.

Every one of these is well designed. Each chose which property to hold and which to release, and
none of them could have been improved by being told to be &ldquo;more reliable&rdquo;.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

**<C id="availability" />** — is it answering now? Measured as the fraction of requests served
successfully over a window, and the window matters: 99.9% over a year and 99.9% over each hour are
very different promises. Achieved by redundancy and failover. It makes no claim about the answer
being right.

**<C id="durability" />** — once a write is acknowledged, does it survive? Achieved by writing to
stable storage, replicating to independent failure domains, and keeping backups. Its measurement is
usually a probability of loss per year, and its real test is **restore**, not backup. An untested
restore is a hypothesis.

**<C id="correctness" />** — does the system produce the answer its specification requires?
Independent of everything above. A system can be available five nines of the time and consistently
wrong, and its dashboards will be green throughout, because availability instrumentation counts
responses rather than truths.

**<C id="fault-tolerance" />** — does it keep working in the presence of faults? The load-bearing
word is **which**. Tolerance is always relative to an enumerated set: one machine, one zone, one
disk, one slow dependency. A claim of fault tolerance without a fault list is not a property, it is
a mood. The faults not on the list are the ones that cause the outage — a correlated failure across
what were assumed to be independent domains, or a configuration error that reaches every replica
simultaneously.

**<C id="scalability" />** — does cost grow acceptably as load grows? It is a **derivative, not a
level**. A system handling a million requests per second is not thereby scalable; a system handling
a hundred whose cost doubles when load doubles is. The interesting failures are super-linear: an
algorithm that becomes quadratic, a coordination step whose cost grows with the number of nodes, a
join whose cost grows with the product of two growing tables.

**<C id="reliability" />** — does it do the right thing consistently over time? This is the
umbrella: correctness, availability and acceptable performance, held together, under real
conditions. That is exactly why &ldquo;make it more reliable&rdquo; is not an instruction, and why
the useful practice is to express it as measurable objectives on specific properties
(<Cite id="sre-book" />).

And the part that makes this a lesson rather than a glossary — **the tensions**:

- **Durability against availability.** Synchronous replication means a write is not acknowledged
  until a second copy exists. That is stronger durability and *lower* availability, because the
  write now fails when the replica is unreachable. Choosing asynchronous replication reverses both.
- **Correctness against availability.** During a partition you may answer with possibly-stale data
  or refuse to answer. There is no third option (<Cite id="gilbert-lynch" />).
- **Scalability against correctness.** Partitioning data across machines is how throughput grows,
  and it is also what makes a transaction spanning two partitions expensive or unavailable.
- **Fault tolerance against cost, and against simplicity.** Every tolerated fault is a mechanism,
  and every mechanism is a thing that can itself fail — a failover that triggers spuriously is a
  new outage source added in the name of reliability.

<Callout kind="info" title="Turning a request into a requirement">
When someone asks for reliability, four questions convert it into something you can build:

1. **Which property?** Availability, durability, correctness, or a latency target.
2. **Measured how, and from where?** Server-side success rate and user-perceived success rate
   routinely disagree, and only one of them is the product.
3. **Over what window, and for whom?** Monthly aggregate hides an hour of total failure. A number
   that holds overall may not hold for the largest customer.
4. **What may be given up to get it?** If the answer is nothing, the requirement is not yet real,
   because every one of these is bought with something.
</Callout>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {

    },
    {

      mitigation: 'Write the fault list down and include correlated faults: bad config, bad deploy, expired certificate, poisoned cache. Then test that the list is complete by causing them.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Durability never verified',
      symptom: 'A restore is attempted during an incident and the backups are unusable or incomplete.',
      cause: 'Backup success was monitored; restore was never exercised.',
      mitigation: 'Restore on a schedule, into a real environment, and measure how long it takes. The recovery time you have not measured is the one you do not have.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Scales up but not down',
      symptom: 'Cost grows with peak load and never recedes, so an unusual day permanently raises the bill.',
      cause: 'Scalability was treated as a capability to add capacity rather than as a cost curve in both directions.',
      mitigation: 'State scalability as cost per unit of load, and check it at a tenth of peak as well as at ten times.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Availability measured in the wrong place',
      symptom: 'Internal availability reads 99.99% while users report frequent failures.',
      cause: 'Measurement happens behind the layer that is failing — a load balancer, a DNS record, a mobile network path — so failed requests are never counted.',
      mitigation: 'Measure as close to the user as possible, and treat a request that never reached you as a failure rather than as absent data.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Redundancy that is not independent',
      symptom: 'Two replicas fail simultaneously, far more often than the multiplied probabilities suggest.',
      cause: 'They shared a rack, a power supply, a network path, a certificate, or a deployment.',
      mitigation: 'Enumerate what the copies share. Independence is the load-bearing assumption in every availability calculation, and it is almost always weaker than assumed.',
    },

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Acknowledge a write only after it exists on a second machine"
  options={[
    'Acknowledge at the primary; replicate asynchronously',
    'Acknowledge after one replica confirms',
    'Acknowledge after a quorum of replicas confirms',
    'Acknowledge at the primary, and reconcile losses from an upstream log',
  ]}
  why="Acknowledging before a second copy exists means the window between acknowledgement and replication is a window of silent data loss. Whether that matters is a product question, and it is answered by asking what one lost minute of writes costs."
  gain="Data survives the loss of the machine that accepted it. The recovery point objective becomes zero for acknowledged writes rather than an unknown quantity nobody has measured."
  sacrifice="Write latency includes a network round trip to the replica. Write availability falls, because the write now requires two machines rather than one, and it fails in strictly more circumstances than before."
  failureModes={[
    'Writes failing whenever a replica is briefly unreachable, which is more often than the replica is down',
    'Latency spikes propagating from the replica into every write',
    'Silent fallback to asynchronous mode under pressure, giving up the guarantee exactly when it was needed',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate. Replica health becomes part of the write path, so replica maintenance is now user-visible and must be scheduled and drained rather than simply performed."
  cost="Extra machines and cross-zone traffic, plus the latency on every write forever. The saving is entirely on the day you lose a machine."
  whenNot="When the data can be reconstructed from somewhere else — a derived view, a cache, a projection of an upstream log — durability at write time buys nothing you do not already have. When write availability matters more than the last few seconds of data, as for telemetry or analytics events, asynchronous is correct. And never adopt it as a default across every store: the right answer differs per dataset, and applying one policy everywhere means paying ledger-grade durability for metrics and accepting metrics-grade durability for ledgers."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-book">
Reliability is expressed as measurable service level objectives on specific indicators rather than
as a general aspiration, precisely so that trade-offs can be made explicitly and so that teams know
when they have enough.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-workbook">
Service level indicators should be measured as close to the user as practical, because measurement
taken inside the system misses failures in the layers between the system and the user.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="gilbert-lynch">
In the presence of a network partition, a system must choose between remaining available and
maintaining linearizable consistency. It cannot provide both, so the choice is between two named
properties rather than a matter of engineering quality.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Synchronous replication guarantees that an acknowledged write exists on a follower, at the cost of
the write failing when that follower is unavailable. Asynchronous replication reverses both
properties, and the difference is a durability decision rather than a performance tuning option.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="aws-well-architected">
Recovery objectives should be stated as explicit targets for how much data may be lost and how long
recovery may take, and recovery procedures should be tested rather than assumed to work.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="release-it">
Mechanisms added to improve stability are themselves failure sources, so each one must be justified
against a specific fault rather than added because it is generally associated with robustness.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

<InterviewLens
  tests="Whether you can say which property a requirement is about, and what you would trade for it. Candidates who use these words interchangeably cannot make a trade-off, because they cannot see two sides."
  depth="Know that availability and durability are opposed at the acknowledgement point. Know that fault tolerance requires an enumerated fault list. Be able to say how you would measure the property you are claiming."

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'Using reliability as a requirement.',
      why: 'It names an umbrella rather than a property, so different engineers optimise different things and some of those things oppose each other. Success is unmeasurable and the work is unbounded.',
      instead:
        'Name the property, the measurement point, the window and the target. "99.9% of checkout requests succeed within 2 seconds, measured at the client, per hour" is buildable and testable.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Claiming fault tolerance without listing the faults.',
      why: 'Tolerance is always relative to something. Systems that survive machine failure routinely fail on a bad configuration applied to every machine at once, and that fault was never on anyone list.',
      instead:
        'Write the list, include correlated faults such as deploys and configuration, and test the ones you claim. A fault you have not caused deliberately is a fault you do not know you tolerate.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Treating backups as durability.',
      why: 'A backup you have never restored is a hypothesis. Backup jobs succeed while producing unusable output — incomplete, unreadable, missing the one table nobody thought to include.',
      instead:
        'Restore on a schedule into a real environment and time it. The number you get is your actual recovery time objective, and it is usually a surprise the first time.',
    },
  ]}
