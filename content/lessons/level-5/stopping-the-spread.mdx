## The problem

You know how a slow dependency becomes an outage. Now you have to stop it, and the available
defences all look like overhead on a normal day.

A circuit breaker rejects requests that might have succeeded. Load shedding returns errors to
users you could have served. Bulkheads reserve capacity that sits idle. Every one of them costs
something when nothing is wrong, and pays only during the incident you have not had yet.

That is the actual difficulty: **these are insurance policies, and they are easy to argue away
in a planning meeting.**

## Intuition

Every defence in this lesson is the same idea in a different place: **put a bound on it.**

- A **timeout** bounds how long one call may consume a worker.
- A **retry budget** bounds how much extra load your retries may generate.
- A **circuit breaker** bounds how long you keep calling something that is clearly broken.
- A **bulkhead** bounds how much of a shared resource one dependency may hold.
- **Load shedding** bounds how much work you accept.
- **Graceful degradation** bounds how much of the product one failure can take with it.

The unifying principle: an unbounded response to failure is what turns a local problem into a
global one. Every bound you add converts an unpredictable failure into a predictable, partial
one.

The second insight is about what you are optimising. During overload the goal is **not** to
serve every request. It is to serve as many as possible *correctly*. A system that accepts
everything and times out on all of it has 100% acceptance and zero goodput. A system that
rejects 40% and serves 60% correctly is more available in every sense a user cares about.

## Concretely

<Example title="Choosing defences by what they bound">

One service, four dependencies, four different treatments:

| Dependency | Nature | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| Auth service | Required; nothing works without it | Tight timeout, retries with jitter, no breaker — failing fast here means failing the request anyway |
| Primary database | Required | Bounded connection pool with an acquisition timeout, so a slow database queues visibly rather than consuming everything |
| Recommendations | Optional | Circuit breaker plus a short timeout, degrading to a default list. One failure must not touch other endpoints |
| Analytics events | Fire and forget | Bounded queue that drops on overflow. Never block a user request for telemetry |

The pattern: **the defence follows from what the dependency is for.** A required dependency
cannot be degraded away, so bound its latency and fail fast. An optional one should be behind a
breaker with a fallback. Telemetry should never be able to affect a user request at all.

A common mistake is applying the same policy everywhere — retries and a breaker on every call —
which produces both unnecessary complexity and, on the required path, a breaker that trips and
turns a slow login into no login at all.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

**<C id="timeout" />** bounds how long a call may hold resources. Derived from the latency
budget, not from observed dependency latency. Propagated as a deadline so the chain gives up
together.

**<C id="exponential-backoff">Backoff with jitter</C>** bounds retry rate and desynchronises
clients. Without jitter, clients that failed together retry together, producing waves.

**<C id="retry-budget" />** bounds total retry load — for example, retries may be at most 10% of
requests. This is stronger than per-request retry limits, because it holds regardless of how many
requests are failing. Under a broad failure, per-request limits still triple the load; a budget
does not.

**<C id="circuit-breaker" />** bounds how long you keep calling something broken. After a
failure threshold it opens and fails immediately, then periodically allows a probe. It converts
slow failures into fast ones, which frees the resources that would otherwise be consumed waiting.

**<C id="bulkhead" />** bounds how much of a shared resource one dependency can consume:
separate connection pools, thread pools, or instances per dependency or per tenant. The name
comes from ship compartments — one flooded compartment does not sink the vessel.

**<C id="load-shedding" />** bounds accepted work. Under overload, reject quickly and cheaply so
the work you do accept completes. Rejection must be far cheaper than service, or shedding
consumes the capacity it was meant to protect.

**<C id="admission-control" />** is shedding with priority: decide *which* requests to reject.
Health checks and paying customers before batch and free tiers; writes before analytics.

<Callout kind="info" title="Circuit breakers on required dependencies need care">
If a dependency is required, an open breaker turns "slow" into "definitely failed". Sometimes
correct — failing fast frees resources and lets callers degrade. Sometimes worse — a breaker
tripping on a transient blip takes down login entirely. Breakers are most clearly right for
optional dependencies with a fallback.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Shedding correctly: reject early, reject cheaply, reject informatively">

Load shedding only works if rejection is much cheaper than service. Three requirements:

**Reject early.** As close to the entry point as possible, before authentication lookups,
before database connections are acquired. A rejection that first does 80% of the work has saved
almost nothing.

**Reject cheaply.** No logging every rejection at full detail, no expensive error construction.
Under overload you may be rejecting tens of thousands per second.

**Reject informatively.** Return a status the client understands, with a retry-after hint.
A rejection that causes immediate retries has made things worse.

The signal to shed on matters too. CPU is a lagging indicator. **Queue wait time is a leading
one**: if a request has already waited longer than the client's remaining deadline, serving it
is wasted work regardless of how much capacity you have.

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-load-shedding">
Using queue depth and wait time as overload signals, and shedding at the entry point to protect
goodput, is established practice. The objective during overload is maximising completed useful
work rather than accepted requests.
</Claim>

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Circuit breaker on a required dependency',
      symptom:
        'A brief blip in the auth service produces a complete login outage lasting the breaker cool-down.',
      cause:
        'The breaker opened on a transient failure, and there is no fallback for a required call, so every request fails immediately.',
      mitigation:
        'Reserve breakers for calls with a fallback. On required paths, use tight timeouts and jittered retries within a budget, and tune thresholds so brief blips do not trip.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Shedding too late in the request',
      symptom:
        'The service sheds heavily and still falls over, with CPU pinned by rejections.',
      cause:
        'Rejection happens after authentication, parsing and a database lookup, so a rejected request costs nearly as much as a served one.',
      mitigation:
        'Move admission control to the entry point, before any expensive work. Make the rejection path deliberately cheap.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Bulkheads sized without measurement',
      symptom:
        'Pools are exhausted at normal load, or so generous that isolation is theoretical.',
      cause:
        'Sizes chosen by intuition rather than from measured concurrency.',
      mitigation:
        "Size each pool from Little's law using measured arrival rate and service time, with headroom. Monitor utilisation and wait time per pool.",
    },
    {
      what: 'Defences never exercised',
      symptom:
        'During a real incident, the breaker does not trip, the fallback throws, or shedding rejects the wrong traffic.',
      cause:
        'The code paths only run during failures, and failures are rare, so the code is effectively untested.',
      mitigation:
        'Exercise them deliberately: fault injection, load tests that trigger shedding, and periodic drills that open breakers in production during business hours.',
    },
  ]}

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Shed load at the entry point during overload rather than accepting everything"
  options={[
    'Accept everything and let queues absorb it',
    'Shed uniformly above a concurrency or wait-time threshold',
    'Admission control by priority: shed low-value traffic first',
  ]}
  why="Accepting more work than you can complete converts an overload into universal failure. Every accepted request consumes memory and a worker whether or not anyone is still waiting for it, so acceptance without capacity destroys goodput for everyone."
  gain="Accepted requests complete within their latency objective. Goodput stays near capacity instead of collapsing. Failures are fast and explicit, so clients can back off or degrade rather than hanging."
  sacrifice="Some users receive errors who would have been served. During a brief spike, a slightly tight threshold produces visible failures where a queue would have absorbed it."
  failureModes={[
    'Rejection that is not cheap enough, so shedding consumes the capacity it protects',
    'Clients that retry immediately on rejection, converting shedding into amplification',
    'Priority rules that shed the wrong traffic — health checks, or the highest-value customers',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate. It needs a good overload signal, a cheap rejection path, and priority rules that are reviewed as the product changes. It also needs testing, because the code runs only under conditions you rarely have."
  cost="No infrastructure cost. The real cost is the conversation about which requests are worth rejecting, which is a product decision as much as a technical one."
  whenNot="For batch or asynchronous work where latency does not matter and completion does, a deep queue is exactly right — absorbing bursts is the point, and shedding discards work for no benefit. Also be careful where a rejected request is not retryable: shedding a payment confirmation callback is not the same as shedding a page view."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-load-shedding">
Shedding load at the entry point using queue-based overload signals, with the objective of
maximising goodput rather than accepted requests, is established practice for services operating
at scale.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="release-it">
Circuit breakers, bulkheads and timeouts are documented stability patterns whose shared purpose
is bounding the propagation of failure between components.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="tail-at-scale">
Hedged requests — issuing a duplicate after a short delay and taking the first response —
reduce tail latency at the cost of additional load. They are appropriate for idempotent
operations where tail latency is the binding constraint.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="sre-book">
Prefer degrading to failing. A response with recommendations missing is better than no response.
Design fallbacks explicitly, and test them, because untested fallback code fails at exactly the
moment it is needed.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We put a circuit breaker on every dependency.',
      why: 'On a required dependency with no fallback, an open breaker converts slow into failed. Applied uniformly, breakers add configuration surface and can amplify a transient blip into a full outage.',
      instead:
        'Classify dependencies as required or optional. Breakers with fallbacks for optional; tight timeouts and budgeted retries for required.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We will add capacity when overloaded.',
      why: 'Capacity arrives in minutes and overload arrives in seconds. During a cascade, more capacity often means more concurrent pressure on the saturated dependency, which prolongs it.',
      instead:
        'Shed first to restore goodput, then add capacity. Autoscaling handles trends; shedding and headroom handle spikes.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We tested the happy path thoroughly.',
      why: 'The defences only execute during failure, so they are the least-tested code in the system and they run at the worst possible moment.',
      instead:
        'Inject faults deliberately. Trip breakers in production during business hours. Load test until shedding engages, and confirm the fallback returns something sensible.',
    },
  ]}
