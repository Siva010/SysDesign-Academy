## The problem

&ldquo;The system should be highly available.&rdquo;

Nobody disagrees, and nobody can act on it. Six months later two arguments are running in
parallel: one team says reliability is unacceptable and must be fixed before any new features;
another says the system has been fine and the work is a waste. Both are pointing at the same
dashboard.

They are arguing because there is no number. Without one, &ldquo;reliable enough&rdquo; is a
matter of temperament, and the loudest person wins.

## Intuition

Reliability is not a property you maximise. It is a budget you allocate.

Every additional nine costs more than the last, and the cost curve is steep:

- 99% — about 3.65 days of downtime a year. Achievable with one machine and some luck.
- 99.9% — about 8.8 hours. Needs redundancy, monitoring, and someone on call.
- 99.99% — about 53 minutes. Needs automated failover, tested regularly, and a strong deploy
  discipline.
- 99.999% — about 5 minutes. Needs multiple regions, deep automation, and an organisation
  structured around it.

Two consequences that people find uncomfortable:

**More reliability is not always better.** Going from three nines to four might cost several
engineers for a year. If users cannot tell the difference — because their own network is less
reliable than that — you have spent that year buying nothing.

**A target of 100% is a decision not to ship.** Every change risks reliability. If the target
admits no failure, the rational response is to stop changing anything, which is not what anyone
actually wants.

The <C id="error-budget" /> makes this explicit: **the allowed unreliability is a resource, and
shipping spends it.** When it is gone, you stop shipping and fix things. When it is plentiful,
you ship faster. It converts a values argument into a shared number.

## Concretely

<Example title="Building an SLO that means something">

**Bad:** &ldquo;The API should have 99.9% uptime.&rdquo;

Uptime measured how? By whom? Is a request that takes 30 seconds and succeeds &ldquo;up&rdquo;?
Is a health check returning 200 while every real request fails &ldquo;up&rdquo;?

**Better:** &ldquo;99.9% of `GET /orders` requests, measured at the load balancer, return a 2xx
or 4xx status within 300 ms, evaluated over a rolling 28 days.&rdquo;

That is actionable because it names five things:

1. **What is measured** — a specific endpoint, not the system in the abstract.
2. **Where** — at the load balancer, which is closer to the user than the application is.
3. **What counts as success** — including 4xx, because a client sending a malformed request is
   not the service failing.
4. **The latency threshold** — because a slow success is a failure from the user's side.
5. **The window** — a rolling 28 days, so one bad afternoon does not consume the year.

The budget follows: 0.1% of 28 days is roughly 40 minutes of failure. That is a number a team
can plan against, and a number a product manager can argue about.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

Three terms, frequently conflated:

- **<C id="sli" />** — a measurement of something a user feels. Success rate, latency at a
  percentile, freshness of data. It must be measurable from the user's side of the system.
- **<C id="slo" />** — the target for that indicator, chosen deliberately below 100%.
- **<C id="sla" />** — a contractual promise with consequences. Always looser than the internal
  SLO, so that you notice a problem before a customer can claim against it.

<C id="availability-math">Availability arithmetic</C> is unforgiving in one direction:

<Formula
  expression="A_series = A₁ × A₂ × … × Aₙ     A_redundant = 1 − (1 − A)ᵏ"
  where={[
    { symbol: 'series', meaning: 'every component a request must pass through, multiplied together' },
    { symbol: 'redundant', meaning: 'k independent copies; the failure probabilities multiply instead' },
    { symbol: 'independent', meaning: 'the load-bearing word. Two instances in one rack are one failure domain.' },
  ]}
/>

Five components at 99.9% in series give 0.999⁵ ≈ 99.5%, which is over 40 hours a year. **Every
synchronous dependency you add multiplies your availability by theirs.** That single fact is the
strongest argument available for removing things from the critical path.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Alert on symptoms and burn rate, not on causes">
An alert for every possible cause produces noise, and noise trains the on-call to ignore alerts.
Alert on what users experience — error rate, latency — and on how fast the error budget is
burning. A slow burn is a ticket; a fast burn is a page.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Burn rate: one alert instead of many">

The naive alert — &ldquo;error rate above 1%&rdquo; — is either too sensitive, firing for
brief blips, or too slow, only firing after significant budget is gone.

Burn-rate alerting reframes it: **how fast are we consuming the error budget relative to the
window?**

If the budget covers 28 days and you are consuming it at 14 times the sustainable rate, it will
be gone in two days. That is worth waking someone for. If you are consuming at 1.5 times the
rate, it is worth a ticket.

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-workbook">
Alerting on error budget burn rate, typically with multiple windows so that both fast and slow
burns are detected, is standard practice. It ties alert severity to user impact rather than to
an arbitrary threshold.
</Claim>

The practical benefit is that one well-constructed alert replaces a dozen threshold alerts, and
its severity is automatically proportional to how much trouble you are in.

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Measuring from inside the system',
      symptom:
        'Dashboards are green during an outage. The service reports 100% success because it is receiving no requests.',
      cause:
        'The SLI is computed from application metrics, which cannot see requests that never arrived.',
      mitigation:
        'Measure at the load balancer or from external probes. If a component can fail without affecting the metric, the metric does not measure availability.',
    },
    {
      what: 'An SLO nobody uses',
      symptom:
        'A number exists in a document. Prioritisation arguments continue exactly as before.',
      cause:
        'The SLO was written without agreement that it would change behaviour when breached.',
      mitigation:
        'Agree the consequence before you need it: exhausted budget means feature work pauses. An SLO with no consequence is documentation, not a control.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Alert fatigue',
      symptom:
        'Dozens of alerts nightly, most requiring no action. Real incidents are missed among them.',
      cause: 'Alerting on causes and on thresholds rather than on user-visible symptoms.',
      mitigation:
        'Every alert must be actionable and urgent. Move the rest to dashboards and tickets. Burn-rate alerting collapses many thresholds into one proportionate signal.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Availability target that arithmetic forbids',
      symptom:
        'A four-nines target on a service with eight synchronous dependencies at three nines each.',
      cause:
        'The target was set without multiplying the dependency chain.',
      mitigation:
        'Do the arithmetic. Then either remove dependencies from the critical path, make them asynchronous, degrade without them, or change the target.',
    },
  ]}

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Set an SLO below 100% and treat the remainder as a budget that feature work may spend"
  options={[
    'No formal target: rely on judgment and incident response',
    'A target with no consequence attached',
    'An SLO with an error budget that gates feature work when exhausted',
    'Maximise reliability continuously',
  ]}
  why="Reliability and change velocity are in direct tension, and pretending otherwise means the trade gets made implicitly by whoever is most persistent. A budget makes it explicit, shared, and arguable with evidence."
  gain="Prioritisation stops being a personality contest. Reliability work is justified by data. Feature work proceeds without guilt while the budget is healthy, which is most of the time."
  sacrifice="You are explicitly accepting that users will experience failures — and someone must be comfortable saying that out loud to a stakeholder who expected perfection."
  failureModes={[
    'The budget is exhausted and the agreement to pause feature work is quietly abandoned, which discredits the whole mechanism',
    'The SLI does not reflect user experience, so the budget tracks the wrong thing',
    'The target is set from what is currently achieved rather than from what users need',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Low technically, meaningful organisationally. Instrumenting the SLI is straightforward; sustaining the agreement about what happens when the budget runs out is the hard part."
  cost="Engineering time to measure properly. Repaid by the reliability work you do not do, because you can now show it is not needed."
  whenNot="For a pre-product-market-fit system with few users, formal SLOs are premature: the constraint is finding out what to build, and the reliability conversation has no stakes yet. Also for genuinely safety-critical systems, where the framing is wrong — you do not spend a budget of patient harm."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="sre-book">
Google's SRE practice defines SLIs as measurements of service behaviour, SLOs as targets for
them, and error budgets as the difference between the target and 100%. The budget is used
explicitly to balance reliability against release velocity.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-workbook">
Multi-window burn-rate alerting is the recommended approach: a fast burn over a short window
pages, a slow burn over a long window creates a ticket. This ties alert urgency to the rate at
which user impact is accumulating.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-well-architected">
Architecture review frameworks treat reliability targets as inputs to design rather than
outputs of it. The target determines whether multi-AZ or multi-region redundancy is justified,
not the other way around.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation">
Measure the SLI where the user is, or as close as you can get. An SLI computed inside the
service will be at its most wrong during exactly the incident you most needed it for.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We aim for five nines.',
      why: 'Usually stated without arithmetic. Five nines is 5 minutes a year, which forbids any manual intervention and requires nearly every dependency to be better than that. Most third-party services do not offer it.',
      instead:
        'Multiply the dependency chain and state the achievable ceiling. Then say what you would remove from the critical path to raise it.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Availability is measured by uptime.',
      why: 'A service can be running and useless: returning errors, timing out, or serving stale data. Users experience successful requests, not processes.',
      instead:
        'Measure the fraction of requests that succeeded within the latency threshold, from as close to the user as possible.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We alert on CPU, memory, disk, queue depth, error rate and latency.',
      why: 'Most of those are causes, not symptoms. Alerting on causes means being woken for conditions that are not affecting anyone, which trains people to ignore alerts.',
      instead:
        'Page on user-visible symptoms and on budget burn rate. Keep cause metrics on dashboards for diagnosis once you are already awake.',
    },
  ]}
/>
