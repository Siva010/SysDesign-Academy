## The problem

Your primary region is failing. You have a healthy standby in another region. The runbook says
&ldquo;update DNS to point at the standby&rdquo;, which you do, with a 60-second TTL.

Ten minutes later, a quarter of your traffic is still arriving at the dead region.

Nothing is broken. This is exactly how DNS is designed to behave, and if your failover plan
depends on it behaving otherwise, the plan does not work.

## Intuition

DNS is a phone book that everybody photocopies.

When you look up a name, you rarely ask the authoritative source. You ask your resolver, which
probably has a copy. Your operating system also kept a copy. So did your browser. So did the
runtime your application is written in. Each copy has a note saying how long it may be
considered current — but that note is advice, and not every holder of a copy takes it.

So when you change the phone book, you are not changing what people read. You are starting a
slow process of copies expiring, at a rate you only partly control.

The design consequence is blunt: **DNS is excellent for saying where something lives, and poor
for saying where something has just moved to.**

## Concretely

<Example title="Counting the caches between a user and your record">

A user types your domain. Before any query reaches your nameservers:

1. The **browser** checks its own DNS cache.
2. The **operating system** checks its resolver cache.
3. The **recursive resolver** — the user's ISP, or a public resolver — checks its cache.
4. Only if all three miss does anything reach your **authoritative nameservers**.

Each of those layers has its own expiry behaviour. Some honour your TTL exactly. Some enforce
a minimum of their own. Some applications resolve a name once at startup and hold the answer
for the lifetime of the process, which may be weeks.

<Claim label="fact" source="rfc1035">
DNS records carry a TTL that specifies how long a record may be cached before it should be
discarded. The TTL is set by the zone's operator.
</Claim>

<Claim label="inference">
Because caching happens at several layers, and because some resolvers apply their own minimum
TTLs, the observed convergence time after a change is a long tail rather than a deadline. The
practical consequence is that the old endpoint must keep working correctly for considerably
longer than the TTL you published.
</Claim>

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="dns" /> resolves a hierarchical name to an address by walking a delegation chain: the
root servers know who is authoritative for `.com`, those servers know who is authoritative for
`example.com`, and those servers hold the record. Caching at every level is what keeps this
usable at internet scale.

Three mechanisms move traffic, and they operate at different speeds:

- **DNS-based routing** returns different answers to different resolvers, often based on the
  resolver's apparent location. Cheap, flexible, and rate-limited by cache expiry.
- <C id="anycast" /> announces the same address from many locations, and the network's own
  routing sends each user to a topologically near one. Failover happens in the routing layer,
  in seconds, with no cache to wait for.
- **Load balancing** moves traffic behind a single stable address. Fastest of all, because
  nothing outside your control has to change its mind.

The general rule that follows: **the further from your infrastructure a routing decision is
cached, the slower you can change it.**

<Deeper title="Why a very short TTL is not the answer">

The obvious response to slow propagation is a 30-second TTL on everything. It has costs.

Every expiry forces a fresh lookup, which adds latency for the user — a full resolution can
take several round trips if the chain is cold — and load on your nameservers, which are now
a dependency on your critical path with a much higher request rate.

It also does not buy what you wanted. Resolvers that enforce minimum TTLs ignore you, and
applications that resolve once at startup ignore you completely. You pay the cost across
every user, every day, to shorten a tail you do not actually control.

<Claim label="recommendation">
Use a moderate TTL — minutes rather than seconds — and get fast traffic movement from a layer
you control: anycast, or a load balancer behind a stable address. Keep the old endpoint
serving correctly during and after any DNS change, and treat DNS as traffic management rather
than as failover.
</Claim>

</Deeper>

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Use anycast rather than DNS-based routing for regional failover"
  options={[
    'DNS-based failover with health checks and a short TTL',
    'Anycast: one address announced from several locations',
    'A single global load balancer address with regional backends',
  ]}
  why="Failover speed is bounded by the slowest cache between you and the user. Anycast moves the decision into network routing, where you can withdraw an announcement and see traffic move in seconds, with nothing outside your control needing to expire."
  gain="Failover in seconds rather than an indefinite tail. Users are routed to a topologically near location automatically, which also improves latency in normal operation. It is also the standard mechanism for absorbing volumetric attacks, because the traffic spreads across many locations."
  sacrifice="Operational complexity and a hard dependency on network-layer capability, which usually means a provider. You have less fine-grained control than DNS gives you: you can withdraw a location, not steer 10% of users."
  failureModes={[
    'A routing change mid-connection can move a user to a different location; long-lived connections may reset',
    'A misconfigured announcement can attract traffic to a location that cannot serve it',
    'Debugging is harder because which location a user reaches depends on network topology you cannot see',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate to high if self-operated: it requires network expertise most application teams do not have. Low if consumed as a provider feature, which is how most organisations use it."
  cost="Typically bundled with a CDN or edge provider and billed per request and per gigabyte. Self-operating it requires network presence in multiple locations, which is a different order of expense."
  whenNot="When users are concentrated in one region and a few minutes of failover time is acceptable, DNS failover is far simpler and adequate. It is also the wrong tool where you need percentage-based traffic steering for a gradual migration, because anycast gives you presence or absence, not proportions."

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="rfc1035">
The DNS delegation hierarchy and TTL-based caching are specified behaviour, not implementation
choices. Caching is what makes global name resolution feasible at all.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="cloudflare-blog">
Anycast is standard practice for edge networks and public DNS resolvers, both for latency and
for absorbing volumetric denial-of-service traffic across many locations rather than
concentrating it at one.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation">
Monitor DNS resolution from outside your own network. A zone problem is invisible from inside,
because internal resolvers often have their own view, and this class of outage is total from
the user's perspective while every internal dashboard stays green.
</Claim>

Inside a data centre the same problem reappears at a different timescale.
<C id="service-discovery">Service discovery</C> exists because instances appear and disappear
faster than any DNS TTL can track, so the mechanism has to push changes rather than wait for
expiry.

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We will fail over by updating DNS.',
      why: 'It works, eventually, for most users. During an incident, "eventually, for most" is the difference between a five-minute event and an hour-long one with a confusing partial-failure signature.',
      instead:
        'Say what the mechanism is and how fast it moves. If DNS is genuinely the mechanism, state that the old endpoint must keep serving, and that recovery has a long tail.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'DNS is just an implementation detail.',
      why: 'It is a dependency in the request path with its own availability, and one of the few whose failure makes everything unreachable while every internal dashboard stays green.',
      instead:
        'Treat resolution as a monitored dependency, measured from outside, with redundant nameservers in independent networks.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'A short TTL gives us fast failover.',
      why: 'It shortens one cache in a chain of several, at the cost of extra latency and nameserver load for every user in normal operation, which is 99.9% of the time.',
      instead:
        'Accept that DNS moves traffic in minutes, and put the fast mechanism somewhere you control.',
