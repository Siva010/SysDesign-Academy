## The problem

A request works from your laptop and fails from the office. Same code, same account, same
everything you can see.

Later, the same request works but arrives at your application claiming to come from an address in
another country. Then a colleague reports that the site says their session expired, but only
sometimes, and only on the corporate network.

None of these are bugs in your program. They are the visible effects of machines between the
client and your code — machines you did not write, cannot see in your logs, and did not know were
there. Reasoning about a system while pretending the wire is a straight line produces exactly this
category of unexplainable behaviour.

## Intuition

Think of posting a letter.

The address on the envelope gets it to a building. That is all the postal system promises: best
effort to a location. It does not promise the letter arrives, arrives once, or arrives in the
order you sent it.

Inside the building, a receptionist takes the letter, decides which department it belongs to,
maybe opens it, maybe keeps a copy, maybe refuses it because you have sent forty today. To you
the building has one address. Behind the desk there could be one person or two thousand.

Two different kinds of intermediary appear in that picture, and confusing them is the source of
most of the confusion in this area:

- Something acting **for the sender**, deciding where their mail goes and possibly reading it on
  the way out. That is a **proxy** — it belongs to the client's side.
- Something acting **for the recipient**, receiving all mail addressed to a building and
  distributing it inside. That is a **reverse proxy** — it belongs to the server's side.

They can be literally the same software. What makes one a proxy and the other a reverse proxy is
**whose interests it represents**, and therefore who configured it and who can change it.

## Concretely

<Example title="Three unexplainable behaviours, explained">

**The request that fails from the office.** A corporate forward proxy inspects outbound traffic
and blocks the domain. Nothing reached your server; there is no log line to find. The failure
lives on a machine your organisation may not even own.

**The request from another country.** Your application reads the source address of the connection
it received. But the connection it received came from your own reverse proxy, standing between it
and the user. Without the proxy passing the original address on in a header, every request in your
logs appears to originate from your own infrastructure. With it, every request carries a claim
about its origin that anyone can write — so it is trustworthy exactly to the extent that you trust
the last proxy to have overwritten it.

**The session that expires only sometimes.** Two application servers behind the reverse proxy hold
session state in local memory. A user whose requests land on server A and then server B has a
session on one and not on the other. The proxy did nothing wrong; it distributed traffic exactly as
designed, which is what made the application's hidden assumption visible.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="ip" /> delivers packets between addresses on a best-effort basis. Not reliable, not
ordered, not deduplicated, not confidential. Every stronger guarantee in networking is built on
top of that by someone paying for it — TCP pays round trips for reliability and ordering, TLS
pays a handshake for confidentiality.

An address identifies an **interface**, not a service and not a person. Several machines can share
one address, one machine can have several, and the address a server sees may belong to the last
device that handled the packet rather than to the client. Treating an address as an identity is
the root of a whole family of security mistakes.

A <C id="proxy" /> forwards traffic on behalf of the client. It is configured by the client side —
a browser setting, a corporate policy, an operating system rule — and the server generally cannot
tell it exists.

A <C id="reverse-proxy" /> forwards traffic on behalf of the server. Clients address it as if it
were the service. It exists because certain jobs are the same for every request and should
therefore not live in the application:

- Terminating TLS.
- Routing by path or host to different backends.
- Caching responses.
- Rate limiting and rejecting obvious abuse.
- Buffering slow clients so a slow reader does not occupy an application worker.

That last one is underrated. An application server holding a connection open while a client on a
poor mobile link reads a response at 20 KB/s has one fewer worker for the duration. A reverse proxy
absorbs the response quickly and drips it out, so the expensive tier is freed immediately.

<C id="tls-termination" /> is the point where the encrypted connection ends and plaintext begins.
It is a *location* decision, and it decides which parts of your infrastructure can read the traffic
and which parts merely move it:

- **At the edge.** One place to manage certificates, and everything behind it can route, cache and
  inspect. The internal network is now carrying plaintext.
- **At the application.** Nothing but the application sees plaintext, so a compromised load
  balancer reveals nothing. But the intermediary can no longer route by path, cache, or do anything
  else that requires understanding the request.
- **Re-encrypted after termination.** Terminate at the edge to get routing and caching, then open a
  fresh TLS connection to the backend. Both properties, at the cost of two handshakes and twice the
  certificate management.

There is no default answer. It follows from your threat model, and specifically from whether
&ldquo;someone is inside our network&rdquo; is a scenario you are designing against.

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'The application trusts a forwarded address',
      symptom: 'Rate limits, geo rules or audit logs are wrong, and can be manipulated by anyone.',
      cause: 'A client-supplied header naming an origin address was read as fact. Any client can send that header.',
      mitigation: 'Trust the forwarded address only when the proxy overwrites it rather than appends, and only for connections arriving from the proxy. Strip it at the edge for everything else.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Everything is rate limited as one client',
      symptom: 'Legitimate users are throttled at random once a reverse proxy is introduced.',
      cause: 'The limiter keys on the connection address, which is now the proxy for every request.',
      mitigation: 'Key limits on the forwarded client identity, and verify the assumption changed when the topology did.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Timeout mismatch between layers',
      symptom: 'Users see a gateway error while the application logs show the request completing successfully.',
      cause: 'The proxy timeout is shorter than the application timeout, so it gives up and the application finishes into a closed connection.',
      mitigation: 'Timeouts must increase outward from the innermost layer, and every layer must know the budget it was given.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Plaintext on the internal network',
      symptom: 'Nothing visible, until an incident review asks what an attacker inside the network could read.',
      cause: 'TLS terminated at the edge with no re-encryption, because it was never a decision, just a default.',
      mitigation: 'Make termination an explicit decision against a threat model, and re-encrypt where internal traffic must stay confidential.',
    },
    {
      what: 'The proxy becomes the single point of failure',
      symptom: 'Every service is unavailable although every service is healthy.',
      cause: 'One tier now sits in front of everything and was deployed as one unit.',
      mitigation: 'Redundant proxies, staged configuration rollout, and a rule that proxy config changes are deploys with the same review as code.',
    },
  ]}

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Terminate TLS at the reverse proxy rather than at the application"
  options={[
    'Terminate at the application: nothing but the application ever sees plaintext',
    'Terminate at the edge: one certificate story, and the edge can route and cache',
    'Terminate at the edge and re-encrypt to the backend',
    'Pass through at layer 4: the proxy moves bytes without understanding them',
  ]}
  why="Anything that needs to route by path, cache a response, or apply a rule based on the request must be able to read it. An intermediary that cannot read the request can only move connections, which rules out most of the reasons to have one."
  gain="Certificates managed in one place. Routing, caching, compression and rate limiting available at the edge. Application servers do no cryptographic work and are freed from slow clients."
  sacrifice="Traffic between the proxy and the application is plaintext unless you re-encrypt. The application no longer observes the client directly and must trust forwarded metadata. One more tier that can be misconfigured."
  failureModes={[
    'Forwarded headers trusted without verifying who set them',
    'Plaintext internal traffic that nobody decided to accept',
    'Proxy and application timeouts that disagree, producing gateway errors on successful requests',
    'Certificate expiry taking down every service at once, because there is now one certificate',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate and easy to underestimate. The proxy configuration becomes production logic with limited testing tools, and it is edited during incidents by people who are in a hurry."
  cost="Small in money. The real cost is a layer that every request passes through and that few engineers fully understand."
  whenNot="When end-to-end confidentiality is a requirement — regulated data, or a threat model that includes your own network — terminate at the application or re-encrypt. When the protocol is not HTTP, or when the proxy would only add a hop without doing any job, layer 4 pass-through is cheaper and simpler. And for a single application server, an added proxy tier is a new failure mode bought with no benefit."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="rfc9110">
HTTP defines intermediaries — proxies, gateways and tunnels — as part of the protocol rather than
as deployment accidents, and specifies that a response may be served by an intermediary from cache
rather than by the origin server.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="rfc8446">
TLS establishes confidentiality and integrity for a connection between two endpoints. An
intermediary that reads plaintext is one of those endpoints by definition, so terminating TLS is
exactly the decision about where the protected connection ends.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="mdn-http">
Forwarded and X-Forwarded-For carry the originating client address through proxies. Because they
are ordinary request headers, a client can send them, so their value is only meaningful when a
trusted intermediary has set or replaced it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="hpbn">
Placing termination close to users shortens the handshake round trips, which dominate the cost of
establishing a connection over long distances. This is a latency argument for edge termination
independent of any caching benefit.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="cloudflare-blog">
Published engineering writing on edge networks describes absorbing abusive traffic before it
reaches an origin as a primary function of the edge tier, rather than as a feature added to it.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'Drawing a load balancer without saying what it does.',
      why: 'A box labelled "LB" hides every decision that matters: layer 4 or layer 7, TLS terminated or passed through, what it caches, what it rejects. Those decisions determine what the rest of the design can assume.',
      instead:
        'Say what it terminates and what it can see. "L7, terminates TLS, routes by path, rate limits by API key" is a design; "load balancer" is a rectangle.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Treating the source IP address as an identity.',
      why: 'Many users share one address behind a carrier or a corporate network, one user changes address between networks, and behind a proxy the address you see is the proxy. Blocking or identifying by address is coarse and evadable at the same time.',
      instead:
        'Use addresses as a weak signal alongside authenticated identity, and be explicit that a forwarded address is a claim whose trust depends entirely on who last wrote it.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Assuming there are no intermediaries you did not put there.',
      why: 'Corporate proxies, mobile carrier middleboxes and enterprise inspection appliances all modify traffic. A failure with no server-side trace is often one of these, and time spent searching your own logs is time wasted.',
      instead:
        'When a request never arrives, treat the path as a suspect. Reproduce from a different network before assuming the bug is yours.',
