## The problem

Everything up to here has been taught. This is the test that matters: **a system nobody has shown
you, with requirements that do not all fit.**

Read the prompt. Then stop, and work it yourself before revealing anything. Reading a solution
produces the feeling of understanding without the substance; the value here is entirely in the
derivation.

Allow 60 to 90 minutes. Write it down — a document, a diagram, whatever you would produce for a
colleague. Writing forces the decisions that thinking lets you skip.

<Callout kind="warn" title="The exercise is the derivation">
The expert treatment below is one defensible answer, not the answer. If yours differs and you can
defend the difference from your assumptions, you have done the exercise correctly. Comparing
assumptions matters far more than comparing diagrams.
</Callout>

## The prompt

> Design a **collaborative document editing platform** for organisations.
>
> - Multiple people edit the same document simultaneously and see each other's changes.
> - Documents contain text and embedded images, and may be very large.
> - Organisations have strict access control: documents are shared with individuals, groups, or
>   an entire organisation, and permission changes must take effect immediately.
> - Full history is retained; any past version can be restored.
> - Customers are global. Some are contractually required to keep their data within a specific
>   jurisdiction.
> - Full-text search across everything a user may access.
> - Target: 40 million users, 5 million documents edited daily, peak 200,000 concurrent editing
>   sessions.

Six primitives, at least two conflicting requirements, and one constraint that quietly
invalidates the obvious architecture.

## Intuition

Before designing anything, ask what makes it hard. Not what it does — what breaks.

Three questions to answer first, because each rules out an entire family of designs:

1. **What is the unit of concurrency?** Documents are edited by a handful of people, not by
   millions. That means contention is per document and low — which is very different from a
   ticket sale.
2. **What must be immediate, and what may lag?** Permission changes must be immediate. Search
   indexes may lag by seconds. Those are different consistency requirements in the same system,
   and conflating them is the most common way to over-build this.
3. **What does data residency do to the design?** It is the constraint that invalidates the
   obvious answer, and noticing it early saves an hour.

## The expert treatment

<Deeper title="Reveal the worked solution — only after you have written yours">

### Primitives present

**Collaborative merge** (unusual; not in the standard eleven), **fanout** (presence and change
notification), **large-object** (images), **search-index**, **scheduling** (snapshot and
compaction jobs), and an access-control problem that behaves like an authorization primitive.

### The load-bearing decisions

**One authoritative session per document.** Concurrent edits require a single ordering point.
Rather than coordinating on every keystroke, one node owns a document's active session, holds its
state in memory, orders incoming operations, and broadcasts the result. Contention is per
document and low, so this scales by document count rather than by user count.

That node holds a **lease**. If it fails, another acquires the lease and rebuilds state from the
persisted operation log. Clients reconnect and replay unacknowledged operations, which they must
retain locally for exactly this reason.

**Operations, not documents, are the unit of storage.** Every edit is an operation appended to a
per-document log. This gives history for free, makes restore a replay to a point, and makes the
merge problem tractable. Periodic snapshots bound how far replay must go.

**Data residency determines the partitioning, and it is the constraint that decides the
architecture.** Each organisation has a home region; all its documents, history, search index and
images live there. This is not a performance optimisation — it is a legal requirement — and it
rules out the single global store that would otherwise be the obvious design.

Consequences that follow:

- Routing is by organisation, resolved at the edge, before anything else happens.
- Search is per-region. A cross-organisation global search is not possible for jurisdiction-bound
  customers, which is a product limitation and must be stated rather than engineered around.
- A user belonging to two organisations in different jurisdictions has two separate data homes,
  and the interface must make that coherent.

**Permissions are checked at request time.** Immediate revocation rules out embedding permissions
in a long-lived token. The permission service is on the critical path, so it needs a local cache
with a very short TTL — seconds — and an explicit invalidation on change. The trade is stated
plainly: a few seconds of revocation delay, not minutes, and never "until the token expires".

**Search is derived and eventually consistent.** Documents are indexed from the operation log
asynchronously. Results are filtered by permission **at query time**, not at index time, because
indexing permissions would require reindexing on every permission change.

**Images bypass everything.** Presigned upload directly to object storage in the organisation's
region, CDN delivery, metadata in the document store. Bytes never touch the application.

### The conflicts, and how they resolve

**"Immediate permission changes" versus "search must be fast".** Resolved by filtering at query
time rather than at index time. Search is fast, permissions are current, and the cost is that a
query fetching 100 candidates may return 40 after filtering — so you over-fetch.

**"Global product" versus "data residency".** Resolved by accepting a product limitation:
cross-jurisdiction search does not exist. Attempting to engineer around this produces either a
legal problem or a system that cannot be reasoned about.

**"Full history forever" versus "cost".** Resolved by tiering: recent operations hot, older
operations compacted into snapshots and moved to cheap storage. Restore from cold storage is
slower, and that is an acceptable, stated degradation.

### What breaks

- **Edit tier node fails mid-session.** Clients reconnect; a new node acquires the lease and
  rebuilds from the log; clients replay unacknowledged operations. The user sees a brief
  reconnecting state. This is the failure everyone forgets to design for.
- **Permission service unavailable.** Fail closed for permission *changes* and serve from the
  short-TTL cache for reads, so existing sessions continue and new grants stall. Failing open on
  reads would be a security incident; failing closed on reads would take the product down.
- **Search index falls behind.** Recent documents are not findable. Degrades rather than breaks,
  and needs alerting on index lag.
- **A document with 500 concurrent editors.** A hot document. The session node becomes the
  bottleneck. Mitigation: this is a product limit, and enforcing a maximum number of concurrent
  editors is more honest than degrading silently.
- **An organisation's region is unavailable.** Their documents are unavailable. Residency
  constrains failover to within the jurisdiction, so redundancy must exist inside the region
  rather than across regions.

### What we did not build

- **Global strong consistency.** Only the per-document operation ordering needs it, and that is
  achieved by a single session owner rather than by a consensus system spanning regions.
- **Cross-region replication of document content.** Residency forbids it for jurisdiction-bound
  customers, and building two paths — replicated for some, not for others — would double the
  complexity for a minority of customers.
- **Permissions in tokens.** Immediate revocation is a hard requirement.
- **A distributed lock service for editing.** The session lease serves the purpose with less
  machinery, and the operation log makes recovery straightforward.

</Deeper>

## Compare

The comparison worth making is not diagram against diagram. It is:

| Dimension | Question to ask yourself |
|---|---|
| Assumptions | Did you state them? Were they defensible? Would a different assumption have led somewhere better? |
| Primitives | Did you identify all six, or design around the ones you noticed? |
| The conflict | Did you find the residency constraint, and did you resolve it or ignore it? |
| Failure | Did you say what happens when the edit tier fails mid-session? |
| Scope | Did you state what you were *not* building, and why? |
| Cost | Did you say roughly what dominates the bill? |

A design that differs from the one above but answers all six is a better outcome than one that
matches it and answers three.

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Give each organisation a home region and forbid cross-region document replication"
  options={[
    'Global replication with per-document residency tags',
    'Home region per organisation, no cross-region replication',
    'Two architectures: replicated for unconstrained customers, pinned for constrained ones',
  ]}
  why="Residency is a legal constraint rather than a preference. A design where data can move and is prevented from moving by policy is one configuration error away from a compliance incident; a design where it cannot move is safe by construction."
  gain="Compliance is structural rather than procedural. Reasoning is simple: an organisation's data is in one place. No cross-region consistency problems, no conflict resolution across jurisdictions."
  sacrifice="No cross-jurisdiction search or sharing. A regional outage takes that organisation offline entirely, because failover outside the jurisdiction is not permitted."
  failureModes={[
    'Regional outage with no legal failover target',
    'A user in two organisations in different regions experiencing an incoherent product',
    'Growth in a region outstripping capacity, with no ability to spill over',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Higher: N regional deployments, each needing its own capacity planning, monitoring and on-call. Made tractable only by strong deployment automation."
  cost="Capacity cannot be pooled across regions, so utilisation is lower. Each region needs its own headroom and its own redundancy."
  whenNot="If no customer has a residency requirement, this is pure cost and a single global architecture with regional read replicas is better in every respect. The trap is building the constrained architecture for everyone because a minority of customers need it — the alternative worth considering is two deployment topologies for two customer classes, which doubles operational surface but keeps the majority simple."

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-well-architected">
Data residency and sovereignty requirements are treated as architectural constraints that
determine regional topology rather than as configuration applied afterwards, because enforcement
by construction is more reliable than enforcement by policy.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="spanner">
Providing strong consistency across geographically distributed replicas requires bounded clock
uncertainty and a commit wait, imposing latency on every commit. Systems that avoid needing it
across regions avoid that cost entirely.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="ddia">
Deriving searchable and analytical views from an authoritative operation log, asynchronously, is
a standard approach to giving several access patterns their own optimised representation without
compromising the source of truth.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="sre-book">
State explicitly what the system does when each component is unavailable. A design without a
failure section is a description of the happy path rather than an architecture.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>
