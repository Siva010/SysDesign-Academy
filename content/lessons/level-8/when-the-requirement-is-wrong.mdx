## The problem

Three requirements, each of which sounds reasonable and is not:

**&ldquo;It must be real-time.&rdquo;** Real-time means different things to a trading system
(microseconds), a chat application (a few hundred milliseconds) and a dashboard (a minute is
fine). Built to the strictest reading, the dashboard costs a hundred times more than it needs to.

**&ldquo;It must never lose data and must always be available.&rdquo;** These conflict during a
partition. One of them has to give, and if nobody chooses, the system will choose — probably
badly, and probably at 3am.

**&ldquo;We need a real-time analytics dashboard using Kafka.&rdquo;** This is a solution wearing
a requirement's clothes. Kafka is an answer; nobody has said what the question is.

The engineering skill at this level is recognising that **the requirement is the problem**, and
resolving it without either blindly complying or refusing.

## Intuition

Requirements arrive in three broken shapes, and each has a different repair.

**Underspecified.** &ldquo;It should be fast.&rdquo; Missing a number. The repair is to propose
one: &ldquo;I will assume p99 under 300 ms. If that is wrong, tell me now, because it changes the
design.&rdquo; Proposing beats asking, because it gives the other person something concrete to
disagree with.

**Overspecified.** &ldquo;Use Kafka with exactly-once semantics and an outbox.&rdquo; A solution
has been supplied instead of a need. The repair is to walk backwards: what problem does this
solve? Sometimes the answer is good and you proceed. Sometimes there is no such problem and you
have just saved a quarter.

**Internally inconsistent.** &ldquo;Never lose data, always available, and strongly
consistent.&rdquo; These cannot all hold during a partition. The repair is not to pick one
silently. It is to **surface the conflict, quantify what each choice costs, and get a decision
from whoever owns the trade.**

The last is the one that distinguishes staff-level work. Junior engineers implement the
requirement. Senior engineers notice it is impossible. Staff engineers get the right person to
choose, with the consequences made legible.

## Concretely

<Example title="Turning a solution back into a need">

**Stated:** &ldquo;We need real-time analytics on user behaviour, using a streaming platform.&rdquo;

The questions that recover the requirement:

| Question | Answer received | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Who looks at this, and when? | The growth team, each morning | &ldquo;Real-time&rdquo; means &ldquo;by 9am&rdquo; |
| What decision does it inform? | Which experiments to keep running | A daily decision, not a live one |
| What happens if it is an hour stale? | Nothing | Freshness requirement is hours, not seconds |
| How much data? | 40 million events a day | Modest for a batch pipeline |

The actual requirement: **a daily aggregate, available by 9am, over 40 million events.**

That is a nightly batch job writing into an analytical store. It costs a fraction of a streaming
platform, has far lower operational burden, and is easier to change when the growth team's
questions change — which they will, monthly.

**None of these questions were technical.** They were about who, when, and what decision depends
on it. The technical answer fell out once the requirement was recovered.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

A requirement is usable when it names three things: **what must be true, for whom, and what it
costs when it is not.**

The recurring failure is a requirement that specifies a mechanism. &ldquo;Use a queue&rdquo;
constrains the solution without stating the need. Walking it back — *what happens if the work is
done synchronously?* — usually recovers a real requirement about latency or about a dependency's
availability, and occasionally reveals that there was none.

Three conflicts appear often enough to name:

**Consistency versus availability.** These cannot both hold during a partition. The resolution is
almost never global: it is per operation, because most operations do not need the strong
guarantee.

**Freshness versus cost.** Every order of magnitude of freshness costs roughly an order of
magnitude. The question is not "how fresh would you like it" — everyone says "as fresh as
possible" — but "what breaks at one hour, one minute, one second?"

**Flexibility versus performance.** A system that answers any question is slower at every
question than one built for a specific access pattern. Requirements that ask for both are asking
for a trade to be made silently.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Surfacing a conflict is not refusing to build">
The failure mode of this lesson is an engineer who uses "the requirements are inconsistent" as a
reason not to proceed. That is worse than building the wrong thing. The move is: state the
conflict, quantify the options, recommend one, and build it — under stated assumptions if no
decision arrives.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Getting a decision from someone who does not want to make one">

The common failure is asking &ldquo;do you want consistency or availability?&rdquo; That is an
engineering question posed to someone who does not have the vocabulary, and the answer will be
&ldquo;both&rdquo;.

Translate it into their terms, with consequences attached:

> &ldquo;During a network problem between regions — which we should expect a few times a year —
> we can either keep taking orders and risk selling the last item twice, or stop taking orders in
> one region for a few minutes. Selling twice means a refund and an apology. Stopping means lost
> sales for that window. Which is worse for us?&rdquo;

Now it is a business question with two concrete outcomes, and the person who owns that trade can
answer it.

Three properties make this work:

- **Both options are real**, and neither is presented as obviously correct.
- **The consequences are concrete**: a refund, or lost sales — not "reduced consistency".
- **The frequency is stated**, so the decision is proportionate.

Write down the answer and who gave it. Not to assign blame later, but because this decision will
be re-litigated in eight months by someone who was not there, and the reasoning is what saves that
conversation.

</Deeper>

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Build under an explicitly stated assumption rather than waiting for the requirement to be resolved"
  options={[
    'Block until the requirement is clarified',
    'Build the strictest interpretation, so any answer is satisfied',
    'State an assumption, build under it, and flag it prominently',
  ]}
  why="Requirements are frequently unresolvable in the abstract, because the person who owns the decision cannot evaluate it without seeing something. A working system under a stated assumption produces the feedback that resolves the question."
  gain="Progress. The assumption is visible, so it can be challenged by anyone who disagrees. Most assumptions turn out to be right, and the ones that do not are discovered while the change is still cheap."
  sacrifice="Some rework when the assumption is wrong. The visibility of the assumption is doing all the work here — an unstated one is a liability rather than a trade."
  failureModes={[
    'The assumption is buried in a document nobody reads, so it is never challenged',
    'It is stated once at the start and forgotten as the design grows around it',
    'A wrong assumption on an expensive-to-reverse decision, such as a partition key',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="None technically. It requires the discipline to keep a visible list of assumptions and revisit it, which is a habit rather than a process."
  cost="Occasional rework, weighed against the delivery time lost to blocking."
  whenNot="When the decision is expensive to reverse and the assumption is genuinely uncertain — a partition key, a data model, a regulatory boundary — the cost of being wrong exceeds the cost of waiting. There, escalate properly and do not proceed on a guess. The discipline is knowing which decisions are which."

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="convention" source="fundamentals-arch">
Architecture characteristics — the qualities a system must exhibit — are described as needing to
be explicit, measurable and prioritised, because they conflict and an unprioritised list provides
no guidance when a trade must be made.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-book">
Service level objectives are recommended as the mechanism for turning vague reliability
expectations into a number that can be traded against delivery velocity, precisely because
"as reliable as possible" is not actionable.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="martin-fowler">
Recording architecture decisions with their context and consequences is standard practice, and
its main value appears later, when someone who was not present asks why the system is the way it
is.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>
