## The problem

You have done the arithmetic properly. 200,000 reads per second, spread across a cache cluster
of 20 nodes. That is 10,000 per node — comfortable.

A celebrity posts. Two million requests per second arrive for a single item.

Your 20-node cluster does not become a 20-way split of 2 million. Every one of those requests
hashes to the same key, and that key lives on exactly one node. One node receives 2 million
requests per second and saturates its network interface. The other nineteen are idle.

Adding nodes does not help. Adding nodes has never helped. **A single key cannot be
partitioned, because that is what "key" means.**

## Intuition

Almost every distributed design begins with a division: split the work across N machines, each
gets 1/N.

That division assumes the work is divisible and evenly distributed. Real workloads are neither.

- A few products account for most of the sales.
- A few users account for most of the API calls.
- A few keys account for most of the reads.
- A few hours of the day account for most of the traffic.

This is not an occasional anomaly; it is the normal shape of human-generated demand. Designing
for the average and discovering the distribution in production is one of the most reliable ways
to be surprised.

Two distinct problems hide under "uneven load", and they need different answers:

**A hot partition** is one shard receiving far more than its share, because the partition key
groups too much traffic together. It is fixable by choosing a better key.

**A hot key** is one *individual key* receiving enormous traffic. No partitioning scheme fixes
it, because there is nothing left to divide.

## Concretely

<Example title="The same skew, three different failures">

**Reads on one key.** Two million requests per second for one cached item. The cache node
holding it saturates. Every other node is idle. Fixes: serve it from the edge, or replicate it
into every application instance's local memory, or split the key.

**Writes on one row.** Every purchase of a flash-sale item decrements the same inventory row.
The database serialises them, so throughput on that feature is the throughput of one row,
regardless of how many application servers you run. Fixes: split the counter into K sub-rows,
or move to an append-only reservation model.

**Fanout on one account.** A user with 50 million followers posts. Fanout-on-write means 50
million writes for one action, which is minutes of work for one post while ordinary posts take
milliseconds. Fixes: exclude large accounts from fanout and merge their posts at read time.

Notice that all three are the same underlying skew, and none of the three fixes are the same.
&ldquo;Handle the hot key&rdquo; is not one technique.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="load-distribution" /> assumes independence between requests. Skew is what happens when
requests are correlated — and they are correlated because people are correlated. Everyone wants
the same viral video, buys the same discounted item, follows the same famous account.

Three responses exist, and the choice depends on the read/write mix:

**Replicate the hot thing.** If it is read-heavy and tolerates staleness, put a copy close to
every reader: a CDN for public content, an in-process
<C id="local-cache">local cache</C> for internal reads. Load divides by the number of
replicas. This is by far the cheapest answer when it applies.

**<C id="key-splitting">Split the key</C>.** Turn one key into K sub-keys — `counter:0`
through `counter:9` — so writes distribute. Reads must now aggregate K values, so you have
traded a cheap read for a cheap write.

**Detect and route.** Identify hot keys at runtime and treat them differently: pin them to
dedicated capacity, admit them at a lower rate, or promote them to a local cache. More
machinery, and it adapts to skew you cannot predict.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Consistent hashing does not solve hot keys">
Consistent hashing distributes *distinct* keys evenly and keeps redistribution cheap when nodes
change. It says nothing about one key being popular. Virtual nodes smooth the distribution of
many keys; they cannot smooth the distribution of one.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Why skew gets worse as you scale, not better">

Intuition says more machines means more headroom. For skewed workloads the opposite is often
true.

With 4 nodes, each holds 25% of the key space, so a hot key sits alongside a lot of other
traffic — the node is already provisioned for a substantial share. With 100 nodes, each holds
1% of the key space and is provisioned accordingly, so a hot key represents a far larger
multiple of what its node expects.

The ratio that matters is not total load over total capacity. It is **the busiest node's load
over one node's capacity**, and splitting the cluster finer makes the denominator smaller while
the hot key's numerator stays the same.

<Claim label="inference">
This is why systems that partition aggressively often need an explicit hot-key mechanism,
while systems on fewer, larger nodes may never notice the skew. The skew did not change; the
capacity available to absorb it did.
</Claim>

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {

      symptom:

      cause:
        'All requests for one key hash to one node. Cluster size is irrelevant to a single key.',
      mitigation:
        'Replicate the value closer to readers: local cache or edge. If it must be shared, split the key and aggregate on read.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Hot partition from a poor partition key',
      symptom:
        'One shard consistently busier than the others, not for one key but for a whole range.',
      cause:
        'The partition key groups correlated traffic: partitioning by date means today is always hot; by country means your largest market is always hot.',
      mitigation:
        'Choose a key with high cardinality and even access. Where a natural key is skewed, prefix it with a hash or a bucket to spread it.',
    },
    {

      symptom:
        'Publishing latency is fine for almost everyone and catastrophic for a handful of accounts. Queue depth spikes on individual posts.',

        'Fanout-on-write costs one write per follower, so a 50-million-follower account costs 50 million writes for one action.',

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Add a small in-process cache in front of the shared cache to absorb hot keys"
  options={[
    'Shared cache only: one consistent view, one node per key',
    'Local in-process cache with a short TTL, backed by the shared cache',
    'Split hot keys into K sub-keys and aggregate on read',
    'Push the hot content to a CDN so it never reaches you',
  ]}
  why="A hot key is by definition read by many callers. Putting a copy in each caller divides the load by the number of callers, which is the only mechanism that scales with your fleet rather than against it."
  gain="Hot-key load on the shared cache divides by instance count. Latency for those reads drops to a memory access with no network hop. It degrades gracefully: if the shared cache is slow, local copies keep serving."
  sacrifice="Instances now actively disagree with each other for up to the local TTL. A value updated centrally is visible on different instances at different moments, so users can see it appear and disappear as they are load balanced."
  failureModes={[
    'Inconsistency between instances, visible to a user moving between them',
    'Memory pressure if the local cache is unbounded',
    'A cold local cache on every deploy, so the shared cache sees a burst after each rollout',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Low: a library, a size bound and a TTL. The genuine cost is conceptual — you now have two cache layers with different invalidation stories, and 'why is this value different on one server' becomes a recurring question."
  cost="A few megabytes per instance. Negligible in money, and one of the highest-leverage changes available for read-heavy skewed workloads."
  whenNot="When the value must be identical for all users at all times — balances, permissions, inventory at point of sale — per-instance disagreement is a correctness problem rather than a staleness one. And when the hot content is public and identical for everyone, the CDN is strictly better: it absorbs the traffic before it reaches you at all."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="dynamo">
Dynamo uses consistent hashing with virtual nodes to distribute keys evenly across nodes and to
limit redistribution when membership changes. The paper addresses distribution of many keys; a
single popular key remains a separate problem.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="meta-eng">
Public engineering writing on large social platforms consistently describes hybrid fanout
strategies rather than a single approach, precisely because follower distributions are
heavy-tailed and no single strategy handles both ends.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="aws-builders-library">
Measure the busiest partition, not the average. Aggregate utilisation across a cluster hides
skew almost perfectly: a cluster at 15% average utilisation can contain a node at 100%, and
only per-node or per-partition metrics reveal it.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>
