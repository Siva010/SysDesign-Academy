## The problem

Your database has one leader and two followers. It works, and then at 03:00 the leader stops
responding.

Nobody knows whether it is dead or merely unreachable, and there is no way to find out — those two
situations look identical from every other machine. If you promote a follower and the old leader
was alive, you now have two machines accepting writes for the same rows. If you wait, and it was
dead, you are down.

You promote. Ninety seconds later the old leader comes back, still believing it is the leader,
holding forty writes it accepted and never replicated. Those writes are in nobody else's copy, and
the new leader has since accepted writes to the same rows.

Nothing here was a mistake. The single-leader design is correct, the failover was reasonable, and
you now have two versions of the truth and no rule for choosing between them.

## Intuition

One leader is a decision about where **order** is decided. Every write goes through one machine,
so that machine can say what happened first, and every replica applies the same sequence. That is
the entire source of the design's simplicity, and it is worth naming, because everything that
follows is about giving it up.

Give it up and you get something. There are only two things worth having:

**Writes accepted in more than one place** — so a user in Sydney does not wait 150 milliseconds for
a leader in Virginia, or so a laptop can accept edits with no network at all.

**Writes that continue during a failure** — so no single machine's death stops the system, and
there is no promotion decision to get wrong.

And you pay for it with the same thing in both cases: **two machines accepted conflicting writes
and neither was wrong to.** With one leader that cannot happen. Without one it is not an edge case
— it is the normal operation of the system, and the design is not finished until you have said what
happens.

The honest framing is that these architectures do not remove difficulty. They **move** it: from a
rare, acute problem (failover, at 03:00, under pressure) to a continuous, chronic one (conflicts,
every day, in application code). Which you prefer is a real question with a real answer, and the
answer depends on your product rather than on which architecture is more sophisticated.

## Concretely

<Example title="One shopping cart, three replication designs">

A user adds a scarf on their phone and, seconds later, removes a hat on their laptop. The two
requests reach different places.

**Single leader.** Both go to the leader. It applies them in the order it received them. The cart
has the scarf and no hat. There is nothing to resolve, because one machine saw both.

**Multi-leader across regions.** The phone writes to Sydney, the laptop to Virginia. Each accepts
locally and replicates asynchronously. Both regions now have a cart the other has not seen, and
they must merge. Last-write-wins picks one whole cart and discards the other — so either the scarf
vanishes or the hat comes back, and the user is certain they did the thing that got discarded.

**Leaderless with quorums.** Both writes go to whichever replicas answer. If the write quorums
overlap, the conflict is visible and both versions are retained for the application to resolve. If
they were sloppy quorums during a partition, the overlap is not guaranteed and the conflict may not
be detected until anti-entropy runs.

Notice the useful thing: for a cart, the correct merge is not a choice at all. **Union the
additions, union the removals, apply removals last.** The conflict disappears because the data type
has a merge rule. That is not a trick — it is the entire idea behind conflict-free replicated types,
and it is available whenever the data has such a rule.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

**Single leader.** One replica accepts writes and propagates them. Simple, and the entire risk
concentrates in <C id="failover" />, which is three hard problems at once:

- **Detection.** A <C id="heartbeat" /> plus a timeout is a *guess*. A dead machine and a slow
  network are indistinguishable from outside, and no timeout separates them — a shorter one
  promotes during a hiccup, a longer one extends every outage.
- **Split brain.** If the old leader is alive and does not know it was replaced, two leaders accept
  writes. Fencing — a term that only increases, which storage refuses to accept when stale — is
  what actually prevents this, and it must be enforced by the resource rather than agreed among
  the leaders.
- **Lost writes.** Anything the old leader accepted and had not replicated is gone, or has to be
  discarded when it returns. Asynchronous replication means this is not a possibility, it is a
  quantity, and it is your recovery point objective whether or not anyone wrote it down.

**<C id="multi-leader" />.** Several replicas accept writes and replicate to each other. Justified
by write latency across regions, or by clients that must work offline. The cost is that conflicts
are guaranteed rather than occasional, and they are guaranteed *for the rows that are edited most*.

**<C id="leaderless" />.** Any replica accepts a write; the client or a coordinator writes to
several and reads from several (<Cite id="dynamo" />). With N replicas, W written and R read,
**W + R &gt; N** forces the read set and the write set to share at least one replica, so a read sees
the latest acknowledged write. This is quorum intersection, and it is the whole mechanism.

Three refinements matter, and the third is the one people miss.

**Read repair.** A read that sees disagreeing replicas writes the newer value back to the stale
one. It converges the data you actually read, which is exactly the data you least need converged.

**<C id="anti-entropy" />.** A background comparison — typically by hashing ranges into a tree so
that identical ranges are dismissed cheaply — that finds and repairs differences in data nobody has
read. Without it, rarely-read records drift indefinitely, and the drift is invisible until a
replica is lost and takes the only correct copy with it.

**<C id="sloppy-quorum">Sloppy quorums</C> and <C id="hinted-handoff">hinted handoff</C>.** During a partition, if the replicas
that should hold a key are unreachable, the write is accepted by *whichever* nodes are reachable,
which hold it as a hint and hand it back when the home nodes return. Availability improves. And the
guarantee changes:

<Callout kind="warn" title="A sloppy quorum is not a quorum">
W + R &gt; N guarantees overlap only when the W nodes and the R nodes are drawn from the same N
home replicas. Under a sloppy quorum the write went to nodes outside that set, so a subsequent read
of the home replicas can legitimately miss it and return an older value.

This is not a bug and it is not misconfiguration. It is the trade being taken: durability and
availability of the write, in exchange for the read guarantee you thought the arithmetic gave you.
Systems that offer it usually offer it as a setting, which means someone chose — possibly by
accepting a default.
</Callout>

**<C id="conflict-resolution" />** is where the design is finished or abandoned. Four options, in
increasing order of effort and correctness:

- **Last write wins.** Order by timestamp, keep one. Trivial, and it silently discards a write —
  and because timestamps come from different machines with different clocks, it can discard the
  *newer* one. The problem is not that it discards; it is that it discards invisibly.
- **Version vectors.** Detect that two writes were concurrent, without ordering them by time. This
  *detects*; it does not resolve. Both versions are returned and someone must decide.
- **Application merge.** The application knows the semantics and can do the right thing — union the
  cart, take the maximum reading, present both to the user. Correct, and it is real work per data
  type.
- **<C id="crdt" />.** A data type whose merge is defined, associative, commutative and idempotent,
  so replicas converge automatically regardless of order. Genuinely automatic for counters, sets,
  maps and sequences. Not available for an opaque value with no merge rule — and applying one there
  degrades into last-write-wins with extra machinery.

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Accept writes on more than one node instead of routing every write through a leader"
  options={[
    'Single leader, synchronous replication to one follower',
    'Single leader, asynchronous replication',
    'Multi-leader across regions',
    'Leaderless with quorum reads and writes',
  ]}
  why="Writes continue when a machine or a region fails, with no promotion decision and no split-brain window; and writes complete at local latency rather than at the distance to a leader. Both matter more as the geography spreads."
  gain="Write availability that survives losing nodes. Local write latency in every region. No failover procedure to get wrong at three in the morning, which removes an entire class of incident."
  sacrifice="Conflicts become ordinary and must be resolved by the application. Read guarantees become arithmetic you have to state rather than a property you inherit. Convergence needs background repair that must be monitored."
  failureModes={[
    'Conflicts resolved by last-write-wins, silently losing user work',
    'Sloppy quorums quietly weakening the guarantee the arithmetic implied',
    'Divergence in unread data, discovered when a replica is lost',
    'Deleted records resurrected by a node offline past tombstone retention',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="High and continuous. Repair processes to monitor, per-type merge logic to maintain, and a consistency model that every engineer touching the data must understand rather than assume."
  cost="More storage and more cross-node traffic, plus real engineering time on merge logic. The saving is on the incident side: no failover runbook, no promotion decision, no split-brain postmortem."
  whenNot="When the data has no meaningful merge — a single-valued field two people edit, a balance, an inventory count. Merging those means choosing whose work to discard, and no framework makes that correct. When one region can serve everyone at acceptable latency, multi-leader buys conflicts for nothing. And when the team cannot commit to operating repair processes and writing merge functions per data type, a single leader with a rehearsed failover is the more reliable system in practice, whatever the architecture diagram suggests."

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="dynamo">
Dynamo uses quorum-style reads and writes with configurable N, R and W, sloppy quorums with hinted
handoff to preserve write availability during partitions, and both read repair and an anti-entropy
process using Merkle trees to converge replicas.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="dynamo">
Concurrent versions are detected with version vectors and returned to the client for
reconciliation, on the grounds that the application knows the semantics of the data and the
storage system does not.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="cassandra-paper">
A partitioned, replicated store with tunable consistency lets each operation choose how many
replicas must respond, so the consistency and availability trade is made per operation rather than
once for the whole system.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="cassandra-docs">
Deletions are recorded as tombstones with a retention period. A replica that is offline for longer
than that period must be rebuilt rather than rejoined, because it can otherwise reintroduce data
whose deletion marker has been removed elsewhere.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="gilbert-lynch">
When a network partition occurs, a system must give up either availability or linearizable
consistency. Accepting writes on both sides of a partition is choosing availability, and the
divergence that follows is the cost, not a defect.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Ordering concurrent writes by wall-clock timestamp is unreliable because clocks on different
machines disagree; last-write-wins therefore discards data and can discard the write that happened
later.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

      instead:
