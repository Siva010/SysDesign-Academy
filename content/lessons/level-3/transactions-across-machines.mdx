## The problem

An order is placed. The code does two things:

    db.insert(order)
    queue.publish(OrderPlaced)

It reads like one action. It is two, and there are four possible outcomes. Two of them are fine.
One leaves an order that nobody downstream ever hears about — no confirmation email, no warehouse
pick, no invoice. The other publishes an event about an order that does not exist, and every
consumer of it fails on a missing row.

Nobody wrote a distributed transaction. Nobody discussed one. But two systems now have to agree,
and there is no mechanism making them.

This is the most common distributed transaction in software, and it usually appears in code
review as two ordinary lines.

## Intuition

A transaction on one machine is atomic for a very ordinary reason: **one process decides, and it
writes the decision down before telling anyone.** The commit record hits the log, and after that
the outcome is a fact that survives a crash. Recovery reads the log and knows what happened.

Involve a second machine and you need two properties that are now separate:

- Somebody must **decide** the outcome.
- Everybody must **obey** the decision.

Between those two moments, any participant can crash, and the network can stop delivering. A
machine that promised to commit and then lost contact cannot decide for itself: committing might
contradict the group, and aborting might too. It must wait. That waiting — while holding whatever
locks it took to make the promise — is the fundamental cost, and it is not an implementation
weakness that a better protocol removes.

So there are three honest strategies, and choosing between them is the content of this lesson:

1. **Avoid it.** Arrange the data so the transaction fits on one machine. Almost always the best
   answer, and it is a decision about boundaries made long before the code.
2. **Coordinate it.** Two-phase commit gives real atomicity and pays in availability and blocking.
3. **Give up atomicity, keep the outcome.** A sequence of local transactions with compensating
   actions. No isolation, more code, and it stays available.

## Concretely

<Example title="One booking, three strategies">

A trip books a flight, a hotel and a car, each owned by a different service.

**Two-phase commit.** All three prepare, promising they can commit and holding their reservations.
The coordinator commits. Either the whole trip books or none of it does, and no user ever sees a
half-booked trip. If the coordinator dies after the prepare, all three sit holding reservations
they can neither release nor confirm, until a human intervenes. If any one service is down, no trip
can be booked at all.

**A saga.** Book the flight and commit. Book the hotel and commit. Book the car — and it fails. Now
run compensations: cancel the hotel, cancel the flight. The trip did not happen and nothing is
stranded. But for about four seconds the user had a flight and a hotel and no car, and if they
refreshed the page they saw it. And the flight cancellation may cost a fee, because compensation
is not rollback.

**Avoid it.** If all three were rows in one database, this would be a single transaction with none
of the above. That is not always possible — three vendors are genuinely three systems — but the
question &ldquo;why is this data in three places?&rdquo; is the one to ask first, because
frequently the answer is a service boundary drawn for organisational reasons that nobody has
revisited.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="distributed-transaction" /> means one atomic outcome across participants that do not share a
log.

**<C id="two-phase-commit" />** is the standard protocol. A coordinator asks every participant to
*prepare*: a participant that votes yes has durably recorded its intent and taken whatever locks
are needed, and has given up the right to abort. Once every vote is yes, the coordinator records
the commit and tells everyone.

It is correct, and it costs three things:

- **Latency.** Two round trips plus a durable write at every participant, on the critical path.
- **Availability that multiplies.** The transaction commits only if every participant is up.
  Five participants at 99.9% give 99.5% for the transaction, so the design has made itself less
  available than any of its parts.
- **Blocking.** This is the one that matters. A participant that voted yes and then lost the
  coordinator **cannot decide alone**, because committing might contradict the group and so might
  aborting. It waits, holding its locks. If the coordinator has crashed with its log, that wait ends
  when a human reads the participant logs and decides. Rows stay locked and unrelated traffic
  touching them stalls.

**<C id="saga" />** abandons atomicity and keeps the business outcome. Each step is a local
transaction that commits immediately; each has a **compensating action** that semantically undoes
it. On failure, run the compensations for the completed steps in reverse.

Three consequences follow, and the third is the one that bites:

- **No isolation.** Intermediate states are visible. Another process can see a half-completed saga
  and act on it. The usual remedy is a *semantic lock* — an explicit `pending` state that other
  operations know to refuse — which is isolation you implement yourself, in the domain.
- **Compensation is not rollback.** It is a new action with its own effects. Cancelling a booking
  may cost a fee; an email cannot be unsent, only apologised for.
- **Some steps cannot be compensated.** Money leaving for a third party, a message delivered, a
  physical item dispatched. So **ordering is a design decision**: put every reversible step first
  and the irreversible step last, so that a failure never strands you after the point of no return.

**<C id="distributed-lock" />** is the tempting third option, and it solves a different problem.
A lock gives mutual exclusion, not atomicity — it stops two processes acting at once and does
nothing to make one process's multi-machine writes all-or-nothing. It also brings the lease
problem: a holder can be paused past its expiry and act while another holder is active, so a lock
is only safe when the protected resource checks a fencing token. Before reaching for one, ask
whether a **conditional write against the single authority** would do the job — it usually would,
it is atomic by construction, and it needs no additional service.

**<C id="strict-serializability" />** — sometimes called <C id="external-consistency" /> — is
serializability plus real-time order: if A completes before B begins, every observer sees A first.
It is the strongest practical guarantee and it is expensive in a specific way worth knowing.
Spanner achieves it using bounded clock uncertainty and **waiting out the error bar** before
acknowledging a commit, so that timestamp order and real-time order cannot disagree
(<Cite id="spanner" />). Commit latency is therefore a function of how well your clocks are
synchronised, which is a striking illustration that the strongest consistency has a physical price
rather than merely an algorithmic one.

<Callout kind="info" title="The pattern that removes most of these problems">
For the specific case of a database write plus a message, there is a standard answer that needs no
distributed transaction at all. Write the business change **and the outgoing message** into the
same local database, in one ordinary transaction. A separate process reads unpublished messages
and publishes them.

The two writes are now one atomic local write. Publication becomes at-least-once, which is a
problem you already know how to solve with an idempotent consumer. Almost every dual write in an
ordinary system can be removed this way, and it is why the transactional outbox pattern is worth
knowing before two-phase commit is.
</Callout>

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Use a saga with compensations instead of a distributed transaction"
  options={[
    'Redesign so the transaction fits in one database',
    'Two-phase commit across participants',
    'A saga of local transactions with compensating actions',
    'Accept eventual consistency with periodic reconciliation and no compensation',
  ]}
  why="Two-phase commit makes availability the product of every participant's availability and can block indefinitely on coordinator failure. A saga keeps each step independently committed, so no participant is ever waiting on a decision that may never arrive."
  gain="Every step commits locally and releases its locks immediately. No coordinator to fail. The system stays available when a participant is briefly down, because retry is a normal part of the flow."
  sacrifice="Atomicity and isolation. Intermediate states are visible and other operations may act on them. Compensation logic must be written, tested and maintained for every step, and it is not the same as rolling back."
  failureModes={[
    'Compensation itself failing, leaving a half-undone saga',
    'An irreversible step ordered before a step that can fail',
    'Another process acting on a visible intermediate state',
    'Compensations that are never exercised outside incidents, and therefore do not work',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Significant. Sagas need state tracking, a stuck-saga alert, idempotent and infinitely retryable compensations, and a manual resolution path. The code is roughly double, because every forward step has a backward one."
  cost="Engineering time rather than infrastructure. The recurring cost is that compensation paths are rarely executed, so they rot quietly and are first exercised when something is already wrong."
  whenNot="When the operation genuinely requires isolation — nobody may observe the intermediate state — a saga cannot provide it and a semantic lock is a partial substitute at best. When all participants share one database, a plain transaction is correct and infinitely simpler. And when the number of steps is large, the compensation matrix becomes the dominant complexity in the system, which is a signal that the service boundaries are wrong rather than that the saga needs more machinery."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Two-phase commit is blocking: a participant that has voted to commit cannot unilaterally decide the
outcome and must wait for the coordinator, holding its locks. If the coordinator fails, that wait
continues until it recovers or a human intervenes.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Writing to two systems without a shared transaction produces states where one write succeeded and
the other did not, and no amount of ordering or retrying removes the window; it only changes which
inconsistency is more likely.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="spanner">
Spanner provides externally consistent transactions by assigning commit timestamps and waiting out
the bound on clock uncertainty before making a commit visible, so that timestamp order matches real
time order. Commit latency therefore depends directly on the clock uncertainty bound.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="postgres-docs">
PostgreSQL supports prepared transactions for two-phase commit, and documents that a prepared
transaction holds its locks until it is committed or rolled back, so a forgotten prepared
transaction blocks other work and consumes resources indefinitely.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="cockroach-docs">
Distributed SQL systems keep transactions that span ranges possible but note that cross-range
transactions cost additional coordination, so schema and key design that keeps related data
together materially improves performance.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="martin-fowler">
The saga pattern is described as a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions, and
is presented as trading atomicity and isolation for availability rather than as a drop-in
replacement for a transaction.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'Writing to the database and then publishing an event.',
      why: 'These are two operations across two systems. A crash in between leaves an order nobody hears about or an event about an order that does not exist, and no retry strategy closes the window.',
      instead:
        'Write the event into the same database in the same transaction and publish it from there. Atomicity becomes local, and delivery becomes an at-least-once problem you already know how to solve.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Reaching for a distributed lock to make an operation safe.',
      why: 'A lock provides mutual exclusion, not atomicity, so multi-machine writes are still not all-or-nothing. And without a fencing token checked by the resource, a paused holder can act after its lease expired.',
      instead:
        'Ask whether a conditional write against the one authority would do it. It usually would, it is atomic by construction, and it removes a service from the design.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Treating compensation as rollback.',
      why: 'Rollback erases; compensation adds a new action with its own effects and its own failure modes. Some steps have no compensation at all, and discovering that during design is much cheaper than discovering it during an incident.',
      instead:
        'For each step, write down its compensation and whether it can fail. Order the steps so that everything irreversible happens last.',
    },
  ]}
