## The problem

&ldquo;We need the system to be consistent.&rdquo;

That sentence has been the start of more wasted architecture than almost any other. It could
mean:

- Every read returns the most recently written value, globally.
- Transactions do not interleave in ways that break my invariants.
- A user always sees their own changes.
- Everyone eventually sees the same thing.
- The data satisfies my foreign keys.

Those are five different guarantees with five different costs, ranging from free to a
cross-region round trip on every operation. Choosing the strongest one by default is expensive;
choosing the weakest by accident produces bugs that look like magic.

## Intuition

Think about what a reader can be promised, from weakest to strongest.

**&ldquo;You will see something.&rdquo;** Maybe stale, maybe from a replica that missed the last
few writes. Fast, always available.

**&ldquo;You will not see time run backwards.&rdquo;** Values may be stale, but you will not see
a newer value and then an older one. A per-reader promise, and cheap.

**&ldquo;You will see your own writes.&rdquo;** Other people's changes may lag, but yours are
always visible to you. Also per-reader, also cheap, and it fixes the anomaly users actually
notice.

**&ldquo;You will never see an effect before its cause.&rdquo;** If a reply exists, the message
it replies to exists. Causal consistency — the strongest guarantee that survives a network
partition.

**&ldquo;You will see the latest committed value, always, everywhere.&rdquo;** Linearizability.
Requires coordination on every operation, and during a partition, the minority side must refuse
to answer.

The critical realisation: **the middle three are usually what people actually want when they say
&ldquo;consistent&rdquo;**, and they are dramatically cheaper than the last one.

## Concretely

<Example title="Choosing per operation, not per system">

One e-commerce system, four operations, four different answers:

| Operation | Guarantee needed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Product page view count | Eventual | Nobody can tell, and nobody would care if they could. |
| Product description | Bounded staleness, minutes | A stale description is a minor annoyance, and cheap edge caching is worth far more. |
| Shopping basket | Read-your-writes | Users must see their own additions. Other users are irrelevant — it is their basket. |
| Stock at checkout | Linearizable | Selling the last unit twice costs money and trust. This is the one operation that justifies coordination. |

One of the four needs the expensive guarantee. Applying it to all four would multiply the cost
of the system for no user-visible benefit — and, worse, would make view counts unavailable
during a partition that only the checkout path genuinely needs to be conservative about.

**Consistency is chosen per operation, not per database.**

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="linearizability" /> is a guarantee about single objects: the system behaves as if there
were one copy, and each operation took effect at a single instant between its invocation and
its response. It respects real-time ordering — if operation A completed before B started, B sees
A's effect.

<C id="serializability" /> is a guarantee about *transactions*: the result equals some serial
execution. It says nothing about which order, and nothing about real time.

They are independent. A system can be one without the other, and combining both is **strict
serializability**, the strongest practical guarantee and the most expensive.

<C id="cap" />, stated properly:

<Claim label="fact" source="gilbert-lynch">
The formal result is that it is impossible for a distributed system to simultaneously provide
linearizable consistency, availability at every non-failing node, and tolerance of network
partitions. Under a partition, a system must sacrifice either linearizability or availability.
</Claim>

The popular framing — &ldquo;pick two of three&rdquo; — is wrong in a way that matters.
Partitions are not a design choice; the network will partition. So P is not optional, and CAP
describes what happens **during a partition only**: answer, or be right.

<C id="pacelc" /> completes it, and the second half is the one you live with daily:

<Claim label="fact" source="abadi-pacelc">
PACELC extends CAP: during a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else — in normal
operation — choose Latency or Consistency. Systems that replicate synchronously pay latency on
every operation, not only during failures.
</Claim>

Partitions are rare. Normal operation is constant. **The latency-versus-consistency trade is
therefore the one that shapes your system**, and CAP alone does not mention it.

<Callout kind="info" title="Eventual consistency is a choice, not a compromise">
For likes, view counts, feeds, search indexes and recommendations, a slightly stale answer is
more valuable to the user than a slow one or no answer at all. These are not systems that
"gave up" on consistency; they chose the guarantee that fits the data.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Session guarantees: most of the value, almost none of the cost">

The gap between "eventual" and "linearizable" is enormous, and most of the practical value sits
just above the bottom.

<C id="session-guarantees">Session guarantees</C> are per-user promises:

- **<C id="read-your-writes" />** — you see your own updates. Implement by routing your reads to
  the leader briefly after you write, or by carrying a write position that a replica must have
  reached.
- **<C id="monotonic-reads" />** — you never see time run backwards. Implement by pinning a user
  to one replica.
- **Monotonic writes** — your writes are applied in the order you made them.
- **Consistent prefix** — you never see an effect before its cause.

Together these make an eventually-consistent system *feel* correct to each individual user,
because the anomalies people notice are almost always about their own actions. Another user's
comment appearing a second late is invisible; your own comment vanishing on refresh is a bug
report.

<Claim label="recommendation">
Before reaching for linearizability, check whether session guarantees would satisfy the actual
complaint. They usually do, and they cost routing logic rather than a coordination round trip
on every operation.
</Claim>

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Applying linearizability everywhere',
      symptom:
        'Every operation is slow, and a partition takes the entire product down rather than degrading it.',
      cause:
        'A blanket "we need consistency" decision instead of a per-operation analysis.',
      mitigation:
        'Classify operations by what a stale answer actually costs. Most cost nothing. Reserve coordination for the ones where being wrong is irreversible.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Assuming eventual consistency means "a bit stale"',
      symptom:
        'Under load or after a partition, replicas are minutes behind, and application logic that assumed seconds breaks.',
      cause:
        'Eventual consistency guarantees convergence if writes stop. It bounds nothing about the interim.',
      mitigation:
        'If you need a bound, say so and enforce it: bounded staleness, with monitoring and a policy for what happens when the bound is exceeded.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Session guarantees lost when the user moves',
      symptom:
        'A user sees their own write on desktop and not on mobile, or loses it after a load balancer reassignment.',
      cause:
        'The guarantee was implemented by pinning to a replica, and the pin is per-connection rather than per-user.',
      mitigation:
        'Carry the write position in a token the client holds, rather than relying on routing stickiness.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Read-your-writes implemented with a fixed delay',
      symptom:
        'Works in testing, fails under load when replication lag exceeds the assumed delay.',
      cause:
        'A hard-coded "read from leader for 500 ms" assumes a lag bound that does not hold during bursts.',
      mitigation:
        'Compare against the actual replication position rather than a wall-clock delay.',
    },
  ]}
/>

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Serve reads with session guarantees rather than linearizability"
  options={[
    'Linearizable reads: coordinate on every read',
    'Session guarantees: per-user coherence, global eventual consistency',
    'Eventual consistency with no session guarantees',
    'Bounded staleness: a contractual maximum lag',
  ]}
  why="The anomalies users actually report are about their own actions — my comment vanished, my setting did not save. Other users lagging by a second is invisible. Session guarantees fix exactly the visible class at a small fraction of the cost."
  gain="Reads served from any replica, so read capacity scales and reads stay available during a partition. The user-visible anomaly disappears. No coordination round trip on the common path."
  sacrifice="Different users can see different states, which is wrong for anything with a cross-user invariant. Implementation requires tracking write positions or routing, which is state you must maintain."
  failureModes={[
    'Losing the session context on device change, reconnection or load balancer reassignment',
    'Cross-user invariants silently violated, because the guarantee is per-user by construction',
    'A lagging replica pinned to a user, giving them a consistently stale view',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate. Routing logic or write-position tokens, plus lag monitoring so a badly lagging replica is removed from the read pool."
  cost="No additional infrastructure. Some engineering, and a permanent requirement that developers know which guarantee each path provides."
  whenNot="When correctness depends on what *other* users did — inventory, seat allocation, permission checks, account balances — per-user coherence is exactly the wrong guarantee, because the invariant spans users. Those operations need linearizability, and the honest design applies it there and nowhere else."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="dynamo">
Dynamo deliberately chose availability over strong consistency for its target workloads,
accepting that concurrent versions may exist and exposing them to the application for
resolution. The paper is explicit that this was a product requirement, not a limitation.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="spanner">
Spanner provides externally consistent transactions across geographically distributed replicas,
using bounded clock uncertainty and commit wait. The latency cost of that guarantee is inherent
to it rather than an implementation detail.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="ddia">
Practical systems commonly offer different guarantees for different operations rather than one
global setting, because the cost of the strongest guarantee is only justified for the small
subset of operations whose correctness depends on it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation">
Write down, per operation, what a stale answer costs. Most rows in most systems cost nothing.
That document is the consistency design, and it is far more useful than a single sentence
naming a database's consistency mode.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'CAP means you pick two of three.',
      why: 'Partitions are not something you choose. The network will partition, so P is a given, and the theorem describes what to do during one. Presenting it as a menu of three suggests you could build a CA system, which in a distributed setting you cannot.',
      instead:
        'State it as: during a partition, answer or be right. Then add PACELC, because outside partitions you are still choosing between latency and consistency on every operation.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We need strong consistency.',
      why: 'For which operations, and what does a stale answer cost for each? Applied globally, it means coordination on every read and unavailability during a partition even for data nobody would notice being stale.',
      instead:
        'Classify. Name the one or two operations whose correctness genuinely depends on seeing the latest value, and be explicit that everything else is eventually consistent.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'Eventual consistency is fine, the lag is only milliseconds.',
      why: 'Eventual consistency promises convergence if writes stop; it promises nothing about the interim. Lag is largest under load, which is when it matters most and when your testing was least representative.',
      instead:
        'If you need a bound, ask for bounded staleness explicitly, monitor the actual lag, and say what happens when it is exceeded.',
    },
  ]}
