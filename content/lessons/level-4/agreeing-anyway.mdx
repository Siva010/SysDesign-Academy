## The problem

You need exactly one node to be the leader. Not zero, which means nothing works. Not two, which
means both accept writes and the data diverges permanently.

But you have just learned that you cannot tell a dead node from a slow one, and that clocks
disagree. So how do you ever safely conclude &ldquo;the old leader is gone, I am in charge
now&rdquo;?

The answer turns out not to be better detection. It is an argument about sets.

## Intuition

Suppose a committee of five must make decisions, and members can be unreachable at any time.

**Rule: no decision without three votes.**

Now consider two groups that have lost contact with each other. Can both make a decision?

For both to decide, both need three members. But there are only five members in total, and
three plus three is six. **Any two groups of three must share at least one member**, and that
member cannot vote for two conflicting decisions.

So at most one group can decide. Not because anyone detected anything, but because arithmetic
forbids it.

That is <C id="quorum-intersection">quorum intersection</C>, and it is the entire foundation.
Everything else — Raft, Paxos, leader election, leases — is machinery built on the observation
that two majorities of the same set must overlap.

There is a price, and it is the thing people misunderstand most:

**If a group cannot reach a majority, it stops.** Not degrades — stops. A consensus system with
three of five nodes unreachable does not serve stale data or accept writes optimistically. It
refuses. That is the guarantee, and it is a deliberate reduction in availability in exchange for
never being wrong.

## Concretely

<Example title="Why a majority, and not any two">

Five nodes. A network partition splits them into a group of three and a group of two.

**With a majority rule (3 of 5):** the group of three can proceed; the group of two cannot. One
side serves, one side stops, and there is exactly one authoritative history.

**With a "any two agree" rule:** the group of two can proceed *and* two members of the group of
three can proceed. Two independent histories, both believing themselves valid. When the
partition heals, there is no principled way to reconcile them — writes have been acknowledged
on both sides that contradict each other.

The majority rule is not conservative engineering taste. It is the minimum requirement for the
overlap argument to hold, and dropping below it removes the guarantee entirely rather than
weakening it.

This is also why consensus clusters have odd sizes. Five nodes tolerate two failures; six nodes
also tolerate two, because a majority of six is four. The sixth node adds cost and coordination
latency and tolerates nothing extra.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="consensus" /> is the problem of getting a set of nodes to agree on a single value, and to
stay agreed through crashes and partitions. The properties an algorithm must provide:

- **Safety** — no two nodes decide differently. This must hold under every failure the model
  admits, including arbitrary delays.
- **Liveness** — a decision is eventually reached, provided enough nodes are reachable.

Safety is absolute; liveness is conditional. That asymmetry is the design.

<C id="raft" /> decomposes the problem into pieces a human can implement:

- **Terms.** Logical time, incremented on each election. A message from an older term is
  rejected, which is how a returning old leader is neutralised without anyone detecting its
  absence.
- **<C id="leader-election" />.** A node that has not heard from a leader becomes a candidate
  and requests votes. A node votes at most once per term, so at most one candidate can win.
- **Log replication.** The leader appends entries and replicates them. An entry is committed
  once a majority has stored it, which — by the overlap argument — means any future leader must
  already have it.
- **<C id="replicated-state-machine" />.** Every node applies committed entries in the same
  order, so all replicas reach identical state.

<Claim label="fact" source="raft">
Raft was explicitly designed for understandability, decomposing consensus into leader election,
log replication and safety, with a strong leader that simplifies log management. The paper
includes the safety argument and the membership-change protocol.
</Claim>

<Callout kind="warn" title="Consensus reduces availability. That is what you are buying.">
A quorum-based system deliberately stops rather than risk divergence. If your requirement is
"always answer, even if the answer is slightly stale", consensus is the wrong tool and you want
an eventually-consistent design. Use consensus where being wrong is worse than being
unavailable — leader election, configuration, membership, and the small set of operations that
genuinely need one order.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Leases, fencing, and the node that comes back">

Consensus gives you agreement among the nodes participating in it. It does not, by itself, stop
a node that *was* the leader from acting on an external resource.

Consider: node A is the leader. It pauses for 30 seconds. The cluster elects node B. Node A
resumes and writes to shared storage, still believing it is the leader.

The storage has no idea a consensus protocol exists.

Two mechanisms close this:

**<C id="lease">Leases</C>.** Leadership is granted for a bounded period. A leader must renew
before acting; a leader that has been paused knows its lease may have expired and must not act
without renewing. This converts a detection problem into a local clock check — which is why it
depends on bounded clock drift, and why lease durations are set conservatively.

**<C id="fencing-token">Fencing tokens</C>.** Each leadership term carries a monotonically
increasing number. Every write to the protected resource includes it, and the resource rejects
any token lower than the highest it has seen. Node A's stale token is refused even though node A
has no idea anything changed.

Fencing is the stronger mechanism because it requires no timing assumptions at all. It does
require the protected resource to participate, which is why "distributed lock" libraries that do
not integrate with the storage layer cannot give the guarantee people assume they do.

</Deeper>

<Deeper title="Paxos, and the failures this does not cover">

Two things are worth knowing precisely here, and in both cases the useful knowledge is the
boundary rather than the mechanism.

**<C id="paxos" />** is the original solution to this problem and the one every later protocol is
measured against. Its core is the same overlap argument used above: a proposer must get a promise
from a majority before it can propose a value, and because any two majorities intersect, a value
that has already been accepted cannot be lost. Its reputation for difficulty comes from what it
leaves unspecified — the basic form agrees on **one value**, and turning that into an ordered
log of many values, with a stable leader and a membership-change procedure, is an engineering
exercise the paper does not carry out.

That gap is why Raft exists and why this lesson teaches Raft. Raft is not a better algorithm in
the sense of agreeing more correctly; it makes the same guarantee. It specifies the parts that
Paxos leaves to the implementer, and it was designed so that the specification can be understood
by the people who have to operate it. If you meet Paxos in production it will be as a
multi-decree variant inside a system somebody else runs, and what you need is the overlap argument
rather than the protocol.

**<C id="byzantine-failure" />** is the assumption underneath all of this, and it is worth stating
because it is almost always left implicit. Raft and Paxos assume nodes fail by **crashing or being
unreachable** — they stop, or their messages are delayed or lost. They do not assume a node
sends different answers to different peers, forges a message, or lies about what it stored. That
is a Byzantine failure, and tolerating it requires different protocols and a larger quorum: three
faulty nodes out of four is survivable for crash faults and not for Byzantine ones.

The reason this curriculum does not pursue it is a judgment about threat model rather than about
difficulty. Inside one organisation's infrastructure, with authenticated links and machines you
control, a node that actively lies is usually a compromise — which is a security problem with
a security response, not a consensus parameter. Byzantine fault tolerance earns its cost where the
participants are **mutually distrusting**, which is the condition public blockchains are built
for and most internal systems are not.

The practical value of knowing this is being able to say what your protocol assumes. A system that
survives a crashed node does not thereby survive a corrupted one, and the gap between those two
sentences has been the subject of more than one confident and wrong design review.

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'Quorum lost, cluster stops',
      symptom:
        'All writes fail. The system is up, healthy, and refusing to do anything.',
      cause:
        'Fewer than a majority of nodes are reachable, so no decision can be safely made.',
      mitigation:
        'This is correct behaviour, not a bug. Restore nodes. Design so that only what genuinely needs consensus depends on it, so the rest of the system degrades rather than stopping.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Election storms',
      symptom:
        'Leadership changes repeatedly, throughput collapses, and no leader lasts long enough to make progress.',
      cause:
        'Election timeouts too close to network latency, or a node repeatedly triggering elections after transient delays.',
      mitigation:
        'Randomised election timeouts well above observed round-trip latency, and a pre-vote step so a partitioned node cannot disrupt a healthy cluster by repeatedly incrementing terms.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Split brain against an external resource',
      symptom:
        'Two nodes both write to shared storage; data is corrupted in a way that is hard to untangle.',
      cause:
        'Consensus made the cluster agree, but the external resource does not participate in the protocol and accepted a write from a stale leader.',
      mitigation:
        'Fencing tokens enforced by the resource. Leases help, and they depend on bounded clock drift; fencing does not.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Membership change losing quorum',
      symptom:
        'Adding or removing nodes causes the cluster to become unavailable or, worse, briefly permits two majorities.',
      cause:
        'Changing the member set changes what a majority means. A naive change can create a window where two disjoint majorities exist.',
      mitigation:
        'Use the algorithm\'s defined membership-change protocol — joint consensus in Raft — and change one node at a time.',
    },

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Use a consensus system for leader election and configuration, not for the data path"
  options={[
    'Consensus for everything: all writes go through the replicated log',
    'Consensus for metadata and leadership; data replicated by simpler means',
    'No consensus: eventual consistency with conflict resolution',
  ]}
  why="Consensus is expensive per operation and reduces availability by design. Leadership and configuration are low-volume and genuinely need one agreed answer; bulk data usually does not, and paying quorum latency for every row write is a large cost for a guarantee most rows do not require."
  gain="Correct leadership and configuration under partition, with no split brain. The data path stays fast and available, and can degrade rather than stop."
  sacrifice="Two consistency models in one system, and engineers must know which operations are which. The boundary between them is a place where reasoning errors accumulate."
  failureModes={[
    'The consensus layer becoming unavailable and taking leadership decisions with it',
    'Assuming data-path operations have the guarantees the metadata path has',
    'A control-plane outage stopping the data plane, which should have kept serving',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Running a consensus cluster is real work: membership changes, disk failures, upgrade ordering, and monitoring for leadership churn. Most teams should use an existing implementation rather than build one."
  cost="Three or five nodes, plus the latency of a majority round trip on every consensus operation. Kept off the data path, that cost is small."
  whenNot="When every operation genuinely requires one global order — a financial ledger where all entries must be totally ordered — putting the data path through consensus is correct and the latency is the price of the guarantee. Conversely, when the data has no cross-object invariants and staleness is acceptable, even the metadata may not need consensus, and a simpler design is better."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="raft">
Raft guarantees that a leader for a given term is unique, because each node votes at most once
per term and a candidate needs a majority. Committed entries are guaranteed to be present on any
future leader, because a majority stored them and any two majorities intersect.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="chubby">
Chubby provides a coarse-grained lock service built on consensus, and its published design notes
that most clients use it for leader election and configuration rather than for fine-grained
locking, with sequence numbers available so protected resources can reject stale holders.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="paxos-simple">
Paxos establishes the safety of consensus through the requirement that any two quorums
intersect. Its safety properties hold regardless of message delays, which is why practical
implementations rely on timeouts only for liveness.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation">
Do not implement consensus yourself. Use a well-tested implementation, and design so that the
number of operations requiring it is small. The interesting engineering is in deciding what
needs agreement, not in the algorithm.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We use Raft, so the system is highly available.',
      why: 'It is the opposite trade. Raft guarantees correctness under partition by refusing to proceed without a majority — availability is deliberately sacrificed to prevent divergence.',
      instead:
        'Say what consensus buys: one agreed order, no split brain, and unavailability when a majority is unreachable. Then say which parts of the system need that and which are better off degrading.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'A distributed lock guarantees mutual exclusion.',
      why: 'It guarantees that at most one client believes it holds the lock at any moment according to the lock service. A paused client can wake believing it still holds a lease that has expired, and act.',
      instead:
        'Require fencing: the protected resource rejects writes carrying a stale token. Without resource participation, the lock protects against contention, not against a stale holder.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We will add more nodes to make consensus more reliable.',
      why: 'Fault tolerance improves only when crossing to the next odd number, while write latency and replication traffic grow with every node, because each commit waits for a larger majority.',
      instead:
        'Use three or five. Beyond that, partition the data and run a consensus group per partition, so groups stay small and work in parallel.',
    },
  ]}
/>
