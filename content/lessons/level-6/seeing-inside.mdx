## The problem

At 02:14 the alert fires: checkout error rate above 5%.

You open the dashboards. CPU is normal. Memory is normal. The database reports healthy. The
error rate is real and rising. You have twelve services and no idea which one is failing, and
the only tool you have is a log search across all of them for the last fifteen minutes.

Ninety minutes later someone notices a third-party address-validation call timing out.

The information existed the whole time. Nothing in your system connected the symptom to the
cause, because nothing tied one user's request to the twelve services it touched.

## Intuition

Three kinds of telemetry, answering three different questions. Using the wrong one is what makes
incidents long.

**Metrics — is something wrong?** Cheap aggregate numbers over time. You can keep them for a
year and query them instantly. They cannot tell you about a specific request, because they
aggregated that away by design.

**Traces — where is the time going?** One request, stitched across every service it touched,
with timings. This is the only tool that answers &ldquo;which of my twelve services is slow&rdquo;
without guessing.

**Logs — why did this specific thing happen?** Detailed events with full context. Expensive at
volume, and irreplaceable once you know what to look for.

The typical incident goes metrics → traces → logs, and the middle step is the one most teams do
not have. Without it, you go straight from &ldquo;something is wrong&rdquo; to grepping twelve
services, which is what turns ten minutes into ninety.

**The cheapest thing you can do, by a wide margin, is propagate a correlation ID.** One
identifier, generated at the edge, passed through every call, attached to every log line. It
costs almost nothing and converts twelve unrelated log streams into one story.

## Concretely

<Example title="What each tool answers, and what it cannot">

Incident: checkout errors at 5%.

**Metric:** `checkout_errors_total` is elevated. You know *that* it is broken and roughly when
it started. You cannot tell which requests, which users, or which dependency.

**Trace:** a sampled failing request shows: gateway 4 ms → checkout 30 ms → inventory 12 ms →
**address-validation 10,000 ms (timeout)** → error. You now know where, in one look.

**Log:** the address-validation client logged `connection reset by peer` with the endpoint and
the correlation ID. You now know why.

Each was necessary. None was sufficient.

Now consider the same incident without correlation IDs. You have the metric. You have logs in
twelve services with no shared identifier, so you cannot tell which log lines belong to the
failing requests. You are reduced to correlating by timestamp across systems whose clocks
disagree.

</Example>

## Formal explanation

<C id="observability" /> is the ability to answer questions about production that you did not
anticipate. That distinction matters: monitoring covers the failures you predicted; observability
covers the ones you did not, which are most of them.

**<C id="golden-signals">The four golden signals</C>** — latency, traffic, errors, saturation —
are the standard starting set for a service. For resources, the equivalent is the USE method:
utilisation, saturation, errors. Saturation is the one teams most often omit, and it is usually
the leading indicator.

**<C id="traces">Distributed tracing</C>** records a span per operation, linked by a trace ID
propagated across service boundaries.

<Claim label="fact" source="dapper">
Dapper describes a distributed tracing infrastructure using trace and span identifiers
propagated through requests, with sampling to control overhead. Sampling is presented as
essential rather than optional at scale.
</Claim>

**<C id="structured-logging" />** means logging fields rather than sentences, so a machine can
filter and aggregate. `level=error service=checkout trace_id=abc user_id=123 dependency=address
latency_ms=10000` is queryable; &ldquo;Failed to validate address for user 123&rdquo; is not.

<Callout kind="warn" title="Cardinality is what makes observability expensive">
Every distinct combination of metric labels is a separate stored time series. Adding a user ID
or a request ID as a metric label turns one series into millions. High-cardinality identifiers
belong in traces and logs, where they are stored once per event, not in metrics where they
multiply the series space.
</Callout>

<Deeper title="Sampling, and why tail sampling is worth the complexity">

You cannot store every trace at high volume. Two strategies:

**Head sampling.** Decide at the start of the request — keep 1%. Simple, cheap, predictable
volume. The problem: the interesting traces are the slow and failing ones, and 99% of those are
discarded before anyone knows they were interesting.

**Tail sampling.** Buffer the trace, decide once it completes — keep all errors, all slow
requests, and a small sample of normal ones. Far more useful, and it requires holding spans in
memory until the trace finishes, which means a buffering layer with its own capacity limits.

<Claim label="recommendation">
Tail sampling on errors and high latency, plus a small baseline of successful traces for
comparison. Without the baseline you cannot tell whether the slow trace is unusual; without the
tail selection you will not have the slow trace at all.
</Claim>

The same reasoning applies to logs. Logging every request at full detail is expensive and mostly
useless; logging every error at full detail, with the correlation ID, is cheap and almost always
what you need.

</Deeper>

## Failure modes

<WhatBreaks
  items={[
    {
      what: 'No correlation between services',
      symptom:
        'An incident takes hours. Engineers grep several services and correlate by timestamp.',
      cause:
        'No identifier propagated across service boundaries, so nothing ties one request together.',
      mitigation:
        'Generate a trace ID at the edge, propagate it in a header through every call, and include it in every log line. This is a small change with a large payoff.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Cardinality explosion',
      symptom:
        'Observability costs multiply after a release, and the metrics backend slows or drops data.',
      cause: 'A label with unbounded values: user ID, request ID, or a URL including parameters.',
      mitigation:
        'Enforce cardinality limits at ingest and alert on new label keys. Put high-cardinality context in traces and logs instead.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Head sampling discarding the interesting traces',
      symptom:
        'A trace of the failing request is needed and does not exist, because 1% sampling kept the healthy ones.',
      cause:
        'The sampling decision was made before the outcome was known.',
      mitigation:
        'Tail sampling: keep all errors and slow requests, plus a baseline sample of normal ones for comparison.',
    },
    {
      what: 'Alerting on causes rather than symptoms',
      symptom:
        'Dozens of nightly alerts, most requiring no action. Real incidents are missed among the noise.',
      cause:
        'An alert per possible cause — CPU, memory, queue depth, disk — rather than on user-visible impact.',
      mitigation:
        'Page on symptoms and on error-budget burn rate. Keep cause metrics on dashboards for diagnosis, not on pagers.',
    },
  ]}

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff
  decision="Adopt distributed tracing with tail sampling"
  options={[
    'Metrics and logs only',
    'Tracing with head sampling at a low fixed rate',
    'Tracing with tail sampling on errors and latency',
  ]}
  why="Once a request touches more than a few services, no amount of per-service metrics tells you which one is responsible. Tracing is the only tool that answers 'where did the time go' directly, and tail sampling ensures the traces you keep are the ones you needed."
  gain="Diagnosis time drops from hours to minutes for cross-service problems. Dependency relationships become visible, including ones nobody documented. Latency attribution stops being a matter of opinion."
  sacrifice="Instrumentation must be present in every service and propagated across every boundary, including through queues, where it is easy to lose. Tail sampling needs a buffering layer with its own capacity and failure modes."
  failureModes={[
    'One service failing to propagate context, breaking traces at that boundary',
    'The tracing backend becoming a dependency whose outage removes your diagnostic capability during an incident',
    'Sampling configured so the interesting traces are the ones discarded',
  ]}
  operationalBurden="Moderate. Instrumentation libraries in every service, a collection pipeline, and a backend to run or pay for. The propagation discipline must survive every new service."
  cost="Meaningful at volume, and controllable through sampling. Cheaper than the engineering hours spent diagnosing without it, though that comparison is rarely made explicitly."
  whenNot="For a single service or a small number with simple call patterns, correlation IDs in structured logs give most of the benefit at a fraction of the cost. Tracing earns its keep when the call graph is deep enough that you cannot hold it in your head."
/>

## In production

<RealWorld>

<Claim label="fact" source="dapper">
Dapper's design propagates trace and span identifiers through requests and uses sampling to
bound overhead, treating low overhead and ubiquitous deployment as hard requirements — an
optional tracing system is not deployed everywhere and is therefore not useful during an
incident.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-book">
The four golden signals — latency, traffic, errors, saturation — are the recommended starting
point for service monitoring, with alerting based on user-visible symptoms rather than on
internal causes.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="brendan-gregg">
The USE method — utilisation, saturation and errors for every resource — is the standard
approach for resource-level investigation, and saturation is the signal most commonly missing
from dashboards.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="datadog-eng">
High-cardinality dimensions are the dominant cost driver in metrics systems, because each
distinct label combination is a separately stored series regardless of how often it is written.
</Claim>

</RealWorld>

## Common mistakes

<Mistakes
  items={[
    {
      mistake: 'We log everything, so we have observability.',
      why: 'Volume is not visibility. Unstructured logs across many services with no shared identifier cannot answer "what happened to this request", which is the question incidents actually pose.',
      instead:
        'Structure the logs, propagate a correlation ID, and add traces once the call graph is deeper than you can hold in your head.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We alert on CPU, memory and disk.',
      why: 'Those are causes. A service can be failing with all three healthy, and all three can be elevated with users perfectly happy. Alerting on causes produces noise, and noise produces ignored alerts.',
      instead:
        'Page on user-visible symptoms and error-budget burn rate. Keep resource metrics on dashboards for diagnosis once you are already looking.',
    },
    {
      mistake: 'We will add a user ID label to the metrics so we can debug per user.',
      why: 'Every distinct label combination is a separate time series. A million users becomes a million series per metric, which is how observability bills multiply overnight.',
      instead:
        'Keep identifiers in traces and logs, where they cost one field per event. Metrics are for aggregates.',
