## What you see

**02:14** — A network fault isolates one rack. The primary database node is in that rack.

**02:14:35** — The failure detector, having seen no heartbeat for 30 seconds, declares the
primary dead. A replica is promoted. Writes resume against the new primary.

**02:19** — The network fault clears. The old primary is reachable again and **has never stopped
believing it is the primary.** From its point of view, nothing happened; it simply could not
reach anyone for five minutes.

**02:19–02:31** — Some clients still hold connections to the old primary. Those writes go to a
node that is no longer authoritative. Other clients write to the new one.

**02:31** — Someone notices that the two nodes have diverged. Both have accepted writes that the
other does not have. There is no correct merge, because both told clients their writes had
succeeded.

## The mechanism

The root cause is not the network fault. It is that **you cannot distinguish a dead node from an
unreachable one.**

From the failure detector's position, these are identical:

- The primary crashed.
- The primary is alive and the network to it is broken.
- The primary is alive and paused — garbage collection, disk stall, hypervisor migration.

The detector must decide anyway. Whichever it decides, it will sometimes be wrong, and this time
it was wrong in the direction that produces two leaders.

<Claim label="inference">
No timeout value fixes this. A longer timeout reduces the frequency and lengthens every genuine
failover; a shorter one does the opposite. The trade is between two kinds of mistake, and safety
cannot come from tuning it.
</Claim>

The second mechanism is that the old primary had no way to learn it had been deposed. It was
partitioned; it received no messages. A node cannot detect its own isolation from the inside.

## Why the obvious fixes make it worse

**Longer failure detection timeout.** Every real failure now takes longer to recover from, and
a sufficiently long pause still produces the same divergence.

**The old primary should check whether it is still primary.** It cannot: during the partition
there is nobody to ask, and a paused process does not know time has passed. Any check has a
window between the check and the write.

**Manual failover only.** Slower, still susceptible — a human can be wrong about whether a node
is dead, and now recovery waits for a human at 02:14.

## What actually works

**Quorum for the decision.** A node may only act as leader if a majority agrees. Since two
majorities of the same set must overlap, and the shared member cannot vote twice, at most one
leader can be elected per term. The minority side cannot elect anyone, so it stops rather than
diverging.

This is why consensus systems become unavailable during a partition. That unavailability is the
guarantee working.

**Fencing tokens for the resource.** Quorum makes the cluster agree. It does not stop a deposed
leader writing to shared storage, which knows nothing about your consensus protocol.

Each leadership term carries a monotonically increasing number. Every write to the protected
resource includes it, and **the resource rejects any token lower than the highest it has seen.**
The old primary's write is refused with no detection required anywhere.

<Claim label="fact" source="raft">
Raft guarantees at most one leader per term, because each node votes at most once per term and a
candidate requires a majority. Messages from an earlier term are rejected, which neutralises a
returning leader without needing to detect its return.
</Claim>

**Leases as a secondary defence.** Leadership is granted for a bounded period and must be
renewed. A leader that has been unreachable knows its lease may have expired and must not act
without renewing. This depends on bounded clock drift, which fencing does not — so fencing is the
stronger mechanism and leases are the convenient one.

## How to prevent it

- **Never build leader election on a simple timeout.** Use a quorum-based mechanism, and use an
  existing implementation rather than writing one.
- **Require fencing at the protected resource.** If the storage layer cannot reject stale tokens,
  the guarantee does not exist regardless of how good the election is.
- **Odd-numbered clusters of three or five.** Six nodes tolerate the same failures as five and
  cost more latency per commit.
- **Know what happens when quorum is lost.** The correct behaviour is to stop. Confirm that your
  system does, and that stopping is survivable for the rest of the product.

## How to detect it early

- **Alert on leadership changes.** Frequent elections indicate the failure detector is
  mis-tuned, and a mis-tuned detector is the precondition for this failure.
- **Alert on any node claiming leadership for an old term**, which means a deposed node is still
  active.
- **Monitor divergence directly** where feasible: compare row counts or checksums between nodes
  that should be identical. Divergence discovered by reconciliation is far cheaper than
  divergence discovered by a customer.

## Related

<Claim label="fact" source="chubby">
A coarse-grained lock service built on consensus can issue sequence numbers so that protected
resources are able to reject requests from stale holders. Clients commonly use such a service for
leader election rather than for fine-grained locking.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="mit-6584">
The impossibility of distinguishing a failed node from a slow one is foundational, and it is why
practical systems rely on majority agreement plus fencing rather than on accurate failure
detection.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="ddia">
Fencing tokens are described as the mechanism that makes distributed locking safe, because they
place enforcement at the resource rather than relying on the client to have correct beliefs about
its own state.
</Claim>
