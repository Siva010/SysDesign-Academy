## What you see

**14:02** — The inventory service has a brief problem. A deploy restarts its instances; for about
40 seconds, roughly 30% of calls fail.

**14:03** — The deploy completes. Inventory is healthy. Error rates should return to normal.

**14:04** — They do not. Inventory is now failing 100% of calls and its CPU is pinned.

**14:20** — Inventory has been restarted twice. Each time it comes up, is immediately
overwhelmed, and fails again within seconds.

**14:45** — Someone blocks traffic at the load balancer. Inventory recovers in under a minute.
Traffic is restored gradually and everything is fine.

The original fault lasted 40 seconds. The outage lasted 43 minutes, and **the original fault was
fixed 41 of those minutes ago.**

## The mechanism

Three layers retry, each independently:

- The mobile client retries up to 3 times.
- The API gateway retries up to 3 times.
- The order service retries its call to inventory up to 3 times.

Under normal conditions nothing fails, so nothing retries and the amplification is invisible.

When inventory starts failing 30% of calls:

1. The order service's retries roughly triple its load on inventory.
2. Gateway retries triple that again.
3. Client retries triple it once more.

At the extreme, one user action becomes 27 requests at the bottom of the stack. Inventory, which
was serving 30% failures, is now receiving many times its normal traffic — so it fails a much
larger share, which triggers more retries.

<Claim label="inference">
This is a positive feedback loop. Beyond a threshold, the retry load alone is sufficient to keep
the service down, which is why fixing the original cause changes nothing. The system is now being
held down by its own response to a fault that no longer exists.
</Claim>

The second mechanism is synchronisation. All the clients failed at the same moment, so they all
back off for the same interval and all retry at the same instant. Load arrives in waves rather
than spread out, and each wave re-triggers the failure.

## Why the obvious fixes make it worse

**Restarting the service.** It comes up with cold caches, empty connection pools and no warmed
runtime state — at its weakest — and immediately receives the full accumulated retry load. It
fails faster than before.

**Adding capacity.** New instances receive the same amplified load. If the bottleneck is a shared
downstream resource, more instances push more concurrency at it. Frequently this extends the
outage.

**Increasing timeouts.** Requests now occupy workers for longer, so concurrency requirements rise
and the queue grows. It converts fast failures into slow ones, which is worse.

## What actually works

**Reduce the load.** Block or shed traffic at the edge, let the service recover with no work to
do, then restore traffic gradually. This feels drastic and is usually the fastest path out.

The order that works:

1. Shed aggressively — accept a small fraction of traffic.
2. Confirm the service is healthy at that fraction.
3. Increase admission in steps, watching goodput rather than throughput.
4. Only then investigate the original fault.

## How to prevent it

**Retry at one layer only.** Pick the layer closest to the failure that has enough context to
retry usefully, and make every other layer pass failures through. Multiplication across layers is
the core of the problem.

**Retry budgets rather than retry counts.** Cap retries as a fraction of total requests — for
example, retries may be at most 10% of traffic. A per-request limit of 3 still triples load
during a broad failure; a budget cannot, by construction.

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-jitter">
Randomised jitter added to exponential backoff spreads retry attempts over time rather than
allowing clients to synchronise into waves, and measurably improves recovery under contention.
</Claim>

**Circuit breakers on optional dependencies.** Once failures are clearly correlated rather than
transient, stop calling entirely. Retrying a broken dependency is not persistence, it is load.

**Distinguish retryable from non-retryable failures.** A 400 is not going to succeed on the
second attempt. Retrying deterministic failures is pure amplification.

## How to detect it early

- **Retry rate as a first-class metric**, separate from request rate. A rising ratio of retries
  to requests is the leading indicator, and most services do not measure it.
- **Goodput, not throughput.** A service completing 50,000 requests per second that all exceed
  the client's timeout has zero goodput and a healthy-looking throughput graph.
- **Amplification factor.** Compare requests received at the bottom of the stack against user
  actions at the top. In normal operation this should be close to 1.

## Related

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-book">
Cascading failure driven by the system's own response to overload — retries, failover and
capacity reduction from health checking — is a documented pattern, and recovery frequently
requires reducing load rather than adding capacity.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="aws-timeouts">
Timeouts should be derived from the caller's latency budget rather than from the dependency's
typical latency, and retries should be bounded as a proportion of traffic rather than per
request.
</Claim>
