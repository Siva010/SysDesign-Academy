## What you see

**07:50** — Nothing. The order-projection consumer is processing 200,000 messages a second across
64 partitions. Total lag on the dashboard: 40,000 messages, which is a fifth of a second of work.
Green.

**11:30** — A support ticket. A customer says their order status has not updated since before
08:00. Support reproduces it and escalates.

**11:45** — The on-call engineer checks the consumer. Total lag is 3.9 million messages. That
sounds alarming until you divide: at 200,000 a second it is twenty seconds of backlog. Still
green. They move on to other hypotheses.

**12:10** — Somebody graphs lag **per partition**. Partition 17 holds 3,870,000 of the 3.9 million.
The other 63 partitions are at zero.

**12:20** — Partition 17 contains a message the consumer cannot deserialise. It has failed on that
message, backed off, and retried, continuously, for four and a half hours.

The affected customers are exactly those whose account id hashes to partition 17 — about 1.5% of
the customer base, completely stopped, for most of a working morning, with every dashboard green
and every alert silent.

## The mechanism

A log has **one position per partition**. A consumer cannot commit an offset past a message it has
not handled without deciding to skip it. So a message that fails deterministically — a malformed
payload, a reference to a row that does not exist, a code path with a bug — never succeeds, and
nothing behind it in that partition is ever processed.

This is head-of-line blocking, and it is not a defect in the log. It is the direct cost of the
ordering guarantee: the reason messages for one account arrive in order is the same reason one of
them can block the rest.

Then the part that made this a four-hour incident rather than a four-minute one.

<Claim label="inference">
Aggregation hides this perfectly, and it does so at every scale. The mean lag over 64 partitions
where 63 are zero is one sixty-fourth of the real number. The sum is a large figure that divides
into a reassuring duration, because the division silently assumes the backlog is spread across all
the consumers. Both of the two most natural ways to summarise per-partition lag produce a healthy
answer while one partition is stopped completely.
</Claim>

There is a second cause with an identical signature, which is why the diagnosis has to be
per-partition before it can be per-message:

- **A hot key.** One account with several hundred times the volume of any other, hashing to one
  partition. The consumer is not stuck; it is working as fast as it can on a partition holding a
  disproportionate share of the traffic.
- **A data-dependent cost.** One key whose messages trigger an expensive path — a large payload, a
  fan-out, a query with a bad plan for that shape. Throughput on that partition is a fraction of
  the others and falls further behind every minute.

The symptom is the same: one partition behind, everything else current, aggregate metrics fine.
The remedies are entirely different, so the first question after "which partition" is "is it
stopped, or is it slow?" — and the retry rate for that partition answers it immediately.

## Why the obvious fixes make it worse

**Restarting the consumer group.** Every member stops, partitions are reassigned, and processing
pauses across all 64 while the group rebalances. Then partition 17 is assigned to somebody and
fails on the same message. Repeated restarts under load can also push the group into a rebalance
loop, at which point nothing progresses anywhere.

**Increasing the retry count or the timeout.** The consumer now spends longer on each doomed
attempt. The loop lasts longer and the backlog grows further.

**Adding consumers to the group.** A partition is assigned to at most one member, so a group can
never usefully have more members than partitions — and even below that limit, extra members
trigger a rebalance and do nothing whatsoever for partition 17.

**Resetting the offset past the bad message.** This does work, and done in a hurry it silently
discards whatever was skipped. If nobody captured the message first, that is data loss with no
record, discovered later by the same customers.

## What actually works

1. **Find the partition.** Maximum lag per partition, not the mean and not the sum.
2. **Decide stopped or slow.** A high retry rate with no offset movement is a poison message. A
   moving offset with low throughput is a hot key or an expensive path.
3. **For a poison message: capture it, then skip it.** Write the payload, the offset and the error
   somewhere durable *before* advancing. The capture is the part people skip when the ticket is
   three hours old.
4. **Then reprocess for the affected customers**, from the captured offset forward, once the bug
   is fixed. They are identifiable — they are the keys that hash to that partition — which is one
   of the few conveniences this failure offers.

For the hot-key variant, none of the above applies: the answer is to reduce the per-message cost,
or to accept that ordering for that key is worth the lag, or to change the partition key — which
is a migration rather than an incident action.

## How to prevent it

**Bounded attempts, then dead-letter, then advance.** Automatically. This is the mechanism that
converts an indefinite stall into one skipped record and one alert, and it must exist before you
need it.

**Distinguish retryable from non-retryable failures rather than only counting attempts.** A
malformed payload will never parse; retrying it five times with backoff wastes ninety seconds and
achieves nothing. A downstream timeout usually succeeds on the second attempt. Classify by the
error, and dead-letter deterministic failures immediately.

**Give the dead-letter destination an owner, an alert, and a tested re-injection path.** A
dead-letter queue nobody reads is a data-loss mechanism with paperwork, and it fails silently by
design.

**Alert on maximum partition lag, in seconds.** Both halves matter. *Maximum*, because any
aggregate hides exactly this shape. *Seconds*, because "partition 17 is four hours behind" is an
incident and "3.87 million messages" is arithmetic that nobody does correctly at 11:45.

**Ask whether you need per-key ordering at all.** If the consumer is idempotent and order does not
matter, a queue with per-message acknowledgement has no head-of-line blocking: the failed message
returns to the queue and everything else proceeds. This failure mode is the price of ordering, and
plenty of workloads are paying it without using it.

## How to detect it early

- **Maximum per-partition lag, in seconds, as a service level indicator with an objective.** This
  single metric would have turned four and a half hours into four minutes.
- **Alert on any partition whose lag exceeds a multiple of the median.** Skew is the signal, and
  it appears long before the absolute number looks bad.
- **Time since last successful commit, per partition.** A partition that has not committed in five
  minutes is stalled whether or not its lag has grown enough to notice yet — this is the leading
  indicator, and lag is the lagging one.
- **Retry rate per partition**, which distinguishes stopped from slow without any investigation.

## Related

<Claim label="fact" source="kafka-docs">
Within a consumer group each partition is consumed by at most one member, so the number of
partitions bounds the effective parallelism of the group and adding members beyond that count
cannot increase throughput.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="kafka-docs">
Ordering is guaranteed within a partition and not across partitions, and a consumer tracks a
single position per partition. Progress past an unprocessed message therefore requires deciding to
skip it.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="kafka-paper">
Consumers maintain their own offset rather than the broker tracking per-message acknowledgement,
which is what allows replay and multiple independent consumers — and what makes the position a
single value per partition rather than a set of outstanding messages.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-builders-library">
Bounding retries and moving repeatedly failing items to a separate destination is established
practice, because unbounded retry of a deterministically failing item consumes capacity
indefinitely without ever succeeding.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Ordering guarantees in a partitioned log apply per partition, so the choice of partition key
determines which events are ordered with respect to each other and, consequently, which events can
block each other.
</Claim>
