## What you see

**11:40** — A routine deploy of the checkout service. Four commits, none of them touching
anything operational.

**11:52** — Dashboards across several unrelated teams start loading slowly. Panels that took
300 ms take nine seconds. Nobody connects it to a deploy in a service they do not own.

**12:05** — Alert evaluation starts running longer than its interval. Two alerts fire six minutes
after the condition they describe.

**12:30** — The platform team is paged. They check everything a metrics platform is supposed to
run out of: samples per second is normal, ingest CPU is at 40%, storage write throughput is
unchanged, disk has months of headroom. Every capacity dashboard is green.

**13:10** — Somebody graphs active series count. It was 52 million this morning. It is now
1.4 billion, and the line has not flattened.

**13:25** — The checkout deploy added a label to one metric. The label value is the request id.

One line of code. Ninety minutes. Every team's observability degraded, and at no point was any
component of the ingest path overloaded.

## The mechanism

A series is identified by its metric name **plus its complete label set**. So series count is not
a sum over metrics — it is a **product** over the distinct values of every label.

A metric with 1,000 series gains a label with an unbounded value space, and it does not gain a
thousand more series. It gains one series per distinct value, forever.

The reason nothing in the ingest path notices is worth being precise about, because it is what
makes the incident so hard to find:

- **Sample volume barely moves.** A request-id label means each new series receives exactly one
  sample and never another. At 4,000 requests per second, that is 4,000 extra samples per second
  against a baseline of five million. An increase of eight hundredths of one percent.
- **Series creation rate goes up four hundredfold.** Normal churn is perhaps ten new series a
  second across the whole fleet — a deploy here, an autoscale there. It is now 4,010.

<Claim label="inference">
The overloaded resource is the index, and the load on it is not measured in samples. It is
measured in *new keys*, which almost nobody graphs. Every capacity dashboard in a metrics platform
watches sample throughput, and sample throughput is the one thing this incident does not touch.
</Claim>

The index is a map from label values to the series carrying them, and it must be searchable by
arbitrary combinations. At 52 million series and roughly 200 bytes of labels each it is about
10 GB — small, memory-resident, fast. At 1.4 billion it is 280 GB, and it no longer fits.

From that moment, **every query slows, including queries that touch none of the affected
metrics**, because every label lookup that used to be a memory access is now a disk read. A team
whose service was not deployed, whose metrics did not change, and whose dashboards reference
entirely different series, watches their tooling degrade for reasons invisible from where they
are standing.

Then the part that turns a ninety-minute mistake into a multi-week problem: **the series already
exist.** Removing the label stops new ones being created and repairs nothing. The 1.4 billion
index entries persist until retention expires them, which for a thirteen-month retention means the
platform is degraded until somebody deletes them deliberately.

## Why the obvious fixes make it worse

**Restarting the storage nodes.** They must rebuild the in-memory index on start, and the index is
now twenty-eight times larger. A ninety-second startup becomes forty minutes, during which that
node serves nothing. Restarting them in sequence extends the outage; restarting them together
removes the platform entirely.

**Adding memory.** It works, briefly, and it ratifies the growth — the platform now runs at a
higher cost with the same absent limit, and the next occurrence starts from a larger base.
Unbounded means unbounded: there is no amount of memory that survives one distinct value per
request.

**Rolling back the deploy.** Correct, necessary, and insufficient. It stops the bleeding. It does
not remove a single existing series, and people who expect recovery within minutes of the rollback
will conclude the diagnosis was wrong.

**Deleting the offending series.** The right destination, and on a time-series store the deletion
path is expensive: it creates markers that compaction must later process, which consumes exactly
the I/O the platform currently has none of. Deleting a billion series during the incident can be
slower than waiting.

## What actually works

The ordering matters, because two of these are urgent and two are not.

1. **Stop new series at the ingest edge.** A per-metric and per-tenant series budget that
   **rejects** new series and keeps serving existing ones. This is the only step that bounds the
   problem, and it must happen before the rollback, because the rollback takes longer.
2. **Protect alerting.** Move alert evaluation to a dedicated query path with strict priority.
   Dashboards being slow is an inconvenience; alerts firing six minutes late is the platform
   failing at its only real job.
3. **Roll back the change**, and tell the owning team what happened in enough detail that they
   understand the mechanism rather than just the instruction.
4. **Reclaim the index**, at whatever pace the store can sustain: drop the affected blocks, or
   shorten retention for the affected metric only, and accept a degraded period bounded by
   whichever you choose.

## How to prevent it

**Series budgets enforced at ingest, per tenant and per metric name.** Enforcement must be at the
edge, because by the time a sample reaches storage its index entry has already been written and
the damage is done.

**Reject rather than degrade.** This is the judgment call, and it is worth stating explicitly:
refusing one team's new series is a small, attributable, fixable problem that produces an error
message with an owner's name on it. Accepting them and degrading is a large, diffuse problem that
nobody owns and everybody feels. The correct failure is the loud one.

**Validate label names in the developer path.** A short deny-list catches most of it: anything
matching `*_id`, `uuid`, `request`, `session`, `trace`, or a path containing an identifier. A CI
check on metric definitions costs nothing and catches this before it exists.

**Show each team their own cardinality budget** in the same place they see their error rate. A
limit nobody can see is a limit people trip over.

<Claim label="convention" source="datadog-eng">
Published engineering writing on metrics platforms identifies cardinality — the number of distinct
label combinations — as the dominant scaling constraint, ahead of raw sample volume.
</Claim>

## How to detect it early

- **Series creation rate**, in new series per second, as a first-class metric. It moves hours
  before anything else does and it is the only signal that leads rather than lags.
- **Active series by metric name, top twenty by growth.** This turns a ninety-minute investigation
  into a thirty-second one, because it names the metric and therefore the team.
- **Index memory as a fraction of capacity**, alerted well below full. Once it stops fitting, the
  degradation is not gradual.
- **A canary query.** Run a fixed, trivial query on a schedule and alert on its latency. Its cost
  should never change, so when it slows, the index did — and it will slow before any human notices
  a dashboard is sluggish.

## Related

<Claim label="fact" source="database-internals">
Index maintenance cost is driven by the number of distinct keys rather than by the volume of data
written, so a workload that adds many new keys imposes far more index work than the same volume
written against existing keys.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="clickhouse-docs">
Deleting data in column-oriented and log-structured stores is implemented as marking rather than
removal, and the space is reclaimed by a later merge. Deletion therefore adds work rather than
freeing resources immediately.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-book">
Monitoring systems are recommended to be simple and to have few dependencies, because a
degradation in the monitoring path removes the ability to observe every other system at the moment
that ability matters most.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="sre-workbook">
Alerting on a small number of user-visible symptoms rather than on every underlying cause directly
reduces the number of series that alerting depends on, which limits the blast radius of a
cardinality event on the paging path.
</Claim>
