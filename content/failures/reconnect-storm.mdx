## What you see

**03:14:20** — A network device in one availability zone is replaced. Roughly forty seconds of
packet loss, affecting about a third of the connection gateways.

**03:15:00** — The network is healthy again. 900,000 of 2.4 million connected clients have
dropped.

**03:15:04** — All 900,000 reconnect. The gateway tier, which handles a few thousand new
connections per second on an ordinary day, receives nine hundred thousand in four seconds.

**03:15:20** — Gateways are out of file descriptors and pinned on TLS handshakes. They begin
refusing connections. Refused clients retry immediately.

**03:16:40** — The gateways that were *not* in the failed zone are now saturated too, because the
displaced clients landed on them. They start dropping the connections they were already holding.

**03:22** — All 2.4 million clients are disconnected and retrying. The authentication service
behind the gateways is at 100% CPU, which means nobody can connect even where there is capacity.

**03:55** — Recovery, after the team blocks reconnects at the edge and admits them in batches.

Forty seconds of packet loss. Forty minutes of outage. And the majority of affected users were on
networks that never failed — they were disconnected by the recovery of people who did.

## The mechanism

Two facts, and everything follows from them.

**Establishing a connection costs orders of magnitude more than using one.** A TLS handshake, a
token validation, a session-registry write, a subscription restore. Sending a message afterwards
costs a few bytes and a syscall. The ratio is easily a thousand to one.

**Steady state hides that completely.** At half a percent daily churn, 2.4 million connections
generate a few thousand handshakes a second, and the gateway tier runs at 5% utilisation. Every
dashboard says the tier is enormously over-provisioned, because for the workload it normally does,
it is.

A correlated disconnect converts the cheap operation into the expensive one for everybody at once.
The tier was sized for the connections it *holds*, and it is now being asked to do the thing it
almost never does, at a rate three hundred times its normal peak.

Then it compounds, in two ways that matter more than the initial spike.

**Retries are synchronised.** All 900,000 failed in the same instant, so a fixed retry delay — or
a backoff with no jitter — schedules them all to return in the same instant. The load arrives as a
series of waves rather than as a rise, and each wave re-triggers the failure.

**Displacement spreads it.** Clients from the failed zone connect to gateways that were healthy.
Those gateways saturate and drop the connections they were already serving, which then reconnect.

<Claim label="inference">
This second phase is what turns a zone incident into a global one, and it is the part that
surprises people. The blast radius of the original fault was one third of the fleet. The blast
radius of the recovery was all of it, because a saturated gateway does not fail politely — it
sheds the connections it is holding, and those become new arrivals somewhere else.
</Claim>

## Why the obvious fixes make it worse

**Restarting the gateways.** Every restart drops that gateway's remaining connections into the
storm. During an incident this is close to the worst available action, and it is the first thing
most people reach for.

**Adding gateway capacity.** New instances come up empty and immediately receive a share of a load
that no instance can absorb — and they authenticate against the same auth service, which is
already the constraint. You have added capacity to the layer that is not the bottleneck.

**Raising file descriptor and connection limits.** More handshakes now start concurrently, so each
takes longer, so more of them exceed the client timeout, so more clients abandon and retry. It
converts a fast refusal into a slow failure, which is strictly worse.

**Waiting for clients to back off.** They will, on whatever schedule was shipped to them months
ago. You cannot change it now, which is precisely why the retry delay needs to be something the
server can dictate.

## What actually works

**Refuse cheaply, before the expensive part.** A refusal must cost far less than an acceptance —
ideally rejected before the TLS handshake completes, returning a signed token that says when to
come back. A refusal that costs as much as an acceptance is not shedding, it is the same load with
no benefit.

Then admit in batches:

1. Block reconnects at the edge entirely. Let the auth service and the registry recover with no
   work to do.
2. Admit a fraction — start at something the auth service can certainly serve, not at what you
   hope it can.
3. Increase in steps, watching **connections successfully established**, not connections
   attempted.
4. Expect this to take longer than feels reasonable. Re-establishing 2.4 million connections at
   20,000 per second is two minutes of pure handshake, and the real rate will be lower.

## How to prevent it

**Jittered backoff sized against your reconnect capacity, not against a convention.** The
arithmetic is the point: if 900,000 clients must reconnect and you can accept 20,000 per second,
the jitter window must be at least forty-five seconds, or the tail of the window is still a storm.
A three-second jitter on a nine-hundred-thousand-client fleet is decoration.

**Server-directed retry delay.** The reconnect response carries the delay. This is the difference
between having a control during the incident and having a policy you shipped last quarter.

<Claim label="fact" source="aws-jitter">
Adding randomisation to exponential backoff spreads retry attempts over time rather than allowing
clients that failed together to return together, and measurably reduces both contention and the
work wasted on collided attempts.
</Claim>

**Make reconnection cheap.** TLS session resumption, a resumption token that avoids a full
authentication round trip, and subscription state restored from a client-held token rather than
rebuilt by querying. Every millisecond removed from the reconnect path multiplies by the size of
your connected population.

**Plan capacity for the reconnect path explicitly**, and state it as a duration: *we can
re-establish the entire connected population in N minutes.* That number is a capacity requirement
in its own right, and almost nobody has it.

**Isolate by cell**, so a zone or region event disconnects a bounded fraction rather than a third
of everything.

**Drain on deploy, never drop.** This is the same path, and a rolling deploy exercises it every
week — which makes it one of the few disaster paths you can be confident is tested, provided you
never restart more than a small percentage of gateways at once.

## How to detect it early

- **Connection establishment rate**, with its own stated capacity. It is a completely different
  metric from connection count, and the tier's real limit is expressed in it.
- **Handshake latency.** It rises before refusals begin, which makes it the leading indicator.
- **Alert on a sudden drop in connected count.** A fall of more than a few percent in a minute is
  the warning; the storm arrives seconds later, and those seconds are enough to start shedding.
- **Rehearse it.** Disconnect ten percent of the fleet deliberately and measure the time to full
  reconnection. If nobody has ever done this, the recovery time is unknown rather than acceptable.

## Related

<Claim label="fact" source="hpbn">
Establishing a connection costs round trips for the transport handshake and again for encryption,
and long-lived connections consume server resources whether or not data is flowing. Connection
count and connection establishment rate are therefore separate capacity dimensions.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-builders-library">
Shedding load early and cheaply, so that rejection costs far less than service, is described as
the mechanism that allows a system to remain useful during overload rather than failing uniformly.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="release-it">
A saturated component that sheds the work it is already holding converts its own overload into new
arrivals elsewhere, which is how a bounded fault propagates into a general one.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="discord-eng">
Published engineering writing on large real-time messaging systems treats mass reconnection as a
primary architectural concern rather than a tuning detail, with the connection-handling tier kept
separate from application logic for exactly this reason.
</Claim>
