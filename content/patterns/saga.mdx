## Problem

Placing an order requires: reserve inventory, charge the card, create a shipment. Three services,
three databases.

You need all three or none. A charge with no shipment is a refund and a complaint; a shipment
with no charge is lost money.

A distributed transaction across all three would work and is expensive: participants hold locks
for the duration, the coordinator becomes a critical dependency, and availability becomes the
product of every participant's. That cost is rarely justified for a business workflow that
takes seconds and involves a third party you do not control.

## Pattern

Give up atomicity. Replace it with a sequence of local transactions, each with a defined
**compensating action** that undoes it.

    Reserve inventory   →  compensate: release reservation
    Charge card         →  compensate: refund
    Create shipment     →  compensate: cancel shipment

If step three fails, run the compensations for steps two and one, in reverse order.

Two ways to drive it:

- **Choreography.** Each service listens for the previous step's event and emits its own. No
  central coordinator, and no single place that describes the workflow — which becomes hard to
  reason about beyond three or four steps.
- **Orchestration.** A coordinator explicitly calls each step and handles failures. One place to
  read the workflow, and one more component to operate.

Every step and every compensation must be idempotent, because retries are guaranteed.

## Advantages

- **No distributed locks.** Each service commits locally and releases immediately, so no
  participant is held hostage by another's latency.
- **Availability is not the product of every participant.** A step can be retried later rather
  than failing the whole operation immediately.
- **Services stay independent.** No shared transaction manager, no coupling of database
  technologies.
- **It works with third parties**, which can never join your transaction anyway.

## Costs

- **Intermediate states are visible.** For a period, inventory is reserved and the card is not
  charged. Every such state must be a state the business can explain.
- **Compensations are not rollbacks.** A refund is not the inverse of a charge: the money moved,
  the statement shows both, and fees may not be returned.
- **Some steps cannot be compensated.** An email has been read. A physical item has shipped.
  Order the saga so irreversible steps come last.
- **Failure handling is most of the code.** The happy path is short; the compensation logic,
  retries and stuck-saga handling are the bulk of it.

## When to use it

- The operation genuinely spans services with separate databases.
- A distributed transaction is unavailable or too expensive.
- Intermediate states can be explained to a user and to support.
- Every step can be compensated, or the irreversible ones can be placed last.

## When NOT to use it

- **When the data is in one database.** Use a transaction. A saga across tables in a single
  database is complexity with no corresponding benefit, and it is a surprisingly common mistake
  in service-oriented codebases.
- **When intermediate states are unacceptable.** If a regulator or an invariant forbids money
  being deducted while an order does not exist, the saga's window is a compliance problem, not a
  technical trade-off.
- **When steps cannot be compensated and cannot be reordered.** If an irreversible action must
  happen first, the pattern does not fit and the workflow needs restructuring.
- **When the workflow is short and synchronous.** Two calls that both must succeed within one
  request may be better handled by making the second idempotent and retrying, without saga
  machinery.

## Example systems

<Claim label="convention" source="martin-fowler">
The saga is described as a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions, contrasted
with distributed transactions, and the choice between choreography and orchestration is framed
as a trade between coupling and observability of the workflow.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="building-microservices">
Service decomposition removes the possibility of a single transaction across the whole business
operation, which is presented as one of the principal costs of splitting services — not as an
incidental detail.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="ddia">
Two-phase commit provides atomicity across participants at the cost of blocking: participants
hold locks until the coordinator returns, so availability depends on every participant and on
the coordinator.
</Claim>
