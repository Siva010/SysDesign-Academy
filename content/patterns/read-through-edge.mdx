## Problem

Two costs, and they are different problems that happen to have one solution.

**Distance.** A user 15,000 km away pays roughly 150 ms per round trip, and no amount of server
optimisation changes that. It is set by the speed of light in fibre.

**Repetition.** The same public content is requested millions of times. Your origin computes or
fetches an identical answer for every one of them, consuming capacity that could serve requests
which are actually different.

## Pattern

Put copies of the response in many locations near users, and let those locations answer.

1. The response carries cache directives saying who may store it and for how long.
2. An edge network stores it at each of its locations.
3. Subsequent requests for that content are answered locally. The origin never sees them.
4. On a miss, the edge fetches from the origin and stores the result.

Because the content is identical for everyone, one origin fetch per location per TTL serves an
unbounded number of users. The origin's load becomes proportional to the number of edge
locations and the TTL, not to the number of users.

<Claim label="fact" source="rfc9111">
HTTP defines freshness and validation semantics that let any cache in the path store and reuse a
response, including directives distinguishing responses that may be shared between users from
those that may not.
</Claim>

## Advantages

- **Distance is removed** for cached content. The response comes from a nearby location rather
  than from across an ocean.
- **Origin load becomes almost independent of user count.** This is the only mechanism that
  handles a genuinely viral single item, because it scales with edge locations rather than with
  demand.
- **Connection setup is short.** Even for uncacheable responses, terminating TLS at the edge
  converts two long handshake round trips into short ones, with a warm connection onward.
- **Volumetric attacks are absorbed** across many locations rather than concentrating at your
  origin.

## Costs

- **Invalidation is weak.** A purge propagates with delay, and content already delivered to a
  browser cannot be recalled at all. Your control over what users see degrades the further out
  you cache.
- **Only shareable content qualifies.** Anything personalised must be explicitly excluded, and
  getting that wrong is a serious incident rather than a performance regression.
- **A third party holds your content** and sits in your trust boundary.
- **Billing is per request and per gigabyte**, so at high volume it becomes a major line item —
  frequently larger than the origin it protects.

## When to use it

- The content is **identical for many users** — assets, media, public pages, public API responses.
- Users are geographically distributed and latency matters.
- The content is large, so bandwidth savings are significant.
- A single item may become extremely popular, and no partitioning scheme can help.

## When NOT to use it

- **For personalised or authenticated responses.** These are not shareable, so the hit rate is
  near zero and the risk of serving one user's data to another is real. The exception is using
  the edge purely for TLS termination and connection reuse, which is valuable and is a different
  benefit from caching.
- **When content must be immediately correct everywhere.** Takedown, price changes and permission
  changes all propagate with delay. If the requirement is sub-second, the edge is the wrong place
  for that decision, and you need a check on the origin path.
- **When traffic is concentrated near your origin.** If most users are one region away, the
  latency saving is small and the added dependency may not be worth it.
- **When egress cost exceeds origin cost.** At high volume of large objects, edge billing can
  exceed what serving directly would have cost. It is worth doing the arithmetic rather than
  assuming the edge is cheaper.

## Example systems

<Claim label="fact" source="mdn-http">
Cache directives distinguish responses that may be stored by shared caches from those that may
only be stored by the user's own browser. Getting this distinction wrong is what causes one
user's data to be served to another.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="cloudflare-blog">
Distributing traffic across many locations, typically via anycast, is standard practice both for
latency and for absorbing volumetric attacks, because an attack large enough to saturate an
origin's connectivity cannot be filtered by that origin.
</Claim>

<Claim label="fact" source="rfc9111">
The stale-while-revalidate directive allows a cache to serve a stale response while refreshing it
in the background, which is a standards-level answer to the stampede problem for cacheable
content.
</Claim>
