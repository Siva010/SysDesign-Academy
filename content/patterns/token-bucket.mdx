## Problem

One caller sends 40,000 requests a second. Everyone else's latency triples.

They are not malicious — a retry loop has a bug. A global rate limit does not trigger, because
the total is within capacity. The problem is the **distribution**, not the volume.

A naive fix — a fixed cap of N requests per second per caller — breaks legitimate clients, because
real traffic is bursty. A client that sends 100 requests in one second and nothing for the next
ten is well-behaved on average and is rejected by a per-second cap.

## Pattern

Model the allowance as a bucket of tokens.

- The bucket holds up to **B** tokens and refills at **R** tokens per second.
- Each request consumes one token. If the bucket is empty, the request is rejected.
- Tokens accumulate while the caller is idle, up to the bucket size.

Two parameters with two distinct meanings:

- **R** is the sustained rate. Over any long period, the caller cannot exceed it.
- **B** is the burst allowance. A caller who has been quiet can spend up to B immediately.

That separation is what makes the pattern work: real clients are bursty, and a limiter that
forbids bursts rejects legitimate traffic while a limiter with no sustained cap does not limit
anything.

The **leaky bucket** is the sibling: requests drain at a fixed rate with no burst allowance,
enforcing a perfectly smooth output. Use it when the thing you are protecting cannot absorb
bursts at all.

## Advantages

- **Bursts are allowed without allowing sustained overuse**, which matches how real clients behave.
- **Two intuitive parameters**, both of which can be explained to a customer.
- **Cheap to implement**: a timestamp and a count per identity, with tokens computed lazily rather
  than refilled by a timer.
- **Composable.** Different limits per tier, per endpoint, or per operation cost — an expensive
  endpoint can consume several tokens.

## Costs

- **State per identity.** With millions of callers, that state must live somewhere and be
  accessible on every request.
- **Distributed enforcement is approximate or expensive.** Node-local buckets multiply the limit
  by fleet size; shared buckets add a round trip to every request.
- **Parameters need tuning** and are a customer-visible contract once published.
- **Rejection must be cheap**, or the limiter consumes the capacity it was protecting.

## When to use it

- A shared resource with many callers, where one caller can affect the others.
- Callers are identifiable — API key, account, tenant, IP.
- Traffic is naturally bursty, so a smooth cap would reject legitimate use.
- You can enforce the limit before doing expensive work.

## When NOT to use it

- **When the protected resource cannot absorb bursts at all.** A leaky bucket, which enforces a
  smooth rate, is the correct choice when passing a burst downstream is the failure you are
  preventing.
- **When callers cannot be identified.** Limiting by IP alone punishes shared networks and is
  trivially evaded. Authentication has to come first.
- **When the real problem is total capacity rather than distribution.** If every caller is within
  their limit and the system is still overloaded, you need load shedding and admission control,
  not a per-caller limit.
- **As a substitute for isolation.** A limit stops one caller consuming everything; it does not
  stop their traffic from competing with everyone else's for the same pool. Bulkheads do that.

## Example systems

<Claim label="convention" source="cloudflare-blog">
Rate limiting per identity at the edge, before requests reach the origin, is standard practice
for public APIs, because rejection at the origin still consumes origin capacity.
</Claim>

<Claim label="convention" source="aws-load-shedding">
Admission control that rejects work early and cheaply, prioritising by caller or request type, is
described as the mechanism for maximising completed useful work during overload.
</Claim>

<Claim label="recommendation" source="sre-book">
Published limits become a contract with your users. Changing them is a customer-facing change,
so choose the initial values with that in mind rather than treating them as tunable
configuration.
</Claim>
